20 Abstract 21 Global electrification of transportation and widespread adoption of grid energy storage are core 22 components of a broader strategy to mitigate the potentially disastrous effects of climate change. 23 However, supply chain issues exacerbated by the limited and heterogenous distribution of essential 24 materials for Li-ion batteries provide strong motivation for the development of alternative 25 technologies to meet global demands. 5 Introduction 6 Environmental and resource sustainability has become increasingly important for the development 7 of next-generation battery technologies needed to meet the ever-expanding market for renewable 8 energy storage in smart grids and electrification of vehicles1, 2. Li-ion battery (LIB) technologies 9 dominate in this space currently due to their high energy density and long cycle life. However, as 10 demand for energy storage capacity continues to accelerate, price and resource volatility due to 11 limitations in the supply of lithium and transition metals are garnering more attention. In addition, 12 LIBs require an energy-intensive manufacturing process, often utilizing toxic and environmentally 13 unfriendly chemicals. The potential safety issues during LIB operation and their tolerance to 14 mechanical abuse are also not completely resolved. 15 Aqueous zinc batteries (AZBs) have emerged as one of the more promising complementary 16 chemistries because the potential of the Zn anode is almost compatible with aqueous electrolytes, 17 Zn is relatively abundant, and there is already a mature recycling infrastructure. Recent application 18 of “water-in-salt” electrolytes have extended the electrochemical stability window of aqueous 19 electrolytes3, 4 and enabled new battery chemistries,5, 6 including zinc batteries that are presently 20 limited to primary use.7, 8 However, the benefits of “water-in-salt” electrolytes for use in AZBs are 21 not enough to overcome barriers to commercialization due to underwhelming reversibility of Zn 22 plating/stripping, insufficient energy efficiency, and poor transport at low temperatures. 23 Different from simply increasing salt concentration in a single salt electrolyte, the addition of 24 concentrated supporting salts has proven to be another promising strategy to largely exclude water 25 from solvating Zn2+ in aqueous electrolytes.9-11 As a result, such electrolytes exhibit a substantial 26

Our approach sees the addition of lithium chloride (LiCl) as a 6 supporting salt into a stronger Lewis acid – zinc chloride (ZnCl2) aqueous electrolyte to form 7 Li2ZnCl4⋅RH2O with varying R. Waters either remain as part of the free solvent network at low salt 8 concentration or are preferentially coordinated by Li+ ions while Cl− ions preferentially form 9 ZnCl42- anions or small [ZnCl4m-]n anion clusters (n<4). Some 11 error bars are smaller than the plotting symbols. 12 Results 13 Origin and properties of nano-phase frustration in Li2ZnCl4⋅RH2O electrolytes 14 In dilute salt-in-solvent electrolytes, all ions tend to be solvated by highly polar solvents (bottom 15 left of Fig. 1a). The strong dipolar interactions between solvent molecules promote structural 16 ordering, resulting in a high glass transition temperature (Tg)13 or freezing point14. As an example, 17 electrolyte viscosity (η) of 2 mol/kg LiCl or 2 mol/kg ZnCl2 aqueous single salt electrolytes 18 increases rapidly as temperature decreases, as shown by the early rollover of ionic conductivities 19 at >−20°C (Fig. 1b). Their deviation from the Vogel–Tammann–Fulcher (VTF) equation15 20 (corresponding dash lines) is due to the spontaneous increase of salt concentration caused by water 21 crystallizing into ice. Adding non-polar or low-polarity solvents with low melting points into salt- 22 in-solvent electrolytes can reduce their viscosity even at a low temperature. However, it also 23 reduces the overall dielectric constant and the availability of charge carriers16, 17, thereby decreasing 24 ionic conductivity. Alternatively, it is well-known that switching to the super-concentrated or 25 “solvent-in-salt” regime disrupts the intermolecular network in free solvent clusters (bottom right 26 of Fig. 1a).12 As a result, the significantly reduced solvent activity suppresses the contraction and 27 crystallization of solvent clusters at a low temperature.
