Abstract Nitrogen (N) and phosphorus (P) limitations constrain the magnitude of terrestrial carbon (C) uptake in response to elevated CO2 and climate change. However, global maps of nutrient limitation are still lacking. Here, we examine global N and P limitation using the ratio of site-averaged leaf N and P resorption efficiency of dominant species across 171 sites. We evaluate our predictions using a global database of N- and P-limitation experiments based on nutrient additions at 106 and 53 sites, respectively. Globally, we find a shift from relative P to N limitation at higher latitudes and with higher precipitation seasonality, lower mean annual temperatures, temperature seasonality, mean annual precipitation, and soil clay fraction. Excluding cropland, urban, and glacial areas, we estimate that 18% of natural terrestrial land area is significantly limited by N alone, whereas 43 % is relatively P limited. The remaining 39% of natural terrestrial land area could be co-limited by N and P or weakly limited by either nutrient alone. This work provides both a new framework for testing nutrient limitation and a benchmark of N and P limitation for models to constrain predictions of the terrestrial C sink. 39 40 Main Primary productivity of terrestrial plants is widely limited by essential nutrients, especially N and P1-4. The substrate-age hypothesis5 predicts increasing P limitation from geologically young arctic and boreal ecosystems towards tropical forests, which have relatively little ‘weatherable’ rock P6, 7. In contrast, biological N fixation and N 2

Page 5

mineralization both show the opposite latitudinal trend, contributing to a general poleward increase of N limitation8-10. Moreover, fire disturbances generally volatilize more N than P and may contribute to N limitation in fire-prone biomes (e.g., boreal forest)11, 12. Anthropogenic N deposition may increase P limitation in hotspot regions13-17, while elevated atmospheric CO2 and warming-induced longer growing seasons are likely resulting in greater nutrient limitation of terrestrial ecosystems18,19. On a global scale, N and P limitations likely constrain current and future ecosystem response to elevated CO2 concentrations and climate change20,21. Therefore, understanding the spatial heterogeneity in nutrient limitation and its causes remains a high-priority. 55 56 Several approaches have been used to infer nutrient limitations, including fertilization experiments, measurements of resorption efficiency, and thresholds of leaf N:P ratios1-3, 22-26. Fertilization experiments can directly test nutrient limitation25, but global meta-analyses of field fertilization experiments have found inconsistent latitudinal trends in nutrient limitation, potentially attributable to combining different growth indicators or using effect size metrics not standardized by the level of N addition1-3. Alternatively, thresholds of leaf N:P ratio (e.g., 14:1 vs 16:1 or 10:1 vs 20:1) have been used to indicate N or P limitation indirectly22-24, but a recent assessment of these thresholds indicates relatively large uncertainties26.

Leaf N resorption efficiency (NRE) and P resorption efficiency (PRE) have been 3

Page 6

shown to increase with lower N and P availability, respectively27, 28, illustrating a tradeoff between nutrient recycling within a plant and uptake from the soil. Han et al.29 proposed an indicator for N and P limitation based on the difference of NRE and PRE for woody plants but found no significant latitudinal or climatic trends. In contrast, Reed et al.30 found that NRE/PRE ratios increased with latitude and decreased with mean annual temperature (MAT) and mean annual precipitation (MAP). However, both of the analyses were conducted for individual species and were not necessarily representative of ecosystem nutrient limitation. 75 76 Based on the stoichiometric homeostasis theory31 and Liebig's law of the minimum32, we define an indicator using plant leaf NRE and PRE to estimate N and P limitation at the ecosystem scale. We further apply this approach to a newly constructed database of Paired Resorption Efficiency of Nitrogen and Phosphorus for terrestrial plants (PRENP, version 1.0) (Figs. 1 & S1; Table S1). Unlike previous studies that focused on single-nutrient resorption separately27, 28, 33-35, our PRENP database only includes studies that simultaneously measured leaf NRE and PRE. We also compiled a global database of field-diagnosed Nutrient (N and/or P) Limitation (NuLi, version 1.0) (Table S2) to validate our predictions of N and P limitation. 85 86 Estimating relative nutrient limitation Leaf nutrient demand is met by both internally resorbed nutrients and externally derived nutrients from the environment10. At the ecosystem scale, the ratio of average leaf NRE to PRE weighted by the leaf mass of all species is in theory capable of indicating N or P limitation. However, species-specific leaf mass is rarely reported together with NRE and PRE. As a result, we used the ratio of site-averaged NRE (NREdom) to site-averaged PRE of dominant species (PREdom) as an approximate indicator (NREdom/PREdom). We modelled global NREdom/PREdom and found that the diagnosed limiting nutrients based on NREdom/PREdom agree well with results of field fertilization experiments in our NuLi database (see below and Table S2). Overall, NREdom/PREdom appears to be a useful indicator of ecosystem N and P limitation. 120 121 Global mapping of N and P limitation NREdom/PREdom in our dataset is log-normally distributed (Shapiro-Wilk test, W = 0.99, p = 0.11) and shows a global geometric mean of 0.90 (median = 0.90), as determined by NREdom (geometric mean = 55 %, median = 57%) and PREdom (geometric mean = 61 %, median =63%) (Figs. 1 & S2). The maxima (95th-100th percentile) of NREdom (81-86 %) and PREdom (80-87 %) are comparable, implying that N and P have similar maximum resorption efficiencies at the ecosystem scale. 128 129 To predict global patterns of N and P limitations, we considered eleven potential predictors as suggested from the literature4,5,8,9,13,36, including variables defining climate (MAT, MAP, temperature seasonality and precipitation seasonality), soil fertility (N deposition, soil clay fraction, soil pH, and cation exchange capacity[CEC]) 6

Page 9

and vegetation characteristics (vegetation type, gross primary production[GPP], and normalized difference vegetation index[NDVI]) (see Table S3 for more details). Model selection analysis, based on corrected Akaike Information Criterion (AICc), showed that the best model describing ln-transformed NREdom/PREdom included MAT, MAP, temperature seasonality, precipitation seasonality, soil clay fraction, the interaction of MAT and MAP and vegetation type (i.e., woody ecosystems and grasslands) as important predictors (Fig. 2a, Table S4; deviance explained 54%). The relative importance of the predictors (Fig. 2a) supported removing soil pH, CEC, NDVI, GPP and N deposition from the final model. In addition, diagnostic analyses showed that statistical assumptions (homoscedasticity, multivariate normality, and independence of residuals, and low multicollinearity) were met for the linear model (Tables S4-S8; Fig. S3 & S4). 145 146 Climate is the most important factor affecting nutrient limitation in our analysis (Fig. 2a). Partial regression analysis indicates that ln-transformed NREdom/PREdom decreased significantly with higher MAT (p < 0.001; Fig. 2b), temperature seasonality (p < 0.001; Fig. 2c) and MAP (p < 0.001; Fig. 2d), but increased with greater precipitation seasonality (p < 0.001; Fig. 2e). The trends of NREdom/PREdom with MAT and MAP were predominantly attributable to a significant decrease of NREdom with both MAT (p < 0.001, Fig. S5a) and MAP (p < 0.001, Fig. S5b). The different trends of NREdom and PREdom might be attributable to the fact that P availability is strongly determined by soil parent materials4, 36, whereas N availability is mainly 7

Page 10

determined by biological N fixation and mineralization, which depend strongly on climate8,10. Moreover, MAT and MAP showed a significant interaction (p < 0.01; Table S4; Fig. 2a), likely attributable, in part, to the fact that temperature can partially counteract the role of additional precipitation by promoting evaporation. 159 160 Partial regression analysis also shows a significant decrease in NREdom/PREdom with higher soil clay fraction (p < 0.001; Fig. 2f). This relationship likely arises because soils with high clay fraction are strongly weathered and relatively poor in P availability4,5. Moreover, we found that grasslands have significantly lower NREdom/PREdom than woody ecosystems (p < 0.001; Fig. 2g, Table S4), implying that plant conservation of P is more important in grasslands. As driven by shifts in climate, soil, and vegetation variables, NREdom/PREdom shows a significant increase towards higher latitudes (p < 0.001; Fig. S6c) with a stronger latitudinal trend for NREdom (Fig. S6a) than for PREdom (Fig. S6b). 169 170 We also employed a Random Forest approach using all eleven predictors in parallel to the linear model, but cross-validated R2 values indicated the linear model to be statistically superior using fewer predictors (Fig. S7). Therefore, we used the linear model to predict global N and P limitation (Fig. 3a). Considering a model residual standard error of 0.16 (Table S4), predicted values of ln-transformed NREdom/PREdom ranging from -0.16 to 0.16 were identified as insufficient statistically to identify nutrient limitation based on a theoretical threshold of 0 for ln-transformed 8

Page 11

NREdom/PREdom (Eq. 4). Within the statistically meaningful ranges (ln-transformed NREdom/PREdom < -0.16 or > 0.16), our approach successfully identified 83% (38/46) of cases of N limitation and 83% (25/30) of P limitation compared with results of field experiments (Table S2). 181 182 Global patterns of N and P limitation Our predictions showed a strong latitudinal pattern of N and P limitation (Fig. 3a) and relatively close agreement with results of field experiments testing both N (Fig. 3b) and P limitations (Fig. 3c) (Table S2). Moreover, there is a transition to N limitation at higher elevations in some lower-latitude regions (e.g., Tibetan Plateau, the Andes, South African Plateau) (Fig. 3a), likely attributable to an elevational decrease in temperature and consequent changes in soil and vegetation. 189 190 Excluding cropland, urban, and glacial areas, our model estimated that 18% of natural terrestrial land area was significantly limited by N (ln-transformed NREdom/PREdom > 0.16), whereas 43 % of the land area was P limited (ln-transformed NREdom/PREdom < -0.16) (Table S9). However, our model was unable to diagnose limiting nutrients in 39% of the natural terrestrial land area (ln-transformed NREdom/PREdom ranging from - 0.16 to 0.16) (Table S9), which could indicate co-limitation by N and P or weak limitation by either nutrient alone. By overlapping our predictions with global map of major terrestrial biomes37, we show that N limitation prevails relatively more in boreal forest, tundra, temperate coniferous forest, and montane grasslands and shrublands, whereas 9

Page 12

P is more limiting in tropical and subtropical forest, temperate broadleaf and mixed forest, deserts, Mediterranean biomes, and grasslands, savannas and shrublands in tropical, subtropical, and temperate regions (Table 1). Overall, our global mapping of nutrient limitations provides additional details and information on spatial heterogeneity than existing assessments based on nutrient fertilization experiments alone1-3. 205 206 Uncertainties and implications Previous studies conducted for individual species27, 28, 33-35 provide important, but incomplete, insights into prevailing nutrient limitations at the ecosystem scale. Our approach based on NREdom/PREdom partially overcomes this challenge and may be better suited for diagnosing prevailing limiting nutrients. Due to the uncertainty of our approach, nutrient limitation was undetermined in a proportion of the natural terrestrial ecosystems. Future studies would benefit from additional data to support analyses using ecosystem mean NRE/PRE weighted by species-specific leaf mass or abundance. We recommend researchers in field studies measure such variables whenever possible and compare results to those of nutrient fertilization experiments. Moreover, nutrients other than N and P and not considered here, such as potassium, calcium, and other micronutrients, can also limit terrestrial productivity38,39 but they are not likely to be limiting nutrients globally. Nevertheless, our map appears fairly robust because it is based on drivers determined by model selection identified from empirical data, strengthening current understanding for patterns of N and P limitations, 10

Our analysis presents a static estimate of global N and P limitation, but many factors

can restructure such patterns. For instance, anthropogenic N emissions have altered global N deposition, which can shift N limitation towards P limitation in some regions13,14,17. CO2 enrichment may increase nutrient limitation attributable to enhanced nutrient accumulation in plant biomass and litters18, 21, 40. Climate warming may favor biological N fixation and N mineralization and thereby improve ecosystem N availability at mid-to-high latitudes8, 41, 42. By providing data on global terrestrial N and P limitation, our work could potentially help vegetation models and Earth System Models to constrain predictions of terrestrial C cycling in response to N deposition, elevated CO2 concentrations and climate change. 233 234 Data availability All data supporting the findings of this study are available are available in figshare (https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.10735652.v1). 237 238 Code availability The code used in this work can be accessed by contacting the corresponding authors. 240 241 References

The key words ‘nitrogen resorption’ and ‘phosphorus resorption’ were used to search published studies of leaf NRE and PRE. Data were included only when NRE and PRE were measured simultaneously. To avoid misattributing natural nutrient limitation due to anthropogenic disturbances, we excluded data for intensively managed ecosystems (e.g., agroforest, fertilized plantations, sown pastures, croplands and urban forests). Overall, our database includes 925 observations of leaf NRE and PRE for 585 species from 135 publications, with 627 observations for 377 dominant species across 171 sites (Figs. 1 & S1; Table S1). We also recorded corresponding information on all species, dominant species, site location (longitude and latitude), MAT (°C), and MAP (mm) from the literature. Information on dominant species was derived primarily based on statements by the authors in the original literature. 409 410 To validate our predictions of relative nutrient limitation, we compiled a global database of nutrient (N and/or P) limitation (NuLi, Version 1.0) based on the growth response to experimental nutrient additions in relatively unmanaged ecosystems. An ecosystem was diagnosed to be limited by a certain nutrient if additions of the nutrient significantly stimulated plant growth. By synthesizing experimental results from 84 publications, the NuLi database includes information on N limitation at 106 sites and P limitation at 53 sites, and information on site location (longitude and latitude), MAT, and MAP (Table S2). 20
