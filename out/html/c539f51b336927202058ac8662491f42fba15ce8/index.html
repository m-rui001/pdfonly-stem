The lithium-sulfur battery is promising as an alternative to conventional lithium-ion technology due to the high energy density of both sulfur and lithium metal electrodes. Extended life time has been demonstrated, but two notable challenges still exist in battery applications: overcoming the undesired high electrolyte/sulfur ratio, and inhibiting Li dendrite growth and its parasitic reaction with the electrolyte. Here we demonstrate that by tuning the electrolyte structure, the challenges at both electrodes can be tackled simultaneously. The sulfur speciation pathway transforms from a dissolution-precipitation route to a quasi-solid-state conversion in the presence of lowered solvent activity and an extended electrolyte network structure as revealed by ab initio calculations, curtailing the need for high electrolyte volumes. With such an optimized structure, the Li plates dendrite-free and shows 20-fold reduction in parasitic reactions with Li, avoiding electrolyte consumption and greatly extending the life time of a low E/S (5 µl/mg) sulfur cell. The battery industry anticipates exploration of new electrochemistry beyond conventional lithium-ion intercalation technology to boost energy density and reduce the cost for electric vehicles (EVs). The lithium-sulfur battery has received enormous attention in the past decade, due to the high theoretical specific energy (Wh kg-1) and earth-abundance of sulfur, which is generally coupled with a high-energy density Li metal anode.1-3 Major efforts have been dedicated to alleviating the problem of the polysulfide shuttling by tailoring the cathode structure, 4 - 7 including using highly porous carbons, 8 - 11 graphene,12 decorated carbon,13 polymers,14 and metal oxides/sulfides/carbides as sulfur hosts.15-17 Much progress has been made in fabricating long-life and stable sulfur cathodes, which has brought lithiumsulfur batteries ever closer to the marketplace.

However, the community has gradually come to a consensus that this prosperity is mostly achieved in a “flooded” system, where a large electrolyte/sulfur (E/S) ratio is required (usually > 20 µl/mg).18,19 This is because of the catholyte nature of the sulfur cells in a conventional 1 M lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI) dioxolane/dimethoxyethane (DOL/DME) electrolyte. Unlike conventional lead-acid and Zebra batteries which also operate on the principle of dissolution-precipitation chemistry, the typical Li-S battery relies on considerable - or full - dissolution of the intermediate polysulfides, thus requiring sufficient electrolyte for this purpose. However, in order to achieve high celllevel energy density, considering the low density of sulfur, a low E/S ratio is essential to compete with current Li-ion technology.20, 21 To achieve this goal, one can utilize high donor number solvents such as dimethylacetamide,22 and dimethyl sulfoxide,23 which show high polysulfide solubility. However, their high reactivity with Li metal necessitates the use of a dense Li+-conducting protective membrane which is expensive for practical applications. Another avenue is to use solid electrolytes (SEs) that promise solidsolid sulfur conversion, free of polysulfide dissolution. 24 , 25 Nevertheless, the promised high energy density cannot be realized until ultra-thin membrane technology is in place and the interfacial challenges (SE/cathode, SE/Li) are solved.26,27 The intrinsic kinetics of solid-state conversion is another potential limiting factor. To achieve a low E/S ratio cell, it is important to decouple the electrolyte volume and sulfur speciation pathway. In light of the high polarization and low round-trip energy efficiency (EE) of non-solvent electrolytes, 28 sparingly solvating electrolytes that dissolve only a small fraction of polysulfides (< ~3% in a cell), emerge as a more promising Li+-conducting medium.29,30 In contrast to a complete solid-state reaction, the presence of a small fraction of polysulfides would enable solution-based polysulfide disproportionation reactions at the interface, which contributes to faster kinetics. Through such a unique reaction scenario - a quasi-solidstate reaction - one may achieve a balance amongst suppressed polysulfide shuttling, a low electrolyte volume fraction and fast reaction kinetics.31 One prerequisite for a long-lived low E/S cell is that the electrolyte is not consumed by parasitic reactions with the Li metal anode. Unlike the intercalation-based graphite anode that forms a stable SEI, Li metal reacts with the electrolyte, a reaction that is further catalyzed by high-surface area dendritic growth.32 In particular, solvent decomposition is a major contribution to the impeding SEI.32 In fact, Li metal cells usually fail due to electrolyte solvent degradation (complete solvent decomposition).33 This problem is obviously not an issue in a flooded system even over long-term cycling, but it is critical for a low E/S cell. Recently, concentrated salt-dimethoxyethane electrolytes have shown great promise in suppressing dendrites and improving SEI formation.34,35 These have been proposed to rely on Li-ether complex pair formation, similar to solvate ionic liquids (SIL) formed by the 1:1 complexes of triglyme 2|Page

Page 3

(CH3–O–(CH2–CH2–O)n–CH3; n = 3) or tetraglyme (n = 4) with LiFTSI salt.36 Seminal work showed that triglyme (“G3”): LiTFSI and tetraglyme (“G4”):LiTFSI SILs are efficient at reducing polysulfide shuttling in Li-S cells.37 However, such electrolytes still exhibit a “two plateau” electrochemical profile that is characteristic of catholyte type cells, which is limited in reducing the E/S ratio and extending cycle life. The acetonitrile (ACN) solvate electrolyte, initially studied for its efficacy in Li-ion cells, and later in Li-S batteries,30,38 exhibits serious decomposition in Li metal cells, as we will show, and is not feasible for low E/S sulfur cells. Furthermore, the emerging safety concerns for EV applications disfavor Li metal batteries, owing to Li dendrite growth and use of volatile electrolytes. It is important to design a nonflammable and non-volatile electrolyte,39 while eliminating Li dendritic growth and stabilizing the SEI. Here, taking inspiration from the SIL systems and with an aim at designing low E/S cells, we solve the challenges at both electrodes simultaneously by tuning the electrolyte structure. We clearly demonstrate - by experiment and computation - that by decreasing the solvent/salt molar ratio in a diglyme (G2) system (denoted as G2:LiTFSI), the sulfur reaction pathway transitions from a dissolutionprecipitation system to a quasi-solid-state conversion regime, thus decoupling the electrolyte and sulfur quantity while prohibiting polysulfide shuttling. Synchronously, we achieve non-dendritic growth and a stabilized SEI at the Li anode side. The structure of the G2 based system is fundamentally different from that of G3/G4 because its chain-length is the right dimension to fully wrap around the TFSI- anion and hence reduce the solvent activity. As the decomposition of the optimized electrolyte is reduced by 20-fold, electrolyte solvent degradation is mitigated, yielding a 4-fold extended lifetime for low E/S (5 µl/mg) sulfur cells. This is achieved at a mild temperature (55 oC), which is practically significant. The thermal management cost can be decreased by effectively utilizing the as-generated ohmic heat output.

Tuning the electrolyte structure The two figures-of-merit for designing such electrolytes are the polysulfide solubility and Li reactivity. Specifically, the electrolyte needs to solvate polysulfides only sparingly and to generate a stable SEI on Li. One route is to reduce the solvent activity, which we have achieved here by tuning the solvent/salt ratio. We prepared a series of electrolyte with varied G2/LiTFSI ratios (mol/mol), ranging from 7:1 to 0.8:1, using hydrofluorinated ether (HFE) as the diluent. G2 was selected because it is relatively stable with Li (unlike high-donor number solvents), and shows a more distinct solvate structure than other glymes, as shown below. Other solvate electrolytes based on DME/DOL (G1:LiTFSI [1.7:1])34, DME (G1:LiTFSI [1.4:1]), triglyme (G3:LiTFSI [0.91:1]) and tetraglyme (G4:LiTFSI [0.8:1]) at their respectively highest concentrations (i.e. saturated) were also prepared for comparison (see Methods and Table S1 in Supplementary Information).

We thus expect a reduced solvent activity for low G2:LiTFSI ratio electrolytes. Adding HFE does not significantly alter the solvation environment of Li+ (Figure S1b,c). Correspondingly, the polysulfide (Li2S6) solubility, as measured by UV-vis spectroscopy, shows a dramatic decrease as the G2:LiTFSI ratio decreases (Figure S2). We further found that among all saturated glyme electrolytes, G2-LiTFSI (0.8:1) exhibits the highest Li+-solvent coordination and thus lowest solvent activity, as indicated by the TFSI- band shift (Figure S1d). This is related to the chain length of the solvent molecules, as discussed below. As the G2:LiTFSI ratio decreases, the 7Li NMR spectra show a more positive chemical shift (ppm), indicating a more shielded nucleus, and thus stronger Li-O solvation (Figure 1b).40 The abnormally lower chemical shift of G2-LiTFSI (0.8:1) is probably due to a small fraction of Li-N and Li-F coordination in the first solvation shell (Figure 1d, Figure S3). The longitudinal relaxation time, T1, and full-width half maximum, FWHM, reveal the symmetry of the quadrupolar 7Li nucleus solvation environment (i.e. electron density), where a higher T1 and lower FWHM is indicative of higher symmetry.40 Here we observe a reduced solvation symmetry as the G2:LiTFSI ratio decreases, indicating the formation of a more locally defined complex in the first solvation shell. We evaluate the atomic coordination Z(r) around the Li+ cations by integrating the radial distribution functions on AIMD trajectories equilibrated at 55 °C (Figure 1d). Evidently, Li+ cations prefer a tetrahedral coordination with O atoms in TFSI- or G2 in all cases (Figure S3). The relative contribution of the O atoms belonging to TFSI- or G2 in the first solvation shell is strongly influenced by the G2:LiTFSI ratio (Figure 1e). In the dilute electrolyte (7:1), the first solvation shell around Li+ is comprised of one sulfonyl O (TFSI-) and three ethereal O (G2) atoms (Figure S3). Upon reducing the fraction of G2, the number of O (TFSI-) around Li+ progressively increases up to ~2.55 for the 0.8:1 electrolyte (Figure 1e, Figure S3). Likewise, the fraction of free G2 (not coordinated to any Li+) drops dramatically from ~71% (for 7:1) to ~12% (for 0.8:1) (Figure 1e, cyan in Figure 1f). Transition in sulfur redox chemistry The sulfur electrochemistry in these electrolytes was investigated at an elevated temperature (55 oC) using micron-sized sulfur to exclude possible nano-effects (Figure 2a). The discharge curves exhibit a transition from the conventional <slope-followed by-plateau> profile (centered at ~2.3 V and 2.1V; G2:LiTFSI [7:1]) to a very distinct <equipotential bi-plateau> profile (~2.2 V, G2:LiTFSI [1:1]), as the G2:LiTFSI ratio decreases. This indicates a different sulfur speciation pathway in the sparingly solvating electrolyte vs the catholyte case. The profile also distinguishes the behavior from a solid-solid conversion (as in solid-state cells) which typically show a single-slope profile,24 or from the behavior in the G3:LiTFSI or G4:LiTFSI systems which show a conventional <slope-followed by-plateau> profile.37 A three-electrode cell experiment excludes the Li anode as a contributor to the unique profile (Figure S7). We conducted galvanostatic intermittent titration (GITT) studies to confirm this behavior is thermodynamic in origin. The two electrolytes (G2:LiTFSI [7:1] and [1:1]) show distinct equilibrium voltage evolution as a function of state of discharge (SOD, Figure 2b). A systematic comparison of all the G2 electrolytes reveal a clear transition trend (Figure 2c). In the 1st stage before the knee point (~400 mAh g-1), the voltage decreases upon decreasing G2:LiTFSI ratio, whereas it increases in the 2nd stage. In general, a higher voltage corresponds to the presence of higher-order polysulfides and vice versa. The voltage relaxation kinetics during the OCV rest in GITT studies further supports a quasi solidsolid reaction (Figure S9). The voltage undergoes much faster relaxation to equilibrium for the lower G2:LiTFSI electrolytes because there are only a small fraction of solvated polysulfides on the surface, as opposed to the fully dissolved polysulfides in a catholyte cell that undergo extensive disproportionation reactions and exhibit a lag due to relatively slow diffusion. This also explains the slightly lower efficacy of polysulfide reduction and thus lower capacity for the G2:LiTFSI (1:1) than the (7:1) cell (Figure 2b). Unfortunately, the presence of a large fraction of S-containing LiTFSI in the electrolytes prevents us from accurately quantifying the polysulfides using X-ray absorption spectroscopy; 28,42,43 but the operando XRD studies that we describe below provide complementary information. Note that the other glyme solvate electrolytes show the same thermodynamic voltage profiles as the G2:LiTFSI (0.8:1) electrolyte, as confirmed by GITT studies (Figure S10). In order to evaluate the effect of sparing solubility, the long-term cycling of Li-S cells was investigated using a conventional E/S ratio of 15:1 (µl/mg) (Figure 2d). All cells were conditioned at C/15 for the first cycle before cycling at C/5; therefore, the capacity drop after the first cycle is largely due to the increase in rate. The cells with a lower G2:LiTFSI ratio generally show better capacity retention and higher coulombic efficiency (CE) (Figure S11a), with the G2:LiTFSI (0.8:1) showing the highest capacity retention of 83% over 100 cycles with an average CE of 99.51% (Figure S11b). The voltage profiles remain almost identical over cycling for the G2:LiTFSI (0.8:1), indicating the excellent reversibility of the S/Li2S redox (Figure S11c,d). The G2:LiTFSI (1:1) electrolyte shows inferior capacity retention, indicating polysulfide shuttling occurs even at such low polysulfide concentrations. Note that some wetting and activation cycles are necessary for the low G2:LiTFSI ratio electrolytes. We also observe significantly improved capacity retention compared to other glyme electrolytes (exemplified by G1:LiTFSI [1.4:1], G3:LiTFSI [0.91:1] and ACN:LiTFSI [2:1]; Figure S11e). Nevertheless, we observe slight capacity fading despite the absence of polysulfide dissolution/shuttling, which is owing to the partial occlusion of electrically disconnected sulfur/Li2S (the fraction of which is determined by cathode architecture). A quasi-solid-state conversion pathway for sulfur speciation To correlate the electrochemistry with the sulfur speciation pathway, we conducted operando XRD studies. Figure 3a,b shows the operando XRD patterns as a function of state of discharge/state of charge (SOD/SOC) for the sulfur cells using two representative G2:LiTFSI (1:1) and (7:1) electrolytes, respectively. XRD patterns for cells using electrolytes of other ratios are shown in Figure S13. Other than the Li2S and S8 reflections, no other diffraction peaks are visible. A clear trend of Li2S formation upon discharge and the re-formation of S8 upon charge exists for both electrolytes; however, the dynamics of sulfur consumption and re-formation is vastly different. The relative amount of each phase is quantified by integrating its major peak (Figure 3c). On discharge, we observe a delayed consumption of sulfur for electrolytes with lower G2:LiTFSI ratio, with the G2:LiTFSI (0.8:1) electrolyte completing sulfur consumption only at 42% SOD and G2:LiTFSI (7:1) at 21% SOD. At the same SOD, the lower G2:LiTFSI electrolytes thus have a lower fraction of sulfur, indicating a higher fraction of short-chain polysulfides,30 which is consistent with the GITT studies above. Note that crystalline Li2S starts to form at the respective “knee” points (voltage drop) in all electrolytes and its evolution generally follows the same trend, as representatively shown for G2:LiTFSI (1:1) (Figure 3c). Upon charge, the G2:LiTFSI (7:1) electrolyte shows formation of sulfur only towards the end of charge (82% SOC), coincident with the completion of Li2S consumption (Figure 3c, Figure S14), and consistent with the two-plateau charge profile. As the G2:LiTFSI ratio decreases, the electrolytes show earlier re-formation of sulfur (Figure 3c). We ascribe the different dynamics of sulfur consumption/re-formation to the different polysulfide solubility. Non-dendritic Li growth and high-efficiency Li plating/stripping Plating/stripping Li dendrite-free and at high coulombic efficiency is critical for a stable low E/S ratio LiS battery. We observe a vastly different Li plating morphology in the three electrolytes (G2:LiTFSI [7:1], [1.33:1], [0.8:1]), with a transition from thin needle-like dendrites to thick nodule-like islands (Figure 4ac).35 The microstructural variation results in different colors of the plated electrodes, where a finer microstructure is darker (insets, Figure 4a-c). The stripping from the Li metal is uniform and pit-free in low G2:LiTFSI electrolyte (Figure S16). In addition to improved safety, we anticipate a significant decrease of parasitic reactions with the electrolyte owing to the reduced electrode surface area. The decomposition of the solvent molecules generally results in an impeding and non-uniform SEI, whereas decomposition of the salt yields LiF.32 As shown by AIMD calculations, as the G2:LiTFSI ratio decreases, the solvent activity decreases and should result in diminished parasitic reactions. Longterm plating/stripping CE studies in Cu|Li cells allows us to evaluate the degree of parasitic reactions and to monitor the electrolyte status (Figure 4e). The G2:LiTFSI (0.8:1) and (1:1) electrolytes show stable cycling for 200 cycles at 1 mA cm-2 and 1 mA h cm-2, with an average CE of 96.2% and 95.8%, whereas the other electrolytes fail prematurely after 100 cycles with lower average CE (21.6-94.8%, Figure S17a). In particular, the G2:LiTFSI (7:1) shows a surprisingly low CE, 21.6%, with a short life of ~30 cycles (Figure S17 b). This means the electrolyte decomposition is greatly reduced by 20-fold each cycle for G2:LiTFSI (0.8:1). Measuring the CE using a different method following the work of Aurbach et al. reveals the same trend (Figure S17a).47 We disassembled the cells and found that all which failed showed complete electrolyte consumption. Further, the representative G2:LiTFSI (1:1) electrolyte shows stable cycling for ~ 400 hours at deep plating/stripping (4 mAh cm-2, Figure S17c). Surprisingly, we found the ACN:LiTFSI (2:1) electrolyte exhibits an extremely low CE (20-30%), even at low current density of 0.25 mA cm-2 (Figure S17d). This, along with the dark brown colored separator after cycling, is evidence of aggravated electrolyte decomposition. In fact, although all ACN molecules are complexed with Li+, upon Li plating, the rapidly de-solvated Li+ results in temporarily de-coordinated ACN that quickly reacts with Li metal. Therefore, this electrolyte is unsuitable for low E/S applications. Conclusions The ultimate goal to build a low E/S ratio Li-S battery cannot be realized without resolving the two central challenges: shuttling of the super-concentrated polysulfides and depletion of the electrolyte by the Li anode. Although efforts have been recently made on fabricating efficient binders and nanostructured hosts, simultaneous attempt to tackle electrolyte depletion has been overlooked. In fact, without eliminating electrolyte decomposition, efforts in optimizing the cathode structure would be fruitless upon long-term cycling. We demonstrate here that by tuning the electrolyte structure, we are able to optimize the two electrodes synchronously, as opposed to solving problems on only one side. By using a diglyme electrolyte with a saturated LiTFSI salt, G2:LiTFSI (0.8:1), we witness not only a transition in sulfur speciation pathway from the conventional dissolution-precipitation to a quasi-solid-state reaction - thus 17 | P a g e

Page 18

inhibiting polysulfide dissolution/shuttling - but also a greatly suppressed parasitic reaction with the Li anode which delays electrolyte depletion at a low E/S ratio. Our approach relies on establishing a network electrolyte structure – as proven by computational studies - that shows reduced solvent activity, which leads to sparing polysulfide solubility and low reactivity with Li. We showcased significantly improved capacity retention for sulfur cells using a low E/S ratio of 5 µl/mg (720 mA h g-1 over 100 cycles, as opposed to 420 mA h g-1 over 20 cycles for the control electrolyte). This class of electrolytes also demonstrates reduced flammability and high safety, which is essential for EV applications. Further development requires optimization of their physiochemical properties, including viscosity and conductivity, by screening the solvent molecules as well as the diluent co-solvents. Improving the sulfur and Li2S utilization in such a quasi-solid-state conversion regime, by rational design of redox mediators, for example, is also helpful to reduce the irreversible capacity. It is also necessary to build a smart structured cathode that can host and activate sulfur at a large areal loading, but which does not exhibit overly high porosity that uptakes a large fraction of inactive electrolyte.
