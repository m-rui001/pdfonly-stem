Lithium-sulfur batteries have great potential to satisfy the increasing demand of energy storage systems for portable devices, electric vehicles, and grid storage because of their extremely high specific capacity, cost-effectiveness, and environmental friendliness. In spite of all these merits, the practical utilization of lithium-sulfur batteries is impeded by commonly known challenges, such as low sulfur utilization (< 80 %), short life (< 200 cycles), fast capacity fade, and severe self-discharge effect, which mainly result from the (i) low conductivity of the active material, (ii) serious polysulfide shuttling, (iii) large volume changes, and (iv) lithium-metal anode contamination/corrosion. Numerous approaches have been reported to effectively mitigate these issues. Indeed, such approaches have shown enhanced lithium-sulfur battery performances. However, many reports overlook the critical parameters, including sulfur loading (< 13 mg cm-2), sulfur content (< 70 wt.%), and electrolyte/sulfur ratio (> 11 µL mg-1), that significantly affect the analyzed electrochemical characteristics, energy density, and practicality of lithium-sulfur batteries. In this review, we highlight the trends and progress in making cells fulfilling these fabrication parameters and discuss the challenges of the amount of sulfur and electrolyte in fabricating the cells with practically necessary parameters and with high electrochemical utilization and efficiency.

1. Introduction Electrical energy probably is the most important life-necessity for human beings to live in the modern society, in addition to oxygen and water. As the world’s population booms and the usage of electricity dramatically increases, the demand for fossil fuel, such as coal and oil, increases steadily in order to provide sufficient energy.[1–4] However, the exploitation of fossil fuel to generate energy has extreme adverse impact on human beings from environmental concerns to humanitarian and political concerns, in terms of undesired gas emissions (e.g., CO2, CO, CH4), air and water pollution, harm to public health, and global warming. Especially, more and more incidents are sparking grave concerns, such as abnormal climate change worldwide,[5–8] due to use of fossil fuels and the steady increase in global warming. Adoption of alternative energy generation technologies based on renewable resources (e.g., solar, wind, hydro-power, and geothermal) can effectively address the above-mentioned issues by both reducing the greenhouse gas emissions as well as our dependency on fossil fuel.[9–12] Even though the increasing feed-in of electricity from intermittent renewable energy sources is beginning to impact the generation of electrical energy, a major bottleneck preventing an efficient adoption of these alternative energy technologies is the lack of efficient and economical energy-storage technologies.[1,4,13] The impact of energy storage technologies is far-reaching; it not only tackles the concerns that have limited the penetration of renewable energy, but also fundamentally alters the longstanding relationship between utility companies and their customers.[3,9,12] Rechargeable batteries have emerged as one of the most promising options for electrical energy storage. Since Sony first commercialized the lithium-ion battery in the early 1990s, lithium-ion batteries have offered the most practical solutions to a wide variety of electrical energy storage applications from portable electronics to electric vehicles because lithium-ion batteries provide the highest energy density among the various rechargeable 2

Unfortunately, the capacities of the conventional insertion-compound cathode (e.g., LiCoO2) and anode (e.g., graphite) materials have reached their ceiling values of, respectively, ~ 250 mA h g-1 and ~ 370 mA h g-1. Thus, new battery systems and electrochemical technologies with higher capacity are being pursued.[14–16] Lithium-sulfur batteries are one of the most promising next-generation battery systems, which are able to provide an extremely high theoretical gravimetric energy density of 2,600 W h kg-1; and some companies have already reported ~ 400 W h kg-1 lithium-sulfur cells.[1,17– 20]

significantly high theoretical charge-storage capacity of 1,675 mA h g-1 and the lithium-metal anode that possesses a high theoretical capacity of 3,860 mA h g-1.[21,22] Additionally, sulfur is the 10th most abundant element in the earth’s crust, so sulfur is cost-effective (~ $150 per ton) as compared to the currently used transition-metal oxide cathodes, such as LiCoO2 (~ $10,000 per ton).[23,24] The lithium-sulfur battery system involves the reversible redox conversion reactions between active sulfur and its end discharge product, lithium sulfide (Li2S). In the discharge process, S-S covalent bonds of solid cyclooctasulfur (S8) are cleaved to form dissolved polysulfides (Li2Sx: x = 4 – 8) and then it is further reduced into solid Li2S through multiple-process reactions.[1,25] Although lithium-sulfur batteries are appealing, there are many obstacles that the researchers have to overcome in order to expand their market potential. Intrinsic properties of both sulfur and lithium electrodes are thought to be responsible for many of the challenging issues, including fast capacity fade, short cycle life, and high self-discharge rates. First, both sulfur (conductivity ~ 10-30 S cm-2) and its end-discharge product Li2S (conductivity ~ 10-14 S cm-2) are electronically and ionically insulating, greatly constraining the efficient electrochemical utilization of the active materials. The poor electrochemical utilization of the active material leads to low practical discharge capacity in lithium-sulfur cells that employ a conventional sulfur cathode configuration.[2] 3

Second, during cycling, sulfur electrochemically undergoes a multi-stage of complex compositional and morphological changes with the formation of polysulfide intermediates (Li2Sx: x = 4 – 8) that are highly soluble in the organic electrolyte commonly used in the lithium-sulfur system. The dissolved polysulfides have strong tendancy to migrate out from the cathode region through the porous polymeric separator to the anode region due to the chemical potential and concentration differences between the two electrode regions. The irreversible polysulfide migration leads to not only the active-material loss and the insoluble sulfide deposits on the cathode surface but also severe lithium-metal degradation and contamination in the anode. This phenomena is the so-called ―shuttling effect,‖ which is also the origin of the low Coulombic efficiency of lithium-sulfur batteries.[25,27] Third, a volumetric change of ~ 80 % between sulfur (density = 2.07 g cm-3) and Li2S (density = 1.66 g cm-3) along with the multiple-phase transition (solid(sulfur)-liquid(polysulfides)solid(sulfide)) during continuous cycling results in a devastation of the cathode structure and integrity. The damaged cathode structure would cause discontinuity of electronic pathways, leading to a serious irreversible capacity fade.[1] In addition to the issues of the sulfur cathode, the lithium-metal anode is equally challenging in lithium-sulfur batteries. A large portion of the failure of lithium-sulfur batteries can be attributed to lithium-metal pulverization (degradation), which is mainly induced by lithium-metal surface passivation, unstable solid-electrolyte interphase, as well as uncontrolled lithium dendrite growth.[21] These challenges lead to inefficient electrochemical utilization, low Coulombic efficiency, and limited cycling life of lithium-sulfur batteries. Although most strategies seem to have ―significant‖ improvements in the electrochemical performance of lithium-sulfur batteries, many of these solutions intentionally neglect some critical parameters that adversely affect the energy or power density and completely neutralize the advantages of the lithium-sulfur system. This review provides an overview of the generally neglected critical fabrication parameters in lithium-sulfur battery research: (i) sulfur loading, (ii) sulfur content, (iii) electrolyte/sulfur ratio, and (iv) battery testing settings. In over 10,000 lithium-sulfur research articles that have been reported until 2017, we selected and carefully analyzed the development trends of reports that have a sulfur loading of at least 10 mg cm-2 and present either the sulfur content or the electrolyte/sulfur ratio of the cell, which a large amount of literature often fails to report. The development of lithiumsulfur technology has been investigated along with the modification of the cell-fabrication parameters in view of the intrinsic problems (i.e., active material, discharge/charge mechanism, and electrochemical characteristics of the cells) and extrinsic problems (i.e., fabrication parameters, battery testing settings, cell capacity, cycle stability, and cycle life). Although the statistical analysis shows possibilities for the development toward practical lithium-sulfur cells, we do not currently have the specific progress in simultaneously boosting the cell performances and practical fabrication parameters. Therefore, in order to understand the real problems and realize solutions to make lithium-sulfur cells practically viable, more work and attention is needed to demonstrate cell performance using parameters that more accurately reflect realistic use.

2. Lithium-sulfur technology Energy is often most conveniently stored as chemical energy, a concept that has led to the development of a wide range of electrochemical energy technologies, many of which offer 5

Page 6

the reversible storage of electrical energy as chemical energy in the electrodes of a rechargeable battery.[26,55] Moreover, the use of conversion-reaction electrodes further bypasses the dependence on the limited crystallographic sites available in the structure of the conventional insertion-reaction electrodes to reversibly insert/extract lithium.[23,26,56] Lithium-sulfur batteries, with no restrictions in maintaining the initial crystal chemistry of the electrode during electrochemical cycling, can allow a full two-electron redox reaction per sulfur atom to occur in a manner that is both reversible and stable. As a result, sulfur cathodes possess a high theoretical capacity of 1,675 mA h g-1. Such an attractive charge-storage capacity is the highest among solid cathode materials for rechargeable lithium batteries and is an order of magnitude higher than that of the state-of-the-art lithium-ion battery cathodes.[16,26,55,57,58] Additionally, being composed of an earth-abundant element, sulfur cathodes are inexpensive and environmentally compatible.[55,59–62] The formation of polysulfides during charging and discharging further enhances the reaction capability of the sulfur cathode. Polysulfides, in the form of Li 2Sx (x = 4 – 8), are soluble in the ether-based liquid electrolyte commonly used in lithium-sulfur batteries, thus creating a catholyte that has strong electrochemical activity with high mobility.[17,63,64] When coupled with a lithium-metal anode, which also has a promising charge-storage capacity of 3,860 mA h g-1, the resulting lithium-sulfur cell promises a high theoretical energy density of 2,600 mW h g-1, a significant improvement compared to that of lithium-ion batteries with a typical graphite-LiCoO2 system (387 mW h g-1). These advantages make the lithium-sulfur batteries a strong candidate for next-generation energy storage devices, particularly for transportation and grid storage applications.[17,26]

2.2. Establishment of cell-fabrication standard Zhang demonstrated that the lithium-sulfur cell performance and electrochemistry are both sensitive toward the amount of sulfur and electrolyte used to fabricate the battery.[36,126,127] Thus, in order to demonstrate reasonable cell performance (i.e., sulfur utilization, cycle life, and cell stability) and for conducting electrochemical analysis (in terms of reversibility, efficiency, and stability of sulfur cathodes), the author proposed the lowest acceptable fabrication parameters for lithium-sulfur cells, including a sulfur loading of 2 mg cm-2 and a sulfur content of 65 wt.% for sulfur cathodes.[36,126,127] However, 60 % of the relevant research articles have utilized a sulfur loading of less than 2 mg cm-2. Additionally, the sulfur content is generally below 65 wt.% for reported pure sulfur and sulfur-based composite cathodes.[86,128,129] In 2013 and 2015, Hagen et al. conducted a series of studies exploring how to make lithium-sulfur cells outperform lithium-ion batteries. They determined that three criteria in fabricating the cells must be fulfilled, including (i) sulfur loading > 6 mg cm-2, (ii) sulfur content > 70 wt.% in the cathode, and (iii) an electrolyte/sulfur ratio of 3 μL mg-1 or lower.[78,130,131] Based on these three fabrication parameters, lithium-sulfur cells must attain a sulfur utilization of > 80 %, which is equal to a high reversible charge-storage capacity of 1,340 mA h g-1, to realize their promise as a next-generation rechargeable battery with high energy density. However, no study has been able to achieve all these criteria at the same time.

Besides the issue of a low amount of sulfur offering an overrated cell performance, the use of excess electrolyte in artificially boosting the performance of lithium-sulfur cells was also revealed in 2014 and 2015 by McCloskey[131] and Scheers et al.[132] Increasing the electrolyte/sulfur ratio dilutes the concentration of dissolved polysulfides in the cell and reduces the viscosity of the polysulfide-containing electrolyte. The result of technically masking the negative effects of polysulfides using a high electrolyte/sulfur ratio leads to a false image of high electrochemical performance, as the irreversible polysulfide migration is concealed and the energy density of the device is low.[131,132] As a result, although lithium-sulfur cells have great potential to advance the battery technology, a major challenge has been persistent overrated battery performance and chemistry. Limiting the amount of sulfur in the cathode and/or an excess of electrolyte added into cell decrease the cathode resistance. It also reduces uncontrolled polysulfide relocation. As a result, it is difficult to observe incurring sluggish electrochemical utilization and battery instability during chemical and electrochemical analysis.[17,19,86] These kinds of fabrication parameters are used to make the battery performance appear better than it truly is, but they reflect neither the realistic developments for achieving future progress nor the true problems for us to investigate and solve.

Intrinsic challenges of fabricating high-loading sulfur cathodes Lithium-sulfur battery performance is highly sensitive to the amount of sulfur used in addition to the electrolyte/sulfur ratio as a result of the material characteristics of sulfur, polysulfides, and sulfides that are produced during the full redox reactions of the cell.[17,19,133,134] Thus, studies have revealed that the recent progress made in lithium-sulfur technology is mainly due to a low amount of sulfur ( sulfur loading of < 7 mg cm-2 and sulfur content of < 65 wt.% in the whole cathode) and excessive electrolyte (electrolyte/sulfur ratios of > 11 μL mg-1).[17,19,86,134–136] Since the amount of sulfur and electrolyte used will impact the 11

3.1. Active material In a typical lithium-sulfur cell, lithium metal is used as the anode and functions as the lithium-ion reservoir. Meanwhile, the cathode can be made of various sulfur-containing species. The two electrodes are separated by an ion conducting liquid or solid electrolyte. The application of different active materials within the cathode has different advantages and challenges reported thus far.[17,57,78,105] Four main active materials have been intensively studied in lithium-sulfur research, including pure sulfur, sulfur-based nanocomposites, polysulfide catholyte, and lithium sulfides.[17,78,101,102] Appling sulfur directly as the active material is a promising cathode for lithium-sulfur batteries because of its chemical stability and low price. For example, sulfur is produced during the removal of H2S as a byproduct during the production of gasoline and liquefied natural gas, and as such the world supply (~ 15 million tons) clearly exceeds the demand. As a result, the price of sulfur is extremely low (~ $150 per ton). Additionally, sulfur is considered an environmentally friendly material as compared to the transition-metal compounds used in commercial lithium-ion battery cathodes.[16,26,55,58] Even though there are clear practical advantages of utilizing pure sulfur cathodes, such a design means that all the intrinsic limitations of the material are also present in the electrode, including the insulating nature of sulfur, uncontrolled polysulfide diffusion, and large volume changes of up to 80 %.[57,105] All these material challenges must be solved in order to attain high sulfur loading and content while reducing the electrolyte/sulfur ratio to as low as possible.[37,38,121]

The use of sulfur-based nanocomposites to address these issues is among the most mature techniques, and has other advantages including excellent compatibility with the fabrication methods applied in conventional lithium-ion battery cathodes. For these reasons, abundant simulation studies, analytical research, and review articles have been focused on the investigation of sulfur-based nanocomposites, resulting in high capacity and long cyclability of these cathodes compared to other active materials.[26,78] In 2015, the Xiao group presented a detailed study introducing a method for using sulfur-based nanocomposites in high-loading sulfur cathodes, increasing the sulfur loading from 2 to 8 mg cm-2 by modulating the electrode porosity and thickness coated onto a two dimensional aluminum-foil current collector. However, such high-loading sulfur cathodes encounter challenges of non-uniform coatings and cracks as well as the inability to sufficiently decrease the electrolyte/sulfur ratio because of the use of highly nanoporous substrates.[136,137] Similar to the limited sulfur loading, a lower sulfur content in composite cathodes can also cause overrated electrochemical performance. The reported sulfur content in most research articles are typically based on the sulfur content of the composite alone rather than the total sulfur content in the cathode, resulting in true sulfur content values that are lower than the recommended 65 – 70 wt.%.[19,86,135] Both pure sulfur and sulfur-based nanocomposites encounter the issue of the formation of polysulfides and their dissolution into the liquid electrolyte. Polysulfide dissolution affects lithium-sulfur battery performance in two very different ways.[64] To attain a high specific capacity and rate capability, lithium polysulfides must be dissolved into the liquid electrolyte to form a liquid-phase catholyte with high mobility and strong electrochemical activity. However, the high mobility of the liquid-state catholyte can also easily migrate to the anode side of the cell. The diffused polysulfide catholyte soon causes a severe polysulfide shuttling effect during the battery charging process, which initiates lithium corrosion, cathode degradation, and surface passivation on both electrodes.[63,138] Clearly, as opposed to the use 13

3.2. Discharge/charge reactions Upon cycling, lithium-sulfur cells require an activation process that typically takes ~ 10 cycles of operation. An initial capacity drop occurs after the first few cycles (1 – 5 cycles), which is necessary for the generated polysulfides to reach an equilibrium state within the cathode and electrolyte.[142,143] The same process is needed for cells fabricated with a high amount sulfur, which results in sluggish reaction kinetics. Furthermore, high-loading sulfur cathodes encounter fabrication problems, such as an inhomogeneous distribution of sulfur, including large clusters of the material, as well as the precipitation of the active material from the catholyte. Thus, the activation process of high-loading sulfur cathodes aims to make the insulating sulfur clusters utilizable by using the dissolved polysulfide that are formed during cell assembly and during initial cell cycling. This results in a detectable capacity increase during the first 10 – 20 cycles when a high-loading cathode is used in the cell.[40,122,144] After the activation process, the rearrangement of the active-material creates an intimate three-phase boundary between the sulfur, electrolyte, and the cathode substrate. Such electrochemically favorable sites at this interface help maintain the high electrochemical reversibility of the cathode.[17,63,64,133] 15

After the initial activation reaction, the cell’s discharge and charge capabilities dominate the reversible charge-storage capacity and Coulombic efficiency of the battery.[126,135,145] Figure 1 shows the illustrations of the discharge/charge voltage profile and cyclic voltammogram of lithium-sulfur cells. The discharge and charge mechanisms of lithium-sulfur cells can be divided into four discharge steps with different corresponding phase transitions and voltage regions, as well as continuous charge steps with two overlapping voltage regions.[78,107] Most published research articles begin with a discharge cycle for sulfur and sulfurbased nanocomposites to a cutoff voltage between 1.5 and 1.8 V.[78,107] The first discharge step (discharge step 1: from ~ 2.4 to ~ 2.2 V) involves a slow transition from solid-state sulfur to high-order liquid-state polysulfides, which relates to the reduction from sulfur into dissolved high-order polysulfides with a formula of Li2S8. The resulting polysulfides have high solubility in the ether-based liquid electrolyte, enabling them to dissolve in the medium and behave as a catholyte. The dissolved polysulfides subsequently activate the residential sulfur that was in the form of clusters in a gradual manner, causing the active material to slowly dissolve into the electrolyte.[78,79,107] With progressive discharging from ~ 2.3 to ~ 2.1 V (discharge step 2), the slow solidto-liquid phase transition completes and a fast liquid-to-liquid phase transition begins, involving the continuous reduction of high-order Li2S8 to low-order Li2S4 polysulfides. This reduction reaction proceeds in multiple steps, producing numerous polysulfide intermediates, such as Li2S8, Li2S6, and Li2S4 in a mixture.[36,78] Among these polysulfide mixtures, the dissolved Li2S8 and Li2S6 lithium polysulfides function as a catholyte.[78,101] In terms of the active material, polysulfide catholyte has strong electrochemical activity that can help facilitate the reaction kinetics of the sulfur cathode that is able to stabilize or localize the dissolved polysulfides within the cathode region. However, if the design of the cathode cannot hold the polysulfide catholyte, the dissolved polysulfides will irreversibly diffuse out of the 16

cathode due to their high mobility. The electrolyte solvent and electrolyte/sulfur ratio also greatly impacts the polysulfide solubility and solution viscosity.[26,36] This suggests that the properties and amount of electrolyte used will impact polysulfide dissolution and the resulting battery performance. In the next discharge step (discharge step 3: 2.1 V – 2.0 V), the low-order Li2S4 polysulfide reduces to form a mixture of insoluble, solid-state Li2S2 and Li2S. This liquid-tosolid phase transition contributes to the major capacity of the sulfur cathode, and its capacity and discharge voltage changes with the polysulfide reduction products.[57,107,108] With a comprehensive, deep discharge process down to 1.5 V (discharge step 4: ~ 2.1 to 1.5 V), the insoluble Li2S2 further reduces to insoluble Li2S. Due to the insulating nature of Li2S2 and Li2S, this solid-to-solid phase transition is kinetically slow and normally suffers high polarization. As a result, it is hard to observe whether the Li2S is the predominant reduction product, as it causes slightly lower discharge voltages.[26,36,126] Moreover, the use of LiNO3 as a co-salt to stabilize the lithium-metal counter electrode requires a higher discharge cutoff voltage of 1.6 – 1.8 V. In order to prevent the fast consumption of LiNO3, a relatively high discharge voltage is often used, which makes the transition between Li2S2 and Li2S incomplete and more difficult.[36,135,146] As shown in Figure 1, the reversible charging step usually starts with a small voltage drop due to the chemical dissolution (oxidization) of Li2S and Li2S2 mixtures by the electrochemically active polysulfides formed in the very beginning of this charging process. Thus, the insulating and insoluble Li2S and Li2S2 can be activated back into polysulfides, which is a solid-to-liquid phase transition.[36,126,147] In the subsequent charging step, polysulfide generates and dissolves in the electrolyte to form catholyte. The dissolved polysulfides again facilitate the conversion reaction by their strong activity for a smooth charge sequence and try to diffuse out of the cathode causing an irreversible drop in electrochemical utilization and efficiency.[26,57,145] In this sequence, the commonly used 17

LiNO3 co-salt in the electrolyte benefits the lithium-sulfur cell’s reversibility in both electrodes, as reported by Zhang. On the anode side, the formation of a stable passivation film by LiNO3 on the lithium-metal anode helps limit the polysulfide shuttle.[146,148] On the cathode side, the nitrate anions of LiNO3 catalyze the conversion of soluble polysulfides to slightly soluble elemental sulfur, which benefits the final liquid-to-solid phase transition.[31,36,146] Clearly, the reversibility and stability of the complex, multistep redox reactions that take place in a lithium-sulfur battery depend on our ability to design suitable active materials and cathode configurations that enable the reversible transitions. However, the intrinsically low conductivity of sulfur and its end-discharge products (i.e., lithium sulfides) causes high cathode resistance and impedance, which result in sluggish reaction kinetics. This drawback becomes severe when the amount of sulfur increases, challenging the development of commercial lithium-sulfur batteries.[16,57]

3.3. Electrochemical characteristics In between the charge and discharge stages, polysulfides form and dissolve in the ether-based liquid electrolyte. The dissolved polysulfides tend to diffuse out from the cathode region of the cells, which causes the irreversible loss of active material from the cathode as well as corrosion of the lithium-metal anode. When the amount of sulfur is increased, the quantity of dissolved polysulfides also increases, further exacerbating the negative effects.[63,138] At the end of each charge and discharge cycle, the liquid-state lithium polysulfides convert to solid-state sulfur and lithium sulfide. The deposition of these insulating end-charge and end-discharge products on the electrode surfaces causes strong interfacial impedance. The result is a fast capacity fade that shortens the cycle life of the battery.[16,57] The amount of sulfur used in the cathode significantly controls the interfacial impedance resulting from the

Page 19

sulfide and sulfur deposition on the electrode surfaces at the end of the charging and discharging states.[19,86] Besides the instabilities of the cathode, the dissolution of polysulfide into the liquid electrolyte inevitably increases its viscosity and decreases its lithium-ion transfer capability. The chemical instability and the incurring consumption of electrolyte brought about by the soluble polysulfides lead to unstable and fading Coulombic efficiency over time. The ratio of the electrolyte and sulfur directly impacts the ion transfer and the chemical stability of the electrolyte due to the relative concentration of the resulting soluble polysulfide.[57,131,132] In section 3, we summarized the intrinsic problems brought about by the active material, the complex reactions, and electrochemical characteristics of lithium-sulfur that we initially encounter during cell assembly and cell cycling. These intrinsic problems intensify when we focus on developing a high-loading sulfur cathode with low electrolyte consumption. Since the issue of the sulfur amount and electrolyte dosage can be controlled during cell fabrication, the section 4 below will summarize the potential and challenges of making a lithium-sulfur cell with as high an amount of sulfur and as low an electrolyte/sulfur ratio as possible while maintaining the cyclability of the cell. As there is an influx of research in this area, we chose to report work that only feature a sulfur loading of at least 10.0 mg cm-2 and that present either the sulfur content or the electrolyte/sulfur ratio of the cell.

5. Perspective Although an exponentially increasing number of work on lithium-sulfur battery technology has been reported in the past decades, it is still a long way to go before the real high-performance lithium-sulfur batteries can be successfully realized for practical applications. In order to accomplish the commercialization of the lithium-sulfur technology to replace the current state-of-art lithium-ion technology, achieving high capacities (> 80% sulfur utilization) along with a long cycle stability (> 200 cycles with 70% – 80% capacity retention) with the cells fabricated with a high-quality cathode attaining a suitable sulfur loading (> 13 mg cm-2) and sulfur content (> 70 wt.%) and lean electrolyte amount (E/S ratio < 4 µL mg-1) are essential.[14,17,19,31,33,126,136,155] For the purpose of demonstrating research progress and/or making a fair comparison, it is necessary to provide detailed experimental parameters, including sulfur content, areal sulfur loading, electrode size, E/S ratio, cycling rate, discharge capacity, and cycle numbers (Table 1). In addition to the cathode parameters, the parameters of lithium-metal anode, such as thickness, weight, and size are also critical, as they significantly affect the electrochemical performance. Therefore, it is highly encouraged to provide a table containing these critical parameters to show the electrochemical performance and explore the remaining challenges.

6. Conclusion In this report, we show that the primary focus of lithium-sulfur battery research should be on the design of high-performance sulfur cathodes that exhibit high electrochemical efficiency and stability while attaining sulfur loadings and contents as high as possible along with low electrolyte/sulfur ratios. We clarify the challenge of making a high-performance cathode with these conditions due to intrinsic material limitations and extrinsic fabrication parameters and cell-testing settings; these parameters have historically been used to mask poor electrochemical performance. Although a statistical analysis shows progress in the development toward practical lithium-sulfur cells, more work and attention are needed to demonstrate cell performance using parameters that more accurately reflect realistic use. In recent years, the lithium-sulfur community has proven the ability to design cells fabricated with high-loading sulfur cathodes with a three-dimensional current collector (13 mg cm-2), high sulfur content (> 70 wt.%), and low electrolyte/sulfur ratios of less than 4 µL mg-1, though only separately and not altogether. Although it is unpleasant to admit, much of the major progress completed on lithium-sulfur batteries has been based on overrated material properties and cell performances. Many of the challenges of lithium-sulfur batteries are still related to the existing, unsolved issues surrounding the sulfur cathode, including inferior sulfur utilization, low discharge/charge efficiency, poor cyclability, and unacceptably short cycle lives. In consideration of the fact that lithium-sulfur cells have sensitive and vulnerable electrochemical characteristics that depend on the amount of sulfur and electrolyte used, more research should be directed to provide experimental data and analysis based on cathodes featuring high sulfur loading and content in cells with low electrolyte/sulfur ratios. By way of understanding promising high-loading cathode studies, more advances and standards or benchmarks could be made. Meanwhile, the presentation of inferior high-loading cathode

The focus of lithium-sulfur batteries is the design of high-performance sulfur cathodes that have sulfur loadings and contents as high as possible along with a low electrolyte/sulfur ratio. The challenges of making high-performance cathodes with these conditions stem from the intrinsic materials limitations and extrinsic cell-fabrication parameters and cell-testing settings that have historically been used to mask poor electrochemical performance.
