Nørskov∗,†,‡ †SUNCAT Center for Interface Science and Catalysis, Department of Chemical Engineering, Stanford University, Stanford, CA 94305–5025, USA ‡SUNCAT Center for Interface Science and Catalysis, SLAC National Accelerator Laboratory, Menlo Park, CA 94025, USA ¶Central Research Institute, Mitsubishi Materials Corporation, 1002–14 Mukohyama, Naka, Ibaraki 311–0102, Japan E-mail: norskov@stanford.edu Abstract Electrochemical reduction of CO2 has the potential to reduce greenhouse gas emissions while providing energy storage and producing chemical feedstocks. A mechanistic understanding of the process is crucial to the discovery of efficient catalysts, and an atomistic description of the electrochemical interface is a major challenge due to its complexity. Here, we examine the CO2 → CO electrocatalytic pathway on Ag(111) using density functional theory (DFT) calculations and an explicit model of the electrochemical interface. We show that the electric field from solvated cations in the double layer and their corresponding image charges on the metal surface significantly stabilizes key intermediates—*CO2 and *COOH. At the field-stabilized sites, the formation of *CO is rate-determining. We present a microkinetic model that incorporates field effects and electrochemical barriers from ab initio calculations. The computed polarization curves show reasonable agreement with experiment without fitting any parameters.

Efficient electrochemical reduction of carbon dioxide (CO2 ) would simultaneously mitigate its effect on the climate, provide a sustainable, carbon-neutral means to store chemical energy, and produce feedstocks of carbon-containing molecules for the chemical industry. 1 One of the simplest reduced products that can be made from CO2 is carbon monoxide (CO), which requires only two proton-electron transfers. Noble metals such as Ag and Au have been shown to be the best catalysts for this process. However, the process still requires a sizable overpotential and it has been hypothesized that the mechanism is limited by the endergonic reaction energy of the first intermediate, adsorbed CO2 . 2–6 Several experimental studies have also shown a pH effect, in which the overpotential for this reaction at pH 14 is lower than that at pH 7. 7–9 A mechanistic understanding of the CO2 reduction process is crucial to the design of more active catalysts. An ab initio investigation of the kinetics is very challenging due to the complexity of the electrochemical interface, where an accurate description of all components (ions, solvent, interfacial charge, and potential) is required. 10–14 Recent developments in methodology have allowed the study of charged surfaces, 15,16 and many approaches have been henceforth used in modeling electrochemical reactions. These include: the computational hydrogen electrode (CHE), 17 the surface charging (SC) method, 18,19 the linearized Poisson-Boltzmann (PB) equation, 19,20 and the self-consistent continuum solvation (SCCS) scheme. 21,22 In this Letter, we use density functional theory (DFT) and an explicit model of the electrochemical interface to investigate the energetics of the CO2 → CO pathway on Ag(111) and compare this to vacuum calculations using the CHE model. We show that, under realistic electrochemical conditions, the adsorbed CO2 intermediate is significantly stabilized by local electric fields exerted by solvated cations and their corresponding image charges at the electrode|electrolyte interface. We develop a microkinetic model that incorporates electric field effects and electrochemical barriers determined through ab initio calculations, and show that this model accounts reasonably for the experimentally observed polarization

The effect of applied potential are included through the CHE model, where the chemical potential of an electron changes with the RHE potential via −eU . 17 Within this model, the electrostatic potential is global and affects the energy of the electrons in the electrode. In an explicit solvation picture, the field effect is local and affects the energy of adsorbates outside the surface. The CHE model then allows us to separate the effect of electrochemical potential from other physical influences at the electrochemical interface. Typically, a barrier of 0.75 eV is considered to be the upper limit for facile kinetics, as it yields a turnover frequency of ∼1 s−1 at room temperature. On Pt(111), the activation of CO2 is facile even at room temperature. The formation of *CO2 as an intermediate before the coupled proton-electron transfer is only moderately uphill in free energy. CO production is still slow on Pt(111), however, due to the strong adsorption of CO which limits *CO desorption. 9,26,27 On Ag(111), on the other hand, both *CO2 and *COOH intermediates are high in free energy, and *CO desorption is facile at the equilibrium potential. Figure 2b shows the free energy diagram for Ag(111) at −0.11 vs. RHE that includes an applied field of −1.0 V/Å. As discussed in what follows, this local field strength is typical of solvated cations at the double layer and the corresponding energy is obtained via Equation 5. The field leads to a significant shift in the free energy of *CO2 (and, to a lesser extent, *COOH), such that the first step is no longer thermodynamically difficult. Therefore, we expect substantial field effects on the CO2 → CO kinetics over Ag and other weakly adsorbing surfaces. For more reactive surfaces such as Pt, this effect should be considerably smaller, since *CO2 is already quite stable there and therefore any field stabilization will not be seen in the kinetics (Figure 2a). Now, we address field effects on adsorbed CO2 in detail. Field effects on adsorption energies have been studied extensively in the literature both to explain alkali promotion in

Page 6

heterogeneous catalysis 28–30 and as a basis for understanding surface electrochemistry. 31–36 Field effects on an adsorbate are strongly dependent on its dipole moment and polarizability: many adsorbates show rather small field effects, such as the common intermediates in the oxygen reduction and evolution reactions. 34,35 An exception is the C–C coupling reaction energy where significant effects have been found on important intermediates. 33 From a modeling perspective, the field effect can be investigated in two ways, via (1) applying an external dipole potential in vacuum, and (2) explicitly considering adsorption at an Ag(111)|water interface (see Figures S3–S5 in the SI for calculation details). We examine the effects of potassium (K+ ) and 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium (EMIM+ ) in the current study, since experiments have suggested that these ions lower the overpotential for CO production. 7,8,37,38 Alkali cations have been hypothesized to adsorb to the electrode surface 7,39 or interact with surface adsorbed species non-covalently. 40,41 Surface X-ray scattering data have suggested some alkali and alkaline earth cations to be solvated and not adsorbed at the electrode surface. 42 EMIM+ has been shown to have a high coverage on metals due to strong van der Waals interactions. 43 EMIM+ has also been hypothesized to catalyze the CO2 → CO mechanism through specific chemical bonding, however the exact nature of this remains unclear 44,45 and we focus only on the field effect in the present study. In contrast to classical models of the electrochemical interface, the field effect is localized on the atomic scale. Figure 4 shows the cation-induced electric fields taken from a z-slice near the center of adsorbed CO2 with K+ solvated at the surface (see Equations S4 and S5 in the SI for details) at a coverage of 1/36. This ion coverage corresponds roughly to a potential of −0.5 V vs. SHE, which is calculated with Equation 12 in the following section. Where the electrochemical interface is explicitly simulated, accurately evaluating the electrochemical potential has been a challenge. 48,49 One of the main problems being that the solvent dipoles, which should average out to 0 in a real physical system at room temperature, 50 contribute significantly to the work function obtained in our simulations. Therefore, no straightforward mapping between the true electrochemical potential and the work function obtained from our simulations exists. Hence, in the present work we have used a classical approach by referencing the potential of zero charge (pzc) and relating the coverage to a realistic potential using Equation 12. Although the field is large in the vicinity of the K+ ion (on the order of −1 V/Å), it decays to ∼0 V/Å outside a ∼5 Å radius. Therefore, only *CO2 in the immediate vicinity 8

of the ion would be affected by the induced field. The atomistic picture also differs from a macroscopic model where the energy of the adsorbate varies continuously with an applied field: here the field is binary—either “on” or “off”—and is always on the order of −1 V/Å in the “on” sites. We find the average field over the entire cell (at this z-slice) to be −0.36 V/Å, which would correspond roughly to that expected from a classical Helmholtz model for an interfacial potential drop of ∼1 V and Helmholtz layer thickness of ∼3 Å. Several studies in the literature have shown that the overpotential for this pathway is considerably greater at pH 7 compared to that at pH 14. 7–9 Using only values obtained from DFT calculations and no fitted parameters, our kinetic model reproduces the onset potential in the experimental data with reasonable agreement, as shown in the Figure 6 inset. At larger current densities, transport limitations can contribute and these effects are not captured in the current model. The dependence of electrochemical rates on the absolute rather than the RHE potential have led to the hypothesis that the potential-determining step is an electron transfer decoupled from the corresponding proton transfer. 61–63 Our computed polarization curves suggest that this dependence can simply arise from step 3, where the barrier decreases as a function of absolute potential. In summary, we have shown that local cation-induced fields have a substantial stabilizing effect on adsorbed CO2 . These cations shift the free energy landscape significantly for the CO2 → CO reaction on weakly adsorbing metals such as Ag so that CO2 adsorption is facile, and the *COOH → *CO step becomes potential-limiting instead. Theoretical and experimental polarization curves show reasonable agreement at experimental onset potentials. The implication is that electric field effects should be included to model CO2 reduction in general, since many intermediates are expected to possess large dipoles or polarizabilities whose energies would be affected significantly by an electric field. These dipole and polarizability values can be calculated in vacuum and added as an additional term in the CHE model since they agree well with explicit solvent calculations. Further work will focus on field effects on CO2 reduction intermediates and transition states beyond CO, which could have significant impact on the resulting kinetics.
