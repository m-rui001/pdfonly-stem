Abstract Solar cells employing a halide perovskite with an organic cation now show power conversion efficiency of up to 22%. However, these cells are facing issues towards commercialization, such as the need to achieve long-term stability and the development of a manufacturing method for the reproducible fabrication of high-performance devices. Here, we propose a strategy to obtain stable and commercially viable perovskite solar cells.. A reproducible manufacturing method is suggested, as well as how to manage grain boundaries and interfacial charge transport. Electroluminescence is regarded as a metric to approach theoretical efficiency. We highlight how optimizing the design of device architectures is important not only for achieving high efficiency but also for hysteresis-free and stable performance. We argue that reliable device characterization is needed to ensure the advancing 1

Introduction Organic-inorganic halide perovskite was first used as a potential light harvester in a liquidelectrolyte-based dye-sensitized solar cell structure in 2006 [1]. Already by 2009, there were hints of the potential of MAPbI3 (MA = CH3NH3) and MAPbBr3 for photovoltaic (PV) applications, although their power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) were only as high as 3%– 4% [2]. In a subsequent study, in 2011, the PCE was doubled by optimizing precursor solution concentration and annealing temperature [3]. The absorption coefficient of MAPbI3 was measured to be one order of magnitude higher than that of ruthenium-based dye (coded N719), thus pointing to a very promising PV material. However, the high solubility of MAPbI3 in in polar liquid electrolytes prevented follow-up studies on perovskite solar cells (PSCs) until solid-state PSCs were first reported [4], confirming the long-term stability of the unencapsulated device in a 500-hour ex-situ light-soaking test. Such stability was attributed to a moisture-tolerant morphology consisting of wrapping nanoscale MAPbI3 dots with holetransporting material (spiro-MeOTAD). Shortly thereafter, a meso-superstructured solid-state PSC employing Al2O3 as a scaffold layer was reported [5], where the thin MAPbI3 layer deposited on Al2O3 transported photoexcited electrons that were collected by an electrode via a thin hole-blocking TiO2 layer. Reports on solid-state PSCs triggered a wave of research on perovskite PV. As a result, a certified PCE of 22.1% was reported in 2016 on the National Renewable Energy Laboratory’s best efficiencies chart [6]. Unlike other devices on such chart, PSCs are marked as “not stabilized”, indicating that validations with stable devices are still required. In addition to stability problems, the observed discrepancy in reverse and forward scanned current-voltage (I-V) curves—so-called I-V hysteresis—is an obstacle to commercialization because of the imprecise estimation of PV parameters. Methodologies for Controlling Grains and Grain Boundaries Regardless of device configuration, the quality of the perovskite layer is of critical importance with regard to obtaining high efficiency. Several methodologies have been proposed to obtain high-quality perovskite films, with the most studied method being the solution-based crystallization via spin-coating of a precursor solution. Post-treatment with antisolvent of MAPbI3 or an intermediate compound is state-of-the-art technology [7,8]. However, very recently a simple alternative vacuum flash assisted deposition method was developed that avoids the use of antisolvents. It enabled reaching a certified record PCE of 19.6% for a 1 cm2 sized device, opening a new promising path to production on an industrial scale [9]. The quality of the perovskite grains is the primary factor influencing PV performance, with crucial importance being ascribed to minimizing non-radiative recombination. The Lewis acid-base adduct approach can serve to reduce such recombination because high-quality perovskite films can be prepared reproducibly via adduct of MAI.PbI2.DMSO [10]. Figure 1a-c schematically displays the procedures of the antisolvent (including the fast deposition-crystallization) and the adduct approaches, as well as the morphology of the resultant perovskite grains. Stoichiometric control is an advantage of the adduct approach. Lewis basicity plays an important role in controlling grain size and crystallinity. For instance, thiourea was found to be better than DMSO for producing FAPbI3 (FA = HC(NH2)2) [11].

Mesoscopic vs Planar Structure Because the first PSCs were developed from dye-sensitized solar cells (DSCs), their architecture employed a mesoporous TiO2 film as a scaffold. Its role was to support the perovskite nanoparticles that replaced the molecular sensitizer as a light harvester and to selectively extract electrons from the photo-excited pigment [16]. However, the observation that organic-inorganic halide perovskite can on its own conduct electrons and holes led to further evolution of the device architecture. The mesoscopic embodiment employs a semiconductor oxide scaffold, whose pores are fully infiltrated with the perovskite that protrudes above the nanostructure to form a capping layer. In planar embodiments of a PSC, a compact perovskite film a few hundred nanometers thick is sandwiched between electron and hole selective contacts [16,17]. The simple structure of planar PSCs would suggest that ultimately all devices may adopt this configuration. However, at present mesoscopic systems - with a certified power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 22% - still hold a substantial lead over planar embodiments whose highest certified PCE is 15.6% [18]. Assuming a charge carrier (exciton) diffusion length of at least 100 nm and a pore radius of 10 nm, the collection efficiency by the mesoporous PSC would be greater than 99%. In order to collect more than 99 % of photogenerated carriers for a planar film of 500 nm thickness and a light absorption length 1/α = 250 nm, the diffusion length should be longer than 2.5 μm, which is difficult to reach with PSCs over large areas. Thus the power of the mesoscopic PSC architecture is that it greatly facilitates collection of photogenerated charge carriers enhancing the potential of perovskite as light harvesting materials in future photovoltaic deployment. It should be noted that the line between planar and mesoscopic cell architectures is often difficult to draw. Frequently “planar” embodiments employ a nanocrystalline oxide films as electron capture layer [21] and moreover the fluoride tin oxide (FTO) covered glass substrates supporting the perovskite are themselves highly corrugated. Conversely, the efficiency of PSCs endowed with a mesoscopic scaffold is boosted further by endowing it with a compact perovskite capping layer. Presently, the latter architecture (mesoscopic) gives a certified PCE of 21% [6].

Electroluminescence as a Metric for High Efficiency The electroluminescence features of fully operational PSCs allow deriving important insight on the key performance metrics of the device, in particular its Voc. For perovskites of composition FA0.85MA015PbI2.55Br0.45, the maximum obtainable Voc measured under standard air mass 1.5 solar radiation is 1.32 V [22], which is about 230 mV smaller than their band gap due to entropic losses [23]. These losses result from a gain in spatial disorder as the direct light from the sun is received within a very small spatial angle, whereas it is reemitted in all directions when the cell is held at open circuit where no photocurrent (Jsc) can flow. In line with these theoretical predictions, the electroluminescence quantum yield of PSCs has been found to correlate with its Voc value. To study the electronic quality of the device and identify the recombination mechanisms, the electroluminescence was recorded under forward bias in the dark with the device operating as a light-emitting diode [22]. The external electroluminescence quantum efficiency (EQEEL) increased linearly with injection current as expected for a device with ideality factor of 2 and approaches 0.5 % for currents in the range of JSC. This value translates into a voltage loss of kT ln(1/EQEEL) = 0.14 V confirming the measured Voc = 1.32 V – 0.14 V = 1.18 V, where 1.32 V is the theoretical maximum for Voc obtained in the radiative limit [22]. An EQEEL of 0.5 % is a record for solution-processed solar cells matching that of the best silicon solar cells [25]. The electroluminescence is orders of magnitude more intense than that of organic solar cells, where the EQEEL is commonly < 10-6 , and among the highest even when compared to perovskite based LEDs. The PSC delivered overall electric power-to-light conversion efficiency of 0.5 % at a voltage of 1.5 V, which is still below the band gap, whereas reported LEDs show the same efficiency at forward bias voltages greater than 3 - 4 V. From these studies, electroluminescence emerges as a powerful diagnostic tool to gauge the electronic quality of the perovskite films as well as the charge carrier selectivity of the contacts, and ascertain balanced injection and transport of electrons and holes.

For the normal PSC architecture based on TiO2 ETL (especially with the planar structure), the unbalanced charge extraction is one of several potential causes for hysteresis in the I-V characteristics. Much effort has been devoted to modifying TiO2 for better charge extraction (e.g., increasing contact area, doping, adding a fullerene modifier, or using SnO2) [26]. A typical inverted PSC using PCBM ETL often exhibits less hysteresis [27]. This is generally attributed to PCBM passivating defects in perovskites, but iodide migration within perovskite toward PCBM could chemically dope PCBM, increasing its conductivity for improved electron extraction [28]. Thus, the interaction of charge-transport layers with the ionic species in perovskites should be considered carefully. It is noteworthy that the kinetics of electron extraction at the ETL/perovskite interface can be affected by hole extraction at the HTL/perovskite interface. It has been shown that by using an HTL based on a thin layer of semiconducting single-walled carbon nanotubes (sSWCNTs), the unique excitonic transitions of the s-SWCNT HTL allow to unambiguously separate the electron- and hole-transfer kinetic pathways [29]. The s-SWCNT HTL facilitates sub-picosecond hole extraction, and the subsequent back-transfer of holes to MAPbI3 takes hundreds of microseconds, which can be explained by the preferred band bending at the perovskite/s-SWCNT interface [30]. Interestingly, the presence of the s-SWCNT HTL also improves electron extraction from the TiO2 ETL, suggesting that the efficacy of the TiO2 ETL can be affected by the HTL material. Substitution of Organic Cation to improve Material Stability Most high-performing PSCs are based on perovskite film they need encapsulation of the whole device as the PCE is sensitive to ambient conditions. The encapsulation process must be carried out carefully so as not to include any trace of moisture because H2O in the device accelerates degradation under heat and/or illumination [35]. Although investigations of degradation and stability issues have been carried out [36], no systematic studies have been carried out in terms of a stability test based on the International Electrotechnical Committee (IEC) standard. These tests include long-time exposure to UV-light, which may induce photocatalytic processes leading to device degradation. However, UV-excitation of the mesoscopic TiO2 scaffold and the perovskite is largely prevented by the FTO conducting glass support and compact TiO2 or SnO2 layer which filters out the UV photons Besides encapsulation, material engineering should be considered to prevent the decomposition of the perovskite film. The use of mixed halide and/or the reduction of structural dimensionality are potential paths; the suppression of chemically labile organic cation is an effective way to achieve relatively stable perovskite materials. FAPbI3, expected to have better performance than MAPbI3 because of the extension of absorption to 840 nm, is however much less stable than MAPbI3 in humid conditions. Moisture instability of FAPbI3 is due to weaker interaction between the FA cation with (PbI3)-. Replacing 10% of the FA ion with inorganic Cs ion was found to improve moisture stability, and thereby, photo-stability [37]. Lattice contraction by incorporating Cs ion in an A-site enhanced chemical stability of the FA ion in the cubo-octahedral cage, which is mainly responsible for improving stability. Thus, perovskite material engineering is another method for fabricating stable PSCs.

Although several causes of hysteresis have been proposed, ionic migration is thought to be the most probable one [39]. Similar to silver halide ionic crystals and semiconductors, hybrid perovskite possesses photovoltaic activity and ionic conductivity simultaneously. Although static dipole polarization and/or weak ferroelectric polarization under external bias has been experimentally observed for some perovskite samples, polarization can also occur as a result of localized ionic distribution. Ionic migration is definitely a crucial factor that affects the carrier dynamics in perovskite, impacting the I-V hysteresis. However, in regard to the study of hysteresis, two aspects are important. First, the material used for hysteresis research should be physically and thermally stable. Hysteresis has been investigated mostly for MA-based lead halide perovskite because of its high voltage and high efficiency. Here, ionic migration assists the generation of high voltage by suppressing recombination in terms of screening charged defects and electron/hole interaction. However, being loosely bound to Pb, MA is thermally unstable, and MA perovskite undergoes tetragonal-to-cubic transition at 60 °C. This temperature-sensitive property also leads to irreproducibility of hysteresis. Because of this nature, MA perovskite will be difficult to be adopted by the solar cell industry. Although fundamental analysis gives important insight in the design of materials, hysteresis suppression in MA perovskite itself is not an important subject for the goal of solar cell engineering. Rather, design of hysteresis-less perovskite cells should be directed to other practically useful (heat-resistant) perovskites such as Cs and FAbased materials. The same research direction is required for improvements in efficiency and durability. A second important aspect is to recognize the influence of device structure on hysteresis. Typically, heterojunctions with PCBM and polymer HTL provide high-efficiency performance without hysteresis. This fact corroborates the idea that ionic migration does not prevent high performance of PV devices. This fact also indicates that the emergence and/or enhancement of hysteresis could be related to the imperfect interfacial structures in the device rather than the bulk properties of perovskite. Reliable Device Characterization Reliable device characterization is important as part of a strategy towards stable and commercial solar cells because power generation figures (such as watt peak) from PV module must be precisely quantifiable. Recently, attention has been drawn to guidelines for accurate cell efficiency reporting, to help avoiding reports of artificially enhanced efficiencies in PCSs [40-42]. The procedures for measuring the PCE of solar cells was initially established in 1984 and involve the use of a reference cell to measure the light level and a spectral correction factor [43]. The area of many thin-film solar cells is determined by the ohmic contact area and not by the semiconductor area, giving rise to the possibility of current being collected from outside the cell area. Thin-film PSCs are typically illuminated through a glass window whose thickness approaches the cell dimensions, increasing the possibility of light impinging on the cell from outside the cell area [44]. For these reasons, small-area PSC’s must be measured with an aperture. Many PSCs are slow-responding and degrade rapidly, which is problematic when measuring their I-V characteristics. Values of FF can change with bias rates as low as 1 mVs-1, and for some devices the bias rate dependence cannot be modeled as a capacitance effect because the photocurrent changes with bias rates as low as 0.5 mVs-1. For this reason, methods have been developed to wait at a given voltage until the current has stabilized. Examples of this asymptotic I-V measurement are given in Figure 4. Issues of cost and

Environmental Impact

Lead is an abundant metal on earth. The material cost of a lead iodide perovskite absorber layer (~400 nm thick) is quite low, around 2 USD per sq. m of module. Perovskite is the cheapest component in the structure, whereas the most expensive material is the transparent conductive substrates (FTO or ITO) of low sheet resistance (<10 ohm per sq.), which cost around 10 USD per sq. Integration of all material costs, including carrier-transport layers and encapsulation materials, gives an estimated minimal value of around 20 USD per sq. m. If carrier-transport and conductive layers, which have wide variation in cost, are selected from cheap materials such as carbon and metal oxides (NiO, etc). A significant factor in cost 14

reduction enabled by perovskite solar cell is the high-throughput solution process (e.g., slot die coating), which is key to minimize the total material cost in mass production. Without high temperature crystallization and wafer slicing processes, manufacturing lines are estimated to cost roughly one-third of those for commercial multicrystalline silicon solar cells. Hence, the total cost for PSCs (except the system costs with installation, power conditioners, etc.) is estimated to be around 40 USD per sq. m., which is half the cost of silicon solar cells (80 USD per sq. m. or 0.5 USD/W). With efficiency comparable with silicon solar cells— namely, 15%–20%— low-cost electric power (<0.3 USD/W) is expected to be realized by large-scale production of PSCs. For perovskite modules with a PCE of 17%, the energy payback time can be less than 0.7 year, with an energy return factor of greater than 8. PSCs can definitely be a major contributor to a low-carbon society. However, lead is toxic and its environmental impact should be assessed

before

commercialization lead PSCs [46, 47]. The Restriction of Hazardous Substances (RoHS) of the European Union strongly regulates the use of Pb in industry. However, this rule exempts lead used in installed solar cells. In fact, metallic lead is employed as “heat-resistant” solders in today’s commercial solar cell modules. For PSCs, a module with a 0.4-μm-thick perovskite layer would contain an amount of ~0.4 g lead per sq. m, which is less than one-fiftieth of the amount of lead solder used in commercial silicon modules (except for non-lead-solder-type modules). Unlike metallic lead, lead ion (Pb2+) contained in perovskite can be easily recovered in the form of an aqueous solution by washing this ionic crystal with an acid solution such as aqueous acetate or nitrate. Solubility of perovskite bears the risk of leakage into the environment by rain water, but the positive aspect is that Pb can be recovered by simple solution treatment. The collected Pb2+-containing solution can be returned to natural soil to allow spontaneous oxidation of Pb2+ to lead oxide. In fact, natural soil contains lead oxide equivalent to 10 to 100 mg of Pb per 1 kg [48]. In other words, a 1-m2 PSC module corresponds roughly to 1-cm-thick natural soil of equivalent area that contains the same amount of lead as is contained in the module (see Figure 5). So, a protocol for lead recovery is expected to be effective for PSCs. Implementation of a water-based preparation of the perovskite absorber may be an effective way toward a water-based lead recovery process for PSCs.

Outlook Although PCEs exceeding 20% have been achieved in organic-inorganic halide perovskite, commercialisation of these cells will require the development of high-efficiency, large-area solar cells (and modules) with long-term stability and no I-V hysteresis. The strategy discussed above - encompassing optimisation of perovskite film formation; the use of electroluminescence as a tool to evaluate the film quality; optimisation of device architectures; grain boundary and interface engineering – provides a possible path towards commercialisation of PSCs. Despite significant progress on perovskite film synthesis and device architecture engineering, fundamental understanding of key aspects of these interfaces (e.g., interfacial energetics and charge transfer/recombination kinetics) is still inadequate. In particular, efforts on design and characterization are needed to understand the dynamic physical/chemical interactions at and coupling between various interfaces, as well as their impact on device operation characteristics (e.g., hysteresis and stability). Establishing reliable protocols to properly evaluate PSC efficiency and stability has become critically important for PSC development 16

toward commercialization. Finally, applications of halide perovskites are not limited to power devices, and are spreading into diverse fields. Several optoelectronic devices have been made of hybrid perovskite compounds [49]. Perovskite-based photodiodes can be used for high-responsivity light and X-ray detectors [50]. MAPbI3 exhibits a photoconductive response to very weak visible light (0.1 μW) under applied reverse voltage (-1.0 to -0.5 V). Perovskite-based photodiodes generates current with linear dependence on incident photon flux with responsivity of more than 600 AW-1, which corresponds to photon-to-current amplification of more than 2000 times. Similar high current-mode responsivity (sensitivity) was also found in X-ray detection. In addition to current-mode detection, perovskite is also useful as a scintillator in X-ray imaging systems because of its high-efficiency photoluminescence. In photo-detecting devices, the functionality of the perovskite diode will be further improved by advances in doping technology applied to perovskite. Besides an extension to optoelectronic applications, perovskite is also a good material for resistive memory (memristor). Millivolt-scale operation was confirmed from the metal-insulator-metal (MIM) structure containing Ag/MAPbI3/Pt, and an on/off ratio as high as 107 was detected [51]. We anticipate that the strategy proposed in this Perspective will benefit the overall development of hybrid halide perovskite technologies extending beyond PV.
