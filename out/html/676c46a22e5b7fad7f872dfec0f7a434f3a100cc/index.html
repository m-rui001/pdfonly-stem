E-mail: guosj@pku.edu.cn The energy conversion efficiency between chemicals and electricity, mainly governed by electrocatalysis, is essential to many renewable energy innovations. Among the biggest challenges in electrocatalysis are the insufficient activity, unsustained durability and unacceptable cost of platinum group metals (PGMs)-based electrocatalysts for the kinetically sluggish oxygen reduction/evolution reaction (ORR/OER)1-4. By optimizing the ligand effect5-7, surface strain8-11 and coordination environment12 of Pt-based nanocrystals, extremely high ORR activities have been realized in acidic media. However, much less success has been achieved on the PGMs (potential alternatives to Pt) for greatly boosting their ORR electrocatalysis in alkaline environment, probably due to the presence of hydroxide and resultant unfavorable oxygen binding strength in such environment. We herein report a highly curved palladium-molybdenum (PdMo) bimetallene with sub-nanometer thickness, which integrates the tunings of atomic utilization, electronic and surface structures for optimized oxygen adsorption strength. The PdMo bimetallene, with an electrochemically active surface area of 138.7 m2 per gram Pd, delivers a mass activity of 16.37 ampere per milligram Pd towards ORR at 0.9 volts versus reversible hydrogen electrode in alkaline electrolyte, 77.9 and 327.4 times higher than those of commercial Pt/C and Pd/C catalysts, respectively. Density functional theory calculations reveal that the high activity of PdMo bimetallene derives from a combination of ligand effect due to the interlayer charge transfer, strain effect due to the curved geometry and quantum size effect due to ultrathin dimension. PdMo bimetallene is also stable for ORR with little activity decay and structural change after 30,000 potential cycles. By further revealing its OER electrocatalysis, PdMo bimetallene can achieve high performance in Zn-air and Li-air batteries. By revealing the striking electrocatalytic property and quantum size effect of PdMo metallene, we envision the great potential of ‘metallene’ materials in energy electrocatalysis. 1 / 19

Suprathin PdMo nanosheet (NS) was synthesized with a one-pot wet-chemical approach by heating a homogenous solution of palladium (II) acetylacetonate (Pd(acac)2), molybdenum (Ⅵ) carbonyl (Mo(CO)6), ascorbic acid (AA), and oleylamine (OAm) at 80 °C for 12 h. As a control, monometallic Pd NS with similar structure was obtained by using the same recipe except changing Mo(CO)6 to CO [see Methods for details]. High-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) image shows the product is dominated with 2D NS with an average lateral size of around 100 nm (Fig. 1a). The high-magnification HAADF-STEM and TEM images reveal as-synthesized NS is highly curved, indicative of its flexible and suprathin character (Fig. 1b, c). Due to its structural analogy with graphene, we denote the PdMo NS as “PdMo bimetallene” herein. Another proof for the suprathin character of PdMo bimetallene is its high sensitivity to the electron beam irradiation under transmission electron microscopy (TEM) operated at high-voltage or high-resolution model (Extended Data Fig. 1a, b). The average thickness of PdMo bimetallene is determined to be 0.88 nm by atomic force microscopy (AFM, Fig. 1d, e), in close agreement with the high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) result (Extended Data Fig. 1c, d). The HRTEM image of PdMo bimetallene shows a lattice spacing of 0.23 nm (inset of Fig. 1c), slightly larger than that of Pd (111) facet. The atomic-resolution HAADF-STEM image and corresponding fast Fourier transform (FFT) pattern suggest the PdMo bimetallene is in a cubic phase with the dominated (111) facet (Fig. 1f and the inset). STEM elemental mapping shows the homogenous distribution of Pd (green) and Mo (red) throughout the metallene (Fig. 1g). The compositional ratio of Pd/Mo is determined to be 87.8/12.2 by inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy, being consistent with the result of TEM energy-dispersive x-ray spectroscopy (TEM-EDX, Extended Data Fig. 1e). The powder x-ray diffraction (PXRD) patterns of various catalysts show their face-centered cubic (fcc) structures (Fig. 1h). In comparison with those of commercial Pd/C, the fcc diffraction peaks of Pd metallene shift negatively, suggesting an increase in the lattice parameter. Due to the addition of larger-atomic-radius Mo into Pd lattice, the diffraction peaks of PdMo bimetallene further shift negatively relative to those of pure Pd metallene, indicating the formation of alloys. Combining the XRD result with the extended x-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS, Extended Data Fig. 1f) analysis, we estimate a tensile strain of around 1.4 % for PdMo bimetallene (see Methods for details)13, resulting from both the curved geometry and the presence of Mo. Classical molecular dynamics simulations of PdMo bimetallene at room temperature further reveal the curved PdMo bimetallene has an average tensile strain of 0.95% (Extended Data Fig. 2a, b), supporting the experimental results. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) of PdMo bimetallene shows the presence of Pd and Mo, while only Pd in Pd metallene (Fig. 1i and Extended Data Fig. 1g). The core level XPS spectra of Pd 3d reveal the majority of Pd in both metallene catalysts is in metallic state, albeit nearly half of that in commercial Pd/C catalyst is in oxidized state (Fig. 1i). This observation can be ascribed to the large proportion of under-coordinated atoms located at the edges or corners on Pd nanoparticles, because these sites are more oxyphilic than those on facets14. Additionally, the Pd 3d XPS peaks of PdMo bimetallene shift to higher binding energy with respect to that of Pd metallene, indicating a downshift of d-band center of Pd in PdMo bimetallene, and in line with previously reported PGM-based alloy system15,16. The core level XPS spectra of Mo 3d shows the Mo in PdMo bimetallene is dominated by metallic state (Extended Data Fig. 1g), different from previous studies 2 / 19

on Mo-involved NPs where the Mo6+ and Mo4+ states dominate17. Given Mo is easy to be oxidized when exposed on the surface, we think that the Mo atoms are mainly buried inside the metallene, as further proved by no galvanic replacement between Mo and Cu (see Methods for details, Extended Data Fig. 1h). The synthesis of anisotropic 2D nanostructures is thermodynamically unfavorable and thus notoriously challenging due to their extremely high surface free energies 18. Although metallic NS with thickness of several nanometers was achieved using different wet-chemical approaches previously19-22, there is no report on sub-nm multimetallic NS in the absence of polymer surfactant (such as PVP, which is detrimental to the catalysis by blocking active sites). Here, we achieve the 0.88 nm free-standing PdMo bimetallene with the distinctively curved geometry by controlling the anisotropic growth kinetics based on several key experimental factors including low-temperature decomposition of metal carbonyl and moderate reducing environment. Otherwise, un-uniform NSs or other products were produced (Extended Data Fig. 3a-h). Additionally, ultra-large metallene could be made when high concentration of metal precursors was used, indicating the feasibility of controlling the lateral size (Extended Data Fig. 3i, j). Our approach not only enables the scalable synthesis of PdMo bimetallene, but also can be generalized to the synthesis of PdW bimetallene by simply changing Mo(CO)6 to W(CO)6 (Extended Data Fig. 3k-o). To shed light on the growth mechanism of PdMo bimetallene, we characterized the morphologies, compositions and optical adsorption properties of reaction intermediates collected at different reaction times by TEM, TEM-EDX and UV-Vis spectrophotometer, respectively (Extended Data Fig. 4). The time-dependent trend on these properties of PdMo bimetallene suggests the synthetic process follows the initial formation of small pieces of pure Pd NS, the subsequent seed-mediated growth along the lateral direction and the inter-diffusion of Mo atoms into the metallene. For the electrocatalytic evaluations, we deposited free-standing 2D nanocrystals onto carbon black, and benchmarked to commercial Pt/C and Pd/C catalysts. Cyclic voltammograms (CVs) of various catalysts show the underpotentially deposited hydrogen (Hupd) peaks of metallene catalysts are larger than those of Pt/C and Pd/C catalysts (Extended Data Fig. 5a-d), indicating higher PGMs utilization of the metallenes due to their suprathin character. Additionally, the Hupd peak of PdMo bimetallene/C obviously shifts negatively relative to that of Pd metallene/C, suggesting a suppression of hydrogen adsorption/desorption on PdMo bimetallene. This phenomenon testifies the weakened adsorptive strength of Pd induced by Mo alloying via the modification of electronic structure, in consistence with the XPS analysis (Fig. 1i). Interestingly, previous reports on Pt-skin/Pt3Ni(111) showed similar suppression of Hupd due to the segregation of M in the subsurface layer, which is the dominant reason for the enhanced ORR activity23,24. The positive shift of hydroxide adsorption and oxide reduction peaks of PdMo bimetallene relative to those of Pd metallene confirms the weakened Pd-O binding by Mo (Extended Data Fig. 5a, b). We calculated the electrochemically active surface areas (ECSAs) of various catalysts by means of Hupd, CO stripping, underpotential deposition copper (Cuupd) and Pd oxide reduction (see Methods for details and Extended Data Fig. 5e-n), while the ECSAs determined from Cuupd method were adopted for further analysis25. Notably, Pd metallene and PdMo bimetallene catalysts have an ECSAs 3 / 19

Page 6

of 149.1 m2 g−1Pd and 138.7 m2 g−1Pd, respectively, due to their suprathin character. The ORR polarization curves in Fig. 2a show the PdMo bimetallene/C has a half-wave potential (E1/2) of 0.95 V in 0.1 M KOH, higher than those of commercial Pt/C (0.85 V) and Pd/C (0.84 V). To further quantitate the intrinsic ORR activity, we obtained the kinetic current of each catalyst using the Koutecky-Levich equation (see Methods for details). The PdMo bimetallene/C delivers a mass activity of 16.37 A mg−1PGMs at the generally-chosen 0.9 V versus RHE, 77.9 and 327.4 times higher than those of commercial Pt/C and Pd/C catalysts, respectively (Fig. 2b). Due to the extremely fast kinetics of PdMo bimetallene/C, 0.9 V is too approaching the diffusion-limiting region to give a reliable kinetic current density. Therefore, we further compared the activity at a potential of 0.95 V where the limitation of O2 diffusion can be eliminated26. As listed in Table 1, the mass activity of PdMo bimetallene/C is still 16.9 and 107.5 times greater than that of Pt/C and Pd/C, respectively. The number of electrons transferred for PdMo bimetallene/C is determined to be 3.95 from the Levich plot (Extended Data Fig. 5o, p), suggesting a complete 4-electron pathway from O2 to OH−. To the best of our knowledge, the ORR activity of PdMo bimetallene/C reported here in alkaline solution has not been achieved by any reported nanomaterials under comparable conditions, including various efficient Pt-based nanostructures and Pd NS synthesized with other approaches (Extended Data Fig. 6 and Extended Data Table 1). In addition, PdMo bimetallene/C also shows higher ORR activity than benchmark Pt/C in acid media (Extended Data Fig. 7). We further assessed their electrochemical durability by conducting accelerated stability tests (ASTs) between 0.6 and 1.0 V versus RHE at a scan rate of 50 mV s−1 in O2-saturated 0.1 M KOH. Fig. 2c compares the ECSAs before and after 15,000 and 30,000 potential cycles, and shows the ECSAs can be preserved for both metallene catalysts, in contrast to those of benchmark Pt/C and Pd/C catalysts. The loss of ECSAs for the latter catalysts can be mainly ascribed to the increase in particle size (Extended Data Fig. 8a-h), resulting from a series of processes including nanoparticulate mobility, aggregation and Ostwald ripening27. Fig. 2d shows the ORR polarization curves of PdMo bimetallene/C almost overlap with each other before and after ASTs. As shown in Fig. 2e, the PdMo bimetallene/C can preserve higher initial mass activity than the control catalysts after 30,000 cycles. Furthermore, the PdMo bimetallene can maintain the initial morphology and structure even after being subjected to 30,000 cycles (Extended Data Fig. 8i-l). All of them indicate the enhanced durability of PdMo bimetallene for ORR, which can be ascribed to a combined result of: (1) large lateral size of 2D nanostructure; (2) multiple anchoring point between the bimetallene and carbon support; (3) minimal proportion of under-coordinated surface atoms on the edges or vertices28; (4) less oxophilicity of surface Pd due to its electronic interaction with Mo (Fig. 1i and Extended Data Fig. 5a, b). DFT calculations were performed to elucidate the origin of enhanced ORR activities. We constructed a four-atomic-layer model for PdMo bimetallene and calculated oxygen adsorption energy (EO), which is widely used as a descriptor for ORR activity. There exists an optimal value of E O, for which the ORR activity reaches the maximum29,30. In this model, the atomic composition of Mo in layer 2 and 3 is 25%, and there is no Mo in layer 1 and 4, yielding a total Mo content of 12.5%, consistent with the experiment. Extensive DFT calculations were performed to determine the most stable 4 / 19

Page 7

atomic configurations in layer 2 and 3, and the results are displayed in Fig. 3a. We have calculated ∆EO of PdMo bimetallene as a function of bi-axial strain, and results are summarized in Fig. 3b. We find that ∆EO reaches the optimal value at ~1% tensile strain, suggesting that the tensile strain is beneficial to ORR activity, consistent with the experimental findings revealed by XRD and EXAFS. In addition, there is substantial charge transfer from Mo substrate to Pd on the top and bottom surfaces (0.89 e per Mo atom). As shown in Fig. 3c, the transferred electrons fill the d-band (the antibonding states) of the surface Pd atoms, shifting its center towards the negative energy by 0.26 eV, relative to the pure four-layer Pd sheet (Pd 4L). The downshift of the d-band center relieves the over-binding of oxygen on the surface, and moves ∆EO towards the optimal value. There is also a downshift of the d-band center (~0.09 eV) of Pd 4L relative to bulk Pd, suggesting that the quantum size effect, albeit much smaller, is also beneficial to ORR activity. Therefore, the ligand, strain and quantum size effects are all responsible for the enhanced ORR activity on PdMo bimetallene, with the ligand effect playing the most important role, which is further supported by the fact that PdMo bimetallene shows the optimal ∆EO among the various Pd-based alloys (Extended Data Fig. 2c). The PdMo bimetallene/C also shows good OER activity by exhibiting 30 mV smaller overpotential at a current density of 10 mA cm−2 than that of benchmark IrO2 catalyst, and also considerably enhanced OER durability (Extended Data Fig. 9a, b). We then take advantage of its bifunctional oxygen electrocatalytic performance, and fabricate an aqueous Zn-air battery using PdMo bimetallene/C as the cathode (Extended Data Fig. 9c-m). In addition to the high open-circuit voltage of 1.483 V and maximum power density of 154.2 mV cm−2, the PdMo bimetallene-based battery delivers a specific capacity and a gravimetric energy density of 798 mAh g−1Zn and 1043 Wh kg−1Zn, respectively, much higher than those of the battery using the benchmarked IrO2+Pt/C as the catalyst (499 mAh g−1Zn and 543 Wh kg−1Zn). We further testified the rate capability at different current densities (Extended Data Fig. 9h), durability at different discharge depths (Extended Data Fig. 9i&j) and high current densities (50 and 75 mA cm−2, Extended Data Fig. 9l-n) of PdMo bimetallene as a bifunctional air cathode electrocatalyst for Zn-air battery, respectively, suggesting its good potential as air cathode in the practical application. The PdMo bimetallene/C also shows enhanced performance in non-aqueous Li-air batteries (Extended Data Fig. 10). METHODS Synthesis of PdMo bimetallene and Pd metallene. In a typical synthesis of PdMo bimetallene, 10 mg of Pd(acac)2, 30 mg of ascorbic acid (AA), 4 mg of Mo(CO)6 and 5 mL of oleylamine (OAm) were mixed in a 20 mL glass vial, and subsequently ultrasonicated for at least 1 h to yield the homogeneous solution. The vial was then sealed and transferred into an oil bath at a temperature of 80 °C for 12 h. After being cooled down to room temperature, the colloidal product was collected by centrifugation, and washed several times with a mixture of ethanol/cyclohexane to remove the excess OAm. The final product was dispersed in cyclohexane for further use. For the synthesis of Pd metallene, 10 mg of Pd(acac)2 and 30 mg of AA were first dissolved in 5 mL of OAm under sonication. Then, the resulting homogeneous solution was transferred into a Teflon high-pressure vessel and saturated with 2 atm CO gas. The vessel was heated from room temperature to 80 °C at a rate of 5 °C min−1 and kept at 80 °C for 12 h. The product was collected by centrifugation and washed thoroughly with ethanol/cyclohexane before being stored in cyclohexane for further use. Synthesis of PtPb nanoplates, hierarchical PtNi nanowires, ultrathin PtNi nanowires and PVP-capped Pd nanosheets. The PtPb nanoplates, hierarchical Pt3Ni nanowires, ultrathin PtNi NWs and PVP-capped Pd nanosheets were synthesized by using previously reported methods31-34. Characterization. Powder X-ray diffractometer (XRD) patterns were collected on a Shimadzu XRD-6000 X-ray diffractometer using a Cu K radiation (λ＝0.15406 nm). The operation voltage and current were 40 kV and 30 mA, respectively. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was conducted on an FEI Tecnai T20 transmission electron microscope at an acceleration voltage of 120 kV. High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) and TEM energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (TEM-EDS) were conducted on a FEI Tecnai F20 transmission electron microscope at an acceleration voltage of 200 kV. High-angle annular dark field scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM) was conducted on a JEOL-2100F with a probe aberration corrector at the acceleration voltage of 200 kV. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) was performed in tapping mode with a Multimode Nanoscope IIIa SPA (Veeco Instruments, Bruker) at ambient condition. Ultrasharp cantilevers with a diamond-like carbon tip (NSG01, NT-MDT, Russia) were used. The image was flattened using the NanoScope Analysis software (version 1.40). The composition of each catalyst was measured by the inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (710-ES, Varian, ICP-AES). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) tests were carried out with Kratos AXIS Supra/Ultra spectrometer. The UV-Vis-near IR absorption spectra of PdMo bimetallene (dispersed in cyclohexane) were taken on a UH4150 spectrophotometer (HITACHI). XAFS experiment and data processing. XAFS measurements at Pd K-edge in both transmission (for Pd foil) and fluorescence (for samples) mode were performed at the BL14W135 in Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF). The electron beam energy was 3.5 GeV, and the stored current was 260 mA (top-up). A 38-pole wiggler with the maximum magnetic field of 1.2 T inserted in the straight section of the storage ring was used. XAFS data were collected using a fixed-exit double-crystal Si(311) monochromator. A Lytle detector was used to collect the fluorescence signal, and the energy was calibrated using the Pd foil. The photon flux at the sample position was 1.8×1011 photons per second. The raw data analysis was performed using IFEFFIT software package according to the standard data analysis procedures36. The spectra were calibrated, averaged, pre-edge background subtracted, and post-edge normalized using Athena program in IFEFFIT software package. The Fourier transformation of the k2-weighted EXAFS oscillations, k2·χ(k), from k space to R space was performed over a range of 2.7–11.4 Å-1 to obtain a radial distribution function. And the data fitting was done by Artemis program in IFEFFIT. Galvanic substitution experiment. To verify whether the Mo atoms located at the surface of PdMo bimetallene, we conducted the galvanic substitution by mixing 5 mg of as-synthesized PdMo bimetallene (Mo3+ + 3e ↔ Mo0, E0 = −0.2 V; Pd2+ + 2e ↔ Pd0, E0 = +0.95 V) and 2 mg of CuCl2 (Cu2+ + 2e ↔ Cu0, E0 = +0.34 V) in 10 mL of ethanol for 5 h. Both ICP and XPS results (Extended Data Fig. 1h) of final products (denoted as PdMo-Cu) suggest no galvanic replacements happened between Mo and Cu, further confirming that Mo located at the inner rather than the surface of bimetallene. Where 𝒂𝒃 is the lattice parameter of bulk Pd (i.e., 0.3890, JCPDS No. 46-1043.), and 𝒂𝒏 is the lattice parameter of corresponding Pd nanocrystal. As determined from the XRD data, the lattice parameters for Pd/C, Pd metallene and PdMo metallene are 0.3889, 0.3928 and 0.3941 respectively. Electrochemical measurements. Before the electrochemical tests, we prepared the catalyst by depositing as-synthesized nanocrystals onto commercial carbon black. Taking the preparation of carbon supported PdMo bimetallene catalyst as an example, 10 mg of PdMo metallene dispersed in 10 mL of cyclohexane, and 40 mg of Ketjen carbon in 40 mL of cyclohexane were mixed, and subsequently sonicated for 60 min. After stirring for another 12 h, the final product was collected by centrifugation, followed by washing for three times with ethanol and drying at 80 °C overnight under ambient conditions. The obtained catalyst powder was further treated in 0.5 M acetic acid solution at 60 °C under N2 atmosphere for 2 h to remove excess surfactant before use. To prepare the catalysts ink for the electrochemical tests, 1 mg of the above dried catalysts powder was dispersed into 1 mL of the solvent containing 750 μL of water, 245 μL of isopropanol and 5 μL of 5 wt.% Nafion solution, and sonicated for 30 min in an ice-water bath. The yielded homogeneous catalyst ink was used to prepare the working electrode. The commercial Johnson-Matthey Pt/C catalyst (20 wt.%, 3-nm Pt nanoparticles supported on carbon black) and commercial Aldrich Pd/C catalyst (10 wt.%, 8-nm Pd nanoparticles supported on activated charcoal) were used as the benchmark catalysts. Electrochemical tests were performed with a three-electrode system on a CHI660E electrochemical analyzer (CHI Instruments) with a catalyst-coated glassy carbon electrode (GC, 5 mm in diameter) mounted on a rotator (Pine Ins.) as the working electrode and a Pt wire as the counter electrode. Prior to the catalysts coating, the GC was polished with alumina polishing slurries and cleaned with ultrapure water. A leak-free saturated calomel electrode (SCE) was used as the reference electrode in acidic and alkaline electrolytes, respectively, while all the potentials in this study were reported on a reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) scale. Before each electrochemical measurement, a HER/HOR polarization curve of a Pt electrode was recorded to accurately calibrate to the RHE. The electrolytes were 0.1 M HClO4 and 0.1 M KOH. 7.5 μL of the prepared catalysts ink was deposited on the GC electrode, yielding a metal loading of around 7.5 μgprecious metals cm−2geo. To obtain a well-defined diffusion-limited current plateau, the metal loading for commercial Pd/C was controlled to be 15 μgPd cm−2geo. Cyclic voltammograms (CVs) were recorded at a scanning rate of 50 mV s−1 after the electrolyte was purged with nitrogen for at least 30 min. Prior to recording, several fast CVs (500 mV s−1) were used to clean and stabilize the catalyst surface until steady state CV could be obtained. The polarization curves of ORR were recorded in O2-saturated electrolyte with a scan rate of 20 mV s−1 and a rotating rate of 1600 rpm. The recorded ORR polarization curves were iR corrected before calculating the kinetic current (Ik) via the Koutechy-Levich equation: Ik = (Ilim × I) / (Ilim − I), where Ilim is the limiting current, and I is the measured current at a given potential. For each catalyst, the specific and mass activities were obtained by normalizing the kinetic current to the corresponding ECSA (determined from Cu stripping) and the precious metal loading, respectively. The accelerated durability tests (ADTs) were conducted by cycling between 0.6 to 1.0 V vs. RHE at 50 mV s−1 for 15,000 and 30,000 cycles. The electrochemically active surface areas (ECSAs) of Pd-based catalysts were determined by using the charges associated with the underpotentially deposited H (Hupd) and Cu (Cuupd), the stripping of CO, and the Pd oxide reduction. The desorption peaks of Hupd from the recorded CVs were used to calculate the surface areas of Pt or Pd catalysts37, assuming a charge density of 210 μC cm−2 (a value for polyhedron) for one monolayer of hydrogen coverage on commercial Pt/C and Pd/C, and a charge density of 240 μC cm−2 (a value for (111) surface) for one monolayer of hydrogen coverage on PdMo NS/C and Pd NS/C. For the Cu stripping experiments, a N 2-saturated solution containing 2 mM CuSO4 and 0.05 M H2SO4 was used as the supporting electrolyte. The potential was first held at 0.3 V versus RHE for 100 s to form a Cuupd monolayer38, and then conducted CV from 0.3 V to 1.0 V at a scan rate of 20 mV s−1. The ECSAs were calculated by subtracting the background CVs that were collected in N2-saturated 0.05 M H2SO4 at a scan rate of 20 mV s−1, assuming a charge density of 470 μC cm−2. For the CO stripping experiments, CO gas (99.99%) was first bubbled into 0.1 M HClO4 while holding the working electrode potential at 0.1 V versus RHE for 10 min. After purging the above electrolyte with N2 for at least 30 min, two CVs were recorded at a scan rate of 20 mV s−1. The ECSAs were calculated from the charge of CO stripping39 (the first CV) by subtracting the background charge (the second CV), assuming a charge density of 420 μC cm−2. For Pd oxide reduction experiments, the CVs of various Pd-based catalysts were recorded from 0.1 to 1.25 V vs. RHE at a scan rate of 50 mV/s. The oxide reduction peak at around 0.8 V was used to calculate the surface area40, assuming a 14 / 19

charge density of 424 μC cm−2Pd. Each electrochemical measurement was conducted for at least three times, based on which the corresponding standard deviations were obtained. DFT models and calculations. We constructed a four-atomic-layer model for PdMo bimetallene and calculated oxygen adsorption energy (EO) on its surface. EO is defined as EO = E[surf + O] − E[surf] − E[O2]/2, where E[surf + O] and E[surf] are the total energies of the surface with and without the oxygen adsorbate, respectively. E[O2] is the total energy of an oxygen molecule. There exists an optimal value of EO, for which the ORR activity reaches the maximum. Here for convenience, we shifted the optimal EO value to 0 eV and used ∆EO to represent the difference of a given EO value relative to this optimal reference. All atoms were allowed to relax in the PdMo model. A six-layer slab model was used to simulate the surfaces of Pt (111) and Pd (111). The atoms in the top three layers were fully relaxed while in the last three layers were fixed to simulate the bulk. To avoid interactions between periodic images, a vacuum layer of 15 Å is added to the adjacent slabs in both models. Our DFT calculations were carried out using Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP)41. The Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof functional within the generalized gradient approximation42 and the projector augmented wave pseudopotential43 were used in the calculations. An energy cutoff of 400 eV was used for the plane-wave basis set. The Brillouin zone was sampled on the basis of the Monkhorst-Pack scheme44 with a 3×3×1 k-point mesh. The force convergence criterion for atomic relaxation was 0.02 eV/Å. Extensive DFT calculations were performed to determine the most stable atomic configurations in layer 2 and 3 of the PdMo model, and we find that on layer of 2 and 3, Mo atoms are uniformly distributed in the plane, but they tend to avoid each other across the planes, resulting in an average nearest neighbor (NN) Mo-Mo distance of 4.58 Å. We also note that the ORR activity on other bimetallenes, such as PdW, PdCu, and PdNi in the same structure, is lower than that of PdMo bimetallene, owing to either over-shift or under-shift of the d-band center induced by excessive or insufficient charge transfer from the substrate to the surface Pd (Extended Data Fig. 2c). The classical molecular dynamics (MD) calculations were performed at 300 K using the LAMMPS package45 with the NVT ensemble and EAM interatomic potential developed by Zhou et al.46 The number of simulated steps was more than 400,000 with a time-step of 1 fs. A 50 × 50 nm PdMo nanosheet (~150,000 atoms) was included in the MD simulations. All NN sites in the distance between 2.25 and 3.25 Å were included in the evaluation of the average strain. For the NN sites i and j, the strain was defined as ɛij = dij/d0 - 1, where dij is the interatomic distance between i and j and d0 is the equilibrium NN distance in bulk Pd. Zn-air battery test. The performance of Zn-air battery was evaluated in a home-made electrochemical cell on battery test system (LANHE CT2001A). The Zn-air battery is composed of PdMo bimetallene/C coated carbon paper as the air cathode, Zn plate (1 mm in thickness) as the anode, and 6 M KOH + 0.2 M Zn(Ac)2 aqueous solution as the electrolyte. For the comparison, the carbon paper coated by commercial catalysts (20 wt% Pt/C:IrO2 = 1:1, mass ratio) was also used as the cathode. The air electrode was prepared by spreading the as-prepared catalysts ink onto carbon paper with a loading amount of 1 mg cm−2geo and dried at 110 °C in vacuum overnight. A commercial blue light-emitting diode (LED, 2.5~3 V) was used as an electronic load. The specific capacity was obtained by normalizing mAh to the mass of consumed Zn during the long-term discharge process, and the energy density equals to the obtained specific capacity times average discharge voltage. Notably, all the measurements of Zn−air batteries were conducted in air rather than pure oxygen stationary atmosphere. The depth of discharge (DOD) value can be calculated with the following equation: DOD value = (the DOD discharge time)/(full discharge time)100%. For the DOD test, the effective geometric surface area of the oxygen cathode and Zn anode is 1.4 cm2 and 1 cm2, respectively, which corresponds to a current density of 10 mA/cm2 at the cathode and a current density of 14 mA/cm2 at the anode. To better directly and unambiguously reflect the super-stability of the PdMo bimetallene cathode as the bifunctional oxygen electrocatalyst in rechargeable Zn-air batteries, during the cycling under the deep-DOD condition, we used a Zn wire as the reference electrode to minimize the influence from zinc anode polarization. Li-air battery test. The electrochemical tests of Li-air batteries were carried out using 2032 coin-type cells with holes on the cathode side. The cells were composed of a lithium metal tablet anode, one slice of glass microfiber separator (Whatman), 0.2 mL electrolyte, and a prepared cathode (11 mm diameter). The batteries were assembled in the argon-filled glove box, and then installed into a home-made chamber. Before the measurements, the chambers were flushed with pure oxygen. Each measurement was begun after a 12 h open circuit potential step to ensure equilibrium in the cell. The electrochemical measurements were carried out using a LAND cycler 15 / 19

(CT2001A). The preparation of the cathodes was carried out as follows: A mixture containing 80 wt% of active material and 20 wt.% of PVDF as binder was well mixed by grinding, and then pressed onto carbon paper, which served as a current collector. Due to the highly hydrophobic property of carbon paper, the suspension could only stay on the surface of carbon paper, and thus would not block the oxygen channels in carbon paper. The cathodes were then dried at 100°C under vacuum for 24 h before use. The loading density of the active material was controlled to be approximately 0.5 mg cm−2. 1 M LiTFSI in TEGDME was employed as the electrolyte, and prepared in an argon-filled glove box with water and oxygen contents below 0.8 ppm.
