1. Introduction Two-dimensional (2D) materials possess high specific surface areas and sometimes exhibit electronic properties that differ from their bulk counterparts.[1-3] Recently, we discovered a new family of 2D transition metal carbides and carbonitrides, which we labeled MXenes.[4] MXenes have the general formula of Mn+1 Xn Tx , where M stands for an early transition metal (e.g. Ti, V, Cr, and Mo), X is either carbon or nitrogen, n = 1-3, and Tx represents surface functional groups such as -O, -OH and/or -F.[5] MXenes are synthesized by selective etching of the “A” layers (mostly from groups 13 and 14) from layered, ternary carbides, MAX phases, using hydrofluoric acid (HF), ammonium bifluoride (NH4 HF2 ), or a solution of lithium fluoride (LIF) and hydrochloric acid (HCl).[6-8] MXenes exhibit good electrical conductivity, hydrophilicity, and can host many different cations between their layers.[4, 9] Due to these characteristics, they have been explored theoretically and experimentally for a number of applications, with particular attention given to energy storage applications, including electrodes for Li-, Na-, and K-ion batteries,[10-12] LiS batteries,[4] Li- ion and aqueous supercapacitors.[8, 9, 13, 14] They can also provide stability and durability for proton exchange membranes for fuel cells. [15] Other potential applications include water purification,[16, 17] electrochemical actuators,[18] photocatalysis,[19] transparent conductive electrodes and sensors.[7, 20] Typically the resistivity of Ti3 C2 Tx films decrease with decreasing temperature from 300 K down to 100 K, below which the resistance increases slightly. [7] At temperature < 50 K, the magnetoresistance is negative and the logarithmic dependence of the resistivity on temperature suggest a weak 2D localization effect. To date more than a dozen different MXene compositions have been reported, all of which were produced by etching the Al- layers from Al-containing MAX phases. [4, 5, 11, 21, 22] Quite 2

Page 3

recently, however, we discovered a new hexagonal ternary nanolaminated carbide, Mo2 Ga2 C, where instead of one “A” layer such, as Mo 2 GaC,[23] two “A” layers are found between the Mo2 C layers.[24] In a subsequent paper,[25] we showed the possibility of selectively etching the Ga layers – using HF – from epitaxial Mo2 Ga2 C thin films, producing the MXene Mo2 CTx . The Mo2 Ga2 C thin films were not completely converted to Mo 2 CTx , therefore we were not able to characterize their properties. Using Boltzmann theory and first-principles electronic structure calculations, Mo2 CTx terminated with F is predicted to be semiconductor with a band gap of 0.25 eV and a high power factor.[26] Thus it can be considered as a promising thermoelectric material. Also quite recently, Xu et al. used chemical vapor deposition to produce large-area, highquality, Mo2 C crystals, as thin as 3.4 nm.[27] In a 3.4- nm-thick crystals, the resistivity was shown to decrease from 300 K to about 50 K; below 50 K the resistivity increased logarithmically suggesting – like in the Ti3 C2 Tx films [7] - a possible weak 2D localization effect. In films thicker than 5 nm, a superconducting transition was observed below ≈ 4 K. At 10 K, the electron mobilities of these films were reported to be ≈ 10 cm2 /Vs. Herein we report on the large-scale synthesis and delamination into single Mo2 CTx flakes by the selective etching of Ga from Mo2 Ga2 C powders using HF or a solution of LiF and HCl. Once etched and delaminated, to produce Mo2 CTx colloidal solutions, henceforth referred to as d-Mo2 CTx , free-standing ‘paper’ was produced by filtering the d-Mo2 CTx . The resistivity, ρ, of the films increased upon cooling from 300 to 10 K, with a positive magnetoresistance observed at 10 K, suggesting semiconductor- like behavior. When tested in aqueous supercapacitors, the paper electrodes showed volumetric capacitances of up to 700 F/cm3 in 1 M sulfuric acid (H2 SO 4 ) and retained these capacitance values even after 10,000 cycles. An electrode of Mo2 CTx delaminated flakes and carbon nanotubes (CNTs) ‘paper’ showed promising performance as electrode for Li-ion batteries.

2. Results and Discussion 2.1. Material Synthesis, Structural and Chemical Characterization The synthesis and delamination of Mo2 CTx flakes are schematically illustrated in Figure 1a. Both methods used for producing d-Mo2 CTx flakes involve etching the Ga layers from Mo2 Ga2 C using acidic solutions containing fluoride ions. The synthesis details of Mo2 Ga2 C, its MXene, ‘paper’, and composites can be found in the experimental section below. In the first method, Mo2 Ga2 C powders were etched using a solution, containing 3 M lithium fluoride (LiF) and 12 M hydrochloric acid (HCl), producing Mo2 CTx , multilayered flakes with Li between the layers. This material will henceforth be referred to as Mo2 CTx -Li (more synthesis details can be found in the experimental section). To delaminate Mo2 CTx -Li, the etched powder was added to deionized (DI) water and hand shaken for 5 min. The end result, after centrifuging for 1 h at 5000 rpm, was a colloidal suspension of delaminated flakes with a concentration of ≈ 0.1 mg/ml. Filtration of the latter produced films that will henceforth be referred to as d-Mo2 CTx -Li. A comparison of the XRD patterns of Mo 2 Ga2 C (black pattern in Figure 1c and 1d) and Mo2 CTx -Li etched for 16 d at 35°C (green patterns in Figures 1c and 1d) clearly shows that all peaks belonging to Mo 2 Ga2 C are totally replaced by (000l) peaks belonging to Mo2 CTx -Li. As typical of all MAX to MXene transformations, the (0001) peaks broaden and shift to lower angles as a result of an increase in the c lattice parameter (c-LP) from 18.1 Å to 23.1 Å. In the second method, 14 M aqueous hydrofluoric acid (HF) was used as the etchant. In this case, the Mo2 Ga2 C powders were etched for 6.6 d at 55°C. These powders will henceforth be referred to as Mo2 CTx . To delaminate the etched powders, the Mo2 CTx powders were first intercalated with tetrabutylammonium hydroxide (TBAOH) in water following the procedure, reported by Naguib et al.[28]. The intercalated Mo2 CTx was delaminated in water either by hand shaking or sonication, yielding a suspension with a concentration of ≈ 4 mg/ml. The resulting material will henceforth simply be referred to as d-Mo2 CTx . The suspension was 4

then vacuum filtered through a nanoporous polypropylene membrane to obtain free-standing d-Mo2 CTx ‘paper’. As can be seen from digital images shown in Figure 1b, the diluted delaminated suspension is brown-pink in color, whereas the films are metallic green. SEM images showing the morphologies of Mo2 Ga2 C, Mo2 CTx and Mo2 CTx -Li powders are shown in Figure S1a, 1b and 1c-d, respectively. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of Mo2 Ga2 C powders after etching with HF (red patterns in Figures 1c and 1d) resulted in the appearance of two (0002) MXene peaks in addition to a peak corresponding to the unreacted MAX phase. The first MXene peak corresponds to a cLP of 21.2 Å, while the second, quite weak peak, and corresponds to a c-LP of 26.9 Å. The difference in the interlayer spacing (∆d = ∆c/2) between them is approximately equal to the van der Waals radius of a monolayer of water, 2.8 Å. [29] This suggests that some of the Mo2 CTx multilayers are intercalated with a monolayer of water, while the other was intercalated by two water layers. Water intercalation is common and has been observed in other MXenes such as Nb2 CTx , Nb4 C3 Tx and V2 CTx .[11, 21] Upon intercalation with TBAOH, two (0002) peaks appeared corresponding to c-LPs of 28.5 and 58.1 Å, respectively (blue patterns in Figures 1c and 1d). The first, small peak arises from TBA intercalated Mo2 CTx .[30] The other (0002) peak with a c-LP of 58.1 Å has a ∆d of 18.5 Å (using the d-spacing of the high angle 0002 peak of Mo2 CTx as a base line), which corresponds to the intercalation of TBA cations (the TBA cation size ranges between 8.5 to 10.5 Å) and from two to three water layers.[30] Similar behavior was reported for the intercalation of graphene oxide with TBAOH. [29] The c-LP of d-Mo2 CTx 'paper' at ~ 37.7 Å (purple patterns in Figures 1c and 1d) is significantly lower than the 58.1 Å of the TBA intercalated powders. Said otherwise, ∆d compared to Mo2 CTx is ~10.2 Å, which is quite close to the size of a single TBA cation. This suggests that while water may be trapped between the multilayers, the delamination, filtration and drying steps removes the water molecules leaving only TBA cations behind. A 5

The flake has a lateral diameter of ~ 2 µm and shows few signs of macroscopic defects, such as pores. Flakes produced after etching for 16 d (Figure S2), on the other hand, are quite defective with a large number of pores clearly visible. Not surprisingly, the Mo2 CTx -Li yields, after etching for 6 d, are significantly lower than those etched for 16 d, as evidenced by the presence of Mo2 Ga2 C peaks in the XRD patterns of the former (Figure S3). Thus, shorter etching times result in less defective flakes with low yields; while longer etching times result in higher yields of more defective flakes. Along the same lines, the d-Mo2 CTx flakes (Figure 2b and 2e) are more defective than their d-Mo2 CTx -Li counterparts (Figure 2a and Figure 2d). Small pin-holes can be observed in the higher magnification TEM image (Figure 2e), which might originate during the TBAOH intercalation step, or more likely, during the HF etching step. It is reasonable to assume that etching with HF solution is more aggressive than using LiF and HCl mixture. Similar behavior was reported for delaminated V2 CTx flakes after TBAOH treatment. [28] Not surprisingly, smaller sized defective flakes, with larger diameter holes, were obtained after sonication for 1 h (Figure 2c and 2f). However, the percentage of single flakes increased from 25 to 50 % after sonication (Figure S4). These results are important because they outline several strategies that can be used to manipulate flake morphologies, from relatively defect- free to highly defective and 6

nanoporous. The former could be used for electronic and optical applications where defectfree flakes with large lateral dimensions are needed. In contrast, the latter could be used for applications where higher accessibility and edge effects may be useful, such as in energy storage or catalysis. Since our interest here is energy storage, in the remainder of this paper, we worked exclusively with the HF etched powders that were sonicated for 1 h. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements performed on the d-Mo2 CTx ‘paper’ were used to identify and quantify the various chemical and termination species. High-resolution XPS spectra peak fittings for Mo 3d, C 1s, O 1s and F 1s are shown in Figure 3a, 3b, 3c, and 3d, respectively. The peak fitting results for the various species and the elemental compositions extracted from the high resolution spectra are tabulated in Tables S1 and S2. The high-resolution XPS spectrum of the Ga 2p region confirm the absence of Ga related peaks after etching (Figure S5). The high-resolution spectrum of the Mo 3d region (Figure 3a) were fitted by components corresponding to the following 3 species: Mo-C, Mo+5 , and Mo+6 .[31, 32] The latter two species – whose peaks are quite small - most probably arise from surface oxidation of the ‘paper’ and represent ~ 12% of the Mo 3d region. The binding energy (BE) of the Mo 3d5/2 for the Mo-C species is at 229.2 eV, which is 1.1 eV higher than the 228.1 eV BE of the Mo-C species reported for Mo2 Ga2 C.[31] This shift to higher BEs for the MXenes compared to their parent MAX phases is typical and is due to the replacement of the “A” element by more electronegative surface terminations species such as O, OH, and/or F.[33] Figure 3b shows the high-resolution spectrum in the C 1s region that were fitted by components corresponding to Mo-C, C-C, CHx , C-O, and COO species. The presence of all but the Mo-C species, result from the TBAOH intercalations, and/or the exposure of this highsurface area material to the ambient during filtration and storage. The fraction of F-terminations here is low compared to other MXenes etched by HF or LiF and HCl,[33] which is not too surprising since it has been shown that treating Ti3 CNTx , Ta4 C3 Tx and V2 CTx with TBAOH resulted in a significant reduction in their F content.[8, 28] Similar reductions in the fraction of F-terminations were reported after treating Ti3 C2 Tx with hydroxide solutions such as KOH, due to the thermodynamic instability of Ti-F bonds in high pH solutions.[35] It is thus reasonable to assume that the same is occurring here. Note that we were not able to identify or quantify the nitrogen associated with the TBAOH, since the N 1s region coincides with the Mo 3p region.

Electrochemical Energy Storage Properties 2.3.1. Electrochemical Performance in Supercapacitors Electrodes of d-Mo2 CTx ‘paper’, 2 μm thick, were tested in a three-electrode configuration using Ag/AgCl as a reference electrode and an overcapacitive activated carbon as a counter electrode in a 1 M H2 SO 4 electrolyte. Figure 5a shows the cyclic voltammetry (CV) profiles collected at 20 mV/s over two different voltage windows. For the most part, testing was carried out between -0.30 V and 0.30 V vs. Ag/AgCl. The non-rectangular shape of the CV loops is likely related to the pseudocapacitive charge storage mechanism, as demonstrated for MoO3-x in neutral salt electrolytes and for Ti3 C2 Tx in 1 M H2 SO 4 .[41, 42] The rate performance of 2 µm thick ‘paper’ is summarized in Figure 5b. At 2 mV/s, the gravimetric capacitance is 196 F/g, while at 100 mV/s it drops to 120 F/g (left hand y-axis in Figure 5b). The respective volumetric capacitances (right-axis, Figure 5b) are 700 F/cm-3 and 430 F/cm-3 . Figure 5c shows no degradation in performance even after 10,000 galvanostatic 10

charge/discharge cycles performed at a current density of 10 A/g with almost perfectly linear charge/discharge profiles maintained after 10,000 cycles (see inset in Figure 5c). Electrochemical Performance of d-Mo2 CTx –CNT as Anode in Li-ion Batteries The CV plots of 3 µm thick d-Mo2 CTx –CNT ‘paper’ electrodes (Figure 6a) showed a lithiation peak around 0.1 V vs. Li/Li+ during the first cycle. This peak shifts to 0.13 V and becomes more intense during the 2nd cycle. With further cycling, however, the intensity of the peak decreases while its position fluctuates. After 20 cycles, the peak intensity stabilizes at ≈ 0.15 V (Figure 6a). Another weak lithiation peak emerges after the 1 st cycle around 1.34 V and its intensity increases slightly with cycling. Its position also changes with cycling till it reaches ~ 1.4 V after 30 cycles. During delithiation, a similar trend of decreasing intensity and fluctuations of position was observed for the peak that initially was located at 1.5 V and ended at ~ 1.45 V, after 30 cycles. Another delithiation peak – observed around 2.6 V during the first cycle – intensifies with cycling and its position shifts to 2.64 V after 30 cycles. In other Mo-based, systems such behavior (peaks evolutions and devolution in addition to shifting) was observed, and

attributed to oxides phase transformations - between monoclinic and orthorhombic - during cycling.[43, 44] Figure 6b shows the voltage profile for a cell cycled at 10 mA/g for the first cycle and then at 0.4 A/g for all subsequent ones. Voltage plateaus were observed around 0.4 V during the 1st lithiation cycle and around 1.28 V during the 1 st delithiation cycle (Figure 6b). A capacity of 821 mAh/g was achieved during the 1st lithiation cycle, but only 76 % of that capacity was recovered upon delithiation. First cycle irreversibility has been a challenge for MXenes tested for LIBs. This irreversibility has been attributed to the formation of a solid electrolyte interphase (SEI), and/or the trapping of Li through irreversible reactions with either surface terminations or other species in-between the Mn+1 Xn Tx layers.[45] However, the first-cycle irreversibility of Mo2 CTx was lower compared to that reported for Ti3 C2 Tx and other MXenes.[10, 11, 46-48] Note that more than 2/3 of the capacity is stored at voltages below 0.5 V, which suggests that Mo2 CTx could well be used as an anode material. After the first cycle, carried out a 10 mA/g, the capacity drops to 423 mAh/g. Thereafter, the cycling rate was increased to 0.4 A/g (inset in Figure 6c). At that rate, the capacity continuously increased with further cycling until it reached 560 mAh/g after 70 cycles (Figure 6c). The capacity shown by Mo2 CTx is higher than the theoretical capacity of graphite anodes (372 mAh/g).[49] Compared to other Mo-based systems, however, this capacity is ~ 7 % lower than what was reported for Mo2 C nanoparticles anchored on graphene (601 mAh/g at 0.4 A/g) at the same rate,[50] and ~ 10% lower than what was reported for MoO 2 /Mo2 C heteronanotubes at slightly higher rates (623 mAh/g at 0.5 A/g).[44] However, this is a first study on Mo2 CTx MXene and optimization of the electrode architecture is expected to enable a major improvement in its capacity, as previously observed for other MXenes.[10, 46, 51] A similar increase in capacity with cycling was reported before for MoO2 /Mo2 C heteronanotubes and was explained by a gradual reduction of the LixMoO2 phase relative to Mo.[44] The same 12

Page 13

argument can be used here for Mo2 CTx since, as shown above, surface oxide species are present. Also, an enhanced electrode accessibility to ions after cycling could be a reason for the observed increase in capacity. The Mo2 CTx -CNT films also showed excellent capability to handle quite high cycling rates. At 5 A/g (~ 20 C) a reversible capacity of ~ 250 mAh/g with coulombic effic iency of > 99 % was retained after 1,000 cycles (Figure 6c). Even at 10 A/g (~ 131 C) a stable reversible capacity of 76 mAh/g was measured even after 1,000 cycles. This suggests d-Mo2 CTx -CNT electrodes could be used as electrodes in high-power batteries or Li- ion capacitors. While the electrodes tested here are relatively thin, thicker electrodes of MXenes are possible. For example 300 µm thick discs of other MXenes systems (viz. Ti3 C2 Tx , and Nb2 CTx ) were studied as electrodes for LIBs. Although they showed lower gravimetric capacity compared commercial graphite, their areal capacities were higher than commercial ones. There is no reason not to assume similar behavior for Mo2 CTx .

3. Conclusions Herein we report, for the first time, on the large-scale synthesis of 2D Mo2 CTx flakes via selective etching of Ga from Mo2 Ga2 C powders using two different etchants and subsequent delamination. The morphologies of the produced flakes depends on the etchant type and delamination method. Etching with LiF/HCl solutions produces less defective flakes as compared to those etched with HF followed by TBAOH treatment. The latter etchant appears to be more aggressive and results in the formation of numerous holes in the flakes. Furthermore, sonication tends to break the flakes into smaller sizes, but produces a higher fraction of single- layer flakes as compared to mild agitation via hand shaking alone. Annealing the delaminated Mo2 CTx 'paper', which appears to drive out the TBAOH molecules - as evidenced by a large contraction in the c-axis lattice parameter - results in an almost 3 orders of magnitude decrease in resistivity at RT. The resistivity decreases with 13

Page 14

increasing temperature, and its temperature dependence changes after annealing indicating a change in transport mechanism(s). At 10 K, the magnetoresistance is positive. Mo2 CTx exhibits semiconducting- like behavior with variable range hopping transport mechanism at low temperatures. The volumetric capacitance of 2 µm thick delaminated-Mo2 CTx ‘paper’ in 1 M H2 SO4 reached 700 F/cm3 at 2 mV/s. The capacitance retention was excellent with almost no degradation after 10,000 charge/discharge cycles. When a 3 µm thick delaminated Mo2 CTx -CNT ‘paper’ electrode was tested against Li, a reversible capacity of 560 mAh/g was achieved at 0.4 A/g, with more than 2/3 of the lithiation capacity stored at voltage below 0.5 V vs. Li/Li+. As importantly, at rates of 5 A/g and 10 A/g, reversible stable capacities of 250 mAh/g and 75 mAh/g were achieved, respectively, for over 1000 cycles, making it a promising anode material for high power batteries and Li- ion capacitors.

4. Experimental Section Synthesis of Mo2 Ga2 C: Powders of Mo2 Ga2 C were synthesized by a solid liquid reaction of Mo2 C and Ga. The details can be found in Ref. [24] In brief, a - 325 mesh Mo2 C powder and Ga (both from Alfa Aesar, Ward Hill, MA, both of 99.5 wt.% purity) were mixed in a 1:8 molar ratio and placed in a quartz tube that was evacuated using a mechanical vacuum pump and sealed. The latter was placed in a horizontal tube furnace that was heated at a rate of 10 °C/min to 850 °C, and held at that temperature for 48 h. After furnace cooling, the lightly sintered material was crushed, using a mortar and pestle, and returned back to the quartz tube. The latter was evacuated, etc. and re-heated at a rate of 10 °C/min to 850 °C and held at temperature for 16 h more. One gram of the lightly sintered Mo2 Ga2 C compact was immersed in a 20 ml of 12 M HCl solution (technical grade, Fisher Scientific, Fair Lawn, NJ) for 2 days, at room temperature 14

Page 15

(RT), while being stirred using a Teflon coated magnet on a stir plate to dissolve any unreacted Ga. Rietveld refinement of the XRD patterns for Mo 2 Ga2 C indicated that < 20 wt. % Mo2 C was present in the powders as a secondary phase (not shown here). The powders were washed with deionized (DI) water several times till a pH of ≈ 6 was reached, then dried by filtration using a nanoporous polypropylene membrane (3501 Coated PP, 0.064 µm pore size, Celgard, USA).

Synthesis of 2D Mo2 CTx: LiF and HCl route: One gram of Mo2 Ga2 C powder was added to a 20 ml of a premixed solution of 3 M of LiF (90+%, Ward Hill, MA, Alfa Aesar) and 12 M HCl. The Mo2 Ga2 C containing mixture was placed on a magnetic stirrer hot plate in an oil bath held at 35 °C for various amounts of time. Afterwards, the mixture was washed through 3 cycles of 1 M of aqueous HCl, followed by 3 cycles of 1 M of aqueous LiCl (Alfa Aesar, 98+%), and finally cycles of DI water until the supernatant reached a pH of approximately 6. In each cycle, the washing was performed by adding 40 mL volume of the solution to the washed powders in a centrifuge tube, and the tube was hand-shaken for 1 min before centrifuging at 5,000 rpm for 2 min. Twenty ml of argon, Ar, deaerated DI water were then added to the washed powder, which was then hand shaken for 5 min., followed by centrifuging for 1 h at 5,000 rpm. The supernatant was collected for further investigation. The settled powder was discarded. HF and TBAOH route: Powders of Mo2 Ga2 C were immersed slowly in a bottle containing 14 M aqueous HF (Fisher Scientific, Fair Lawn, NJ) in a ratio of 1 g to 40 ml. Afterwards the bottle was placed in an oil bath over a stirred hot plate held at 55°C for 160 h, while stirring using a Teflon coated magnet. The resulting suspension was washed with DI water through several cycles till a pH of ≈ 6 was reached. In each cycle, the washing was performed by adding 40 mL of DI water to a centrifuge tube containing the sediment, and the tube was hand-shaken for 1 min before centrifuging at 5,000 rpm for 2 min. 15

Page 16

The settled powder was removed from the centrifuge tube and filtered through a nanoporous polypropylene membrane (3501 Coated PP, 0.064 µm pore size, Celgard, USA) for further investigation. One gram of this filtered powder was added to a 10 ml of an aqueous solution of 54-56 wt.% TBAOH, (C 4 H9 )4 NOH, (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). The mixture was stirred at RT for 18 h. After the prescribed mixing time, the mixture was washed 3 times by 40 ml deaerated DI water each time and the sediment was used for further characterization and delamination. General Characterization Methods: The microstructures and morphologies of the various samples and powders were characterized by TEM (JEOL JEM-2100, Japan) using an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. The TEM samples were prepared by dropping several drops of the sample diluted in deionized water onto a copper grid and dried in air. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was performed using Zeiss Supra 50VP (Carl Zeiss SMT AG, Oberkochen, Germany). A XRD diffractometer (Rigaku Smartlab (Tokyo, Japan) equipped with Cu Kα radiation (40 kV and 44 mA) was used for phase identification. A step scan 0.02°, time per step 2 s, 10x10 mm2 window slit was used. XPS spectra of the d-Mo2 CTx 'paper' were measured by a spectrometer (Physical Electronics, VersaProbe 5000, Chanhassen, MN) employing a 100 µm monochromatic Al-Kα X-ray beam to irradiate the sample surface. Photoelectrons were collected by a 180 ° takeoff angle between the sample surface and the path to the analyzer. Charge neutralization was performed using a dual beam charge neutralizer irradiating low-energy electrons and ion beam to avoid shift in the recorded BE. High-resolution spectra for Mo 3d, C 1s, O 1s, F 1s, and Ga 2p were taken at a pass energy of 11.75 eV with a step size of 0.05 eV. The binding energy scale of all XPS spectra was references to the Fermi- edge (Ef), which was set to a BE of zero eV. To obtain the spectra a free-standing film was mounted on a double sided tape and 16

Page 17

was ground using a copper wire. The quantification, using the obtained core- level intensities, and peak fitting of the core- level spectra was performed using a software package (CasaXPS Version 2.3.16 RP 1.6). Prior to both the quantification and peak fitting the background contributions were subtracted using a Shirley function. The intensity ratios of the 3d5/2 and 3d3/2 peaks were constrained to be 3:2. The temperature-dependent in-plane resistivity measurements were performed in a Physical Property Measurement System (Quantum Design, San Diego). A linear four-point probe geometry was used. Gold wires were attached to the films using silver paint. Positive and negative currents were applied at each temperature to eliminate any thermal effects. The error in the resistivity values is estimated to be ~5%, corresponding to the uncertainty in the sample thickness. The magnetoresistance (MR) measurements were performed with a magnetic field, up to ±9 T, applied out of the plane of the film. Electrochemical Characterization: Supercapacitors: Activated carbon electrodes were prepared by mixing activated carbon (YP-40) with 5 wt. % polytetrafluroetylene (PTFE) in ethanol on a magnetic stirring plate for 24 h at 25 o C. The powders were then kneaded, rolled, and cut into electrodes with a diameter of 9 mm. The Mo2 CTx electrodes were prepared from delaminated Mo2 CTx flakes obtained by HF etching of Mo2 Ga2 C followed by TBAOH intercalation as described above. After TBAOH treatment, the sediment was mixed with DI water (1 g Mo2 CTx per 10 ml of water), deaerated using Ar gas, and followed by sonication in an ice cooled ultrasonic bath for 1 h. The mixture was then centrifuged for 1 h at 5,000 rpm, and the supernatant, which was dark purple in color, was collected. The latter was further diluted with deaerated DI water to a concentration of 1 mg/ml. The diluted suspension was vacuum- filtered onto nanoporous polypropylene membranes (Celgard 3501, 0.064 µm pore size, Celgard LLC) in air. The d-Mo2 CTx ‘paper’

Page 18

was easily separated from the membrane to obtain free-standing Mo2 CTx electrodes that were ≈ 2 µm thick. The electrodes were assembled in a three-electrode Swagelok cell with platinum current collectors, a polypropylene separator (Celgard 3501, Celgard LLC), and a reference electrode of Ag/AgCl in KCl. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) and galvanostatic cycling were performed using a VMP3 potentiostat (Biologic, France). The CV scans were performed from 2 mV/s to 100 mV/s after an initial pre-cycling step to stabilize the current-voltage characteristic. Galvanostatic charge-discharge tests were also performed on a two electrode Swagelok cell with an activated carbon counter electrode for 10,000 cycles using a current density of 10 A/g. Li-Ion Batte ries: The d-Mo2 CTx -CNT paper electrodes were prepared using an alternating vacuum-assisted filtration approach similar to what was described in Ref. [52]. In brief, delaminated Mo2 CTx in DI water (~ 2 mg/mL) and CNT dispersed in DI water (~ 0.2 mg/mL) were alternately filtered to create a layered MXene/CNT structure. The CNT dispersion used in this study was achieved by sonicating 20 mg of multi- walled CNTs in 100 mL of DI water that contained 10 wt. % sodium dodecyl sulfate ( ≥ 99%, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 0.5 h. One mL of Mo2 CTx suspension was filtered, first through a mixed cellulose esters membrane (50 nm, MF-Millipore, EMD Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany), and then 1 mL of CNT suspension was filtered. This alternating filtration process was repeated 10 times. At the end, another 1 mL of Mo2 CTx suspension was filtered. After filtration the materials on the membrane were left to dry at RT in air for 18 h. Afterward, the MXene-CNT ‘paper’ (~8 wt.% CNT) paper, with a thickness of ~ 3 µm, was readily peeled off the membrane and dried under vacuum at 90 °C for 18 h prior to testing. To test the performance of this d-Mo2 CTx -CNT composite electrode, stainless steel coin cells (CR-2032, Hohsen Corp., Osaka, Japan) were assembled in an Ar filled glovebox (O 2 and H2 O less than 1 ppm) using an automatic crimping machine (Hohsen Corp., Osaka Japan). In the coin cells, Li foil was used as a counter electrode, and the free-standing Mo2 CTx -CNT 18

Page 19

paper as the working electrode. The two electrodes were separated by borosilicate glass fiber paper (Whatman GF/A, Buckinghamshire, UK) that was soaked with an electrolyte of 1.2 M LiPF6 solution in a mixture of ethylene carbonate, EC, and dimethyl carbonate, DMC in a 1:2, respectively by weight ratio. The cyclic voltammograms (CV) were recorded at a rate of 0.2 mV/s between 3.0 V and 5.0 mV using VSP300 potentiostat (Biologic, Claix, France), while the galvanostatic cycling between 5.0 mV and 3.0 V at different currents was conducted using Series 4000 MACCOR batteries cycler (MACCOR Inc., Tulsa OK, USA) both tests were carried out at 25 °C.
