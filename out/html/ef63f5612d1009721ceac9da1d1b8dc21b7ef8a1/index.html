Over the past decade, metal halide perovskite photovoltaics have been a major focus of research, with single-junction perovskite solar cells evolving from an initial power conversion efficiency of 3.8% to reach 25.5%. The broad bandgap tunability of perovskites makes them versatile candidates as the subcell in a tandem photovoltaics architecture. Stacking photovoltaic absorbers with cascaded bandgaps in a multi-junction device can potentially overcome the Shockley−Queisser efficiency limit of 33.7% for single-junction solar cells. There is now intense activity in developing tandem solar cells that pair perovskite with either itself or with a variety of mature photovoltaic technologies such as silicon and Cu(In,Ga)(S,Se)2 (CIGS). In this review, we survey recent advances in the field and discuss its outlook.

P

hotovoltaic (PV) technology has been considered the most promising substitute for obtaining energy from traditional fossil fuels. In 1966, Shockley and Quessier calculated the theoretical power conversion efficiency (PCE) limit of a single-junction PV cell to be approximately 33%, based on the principle of detailed balance1. In this case, most of the solar energy that cannot be utilized is attributed to spectrum loss, because of the limited optical response of semiconductor absorbers. Photons with an energy above the bandgap (Eg) can be absorbed by a semiconductor material and generate carriers with energy identical to Eg. The excess energy is lost owing to thermalization, and the photons with energy below Eg cannot be utilized. The Eg of an absorber is crucial to the PCE of a solar cell because Eg is correlated with the short-circuit current (Jsc) and the open-circuit voltage (Voc), which are the two crucial parameters of the PCE. To overcome the Shockley−Quessier limit, tremendous strategies have been proposed and developed, such as multiple exciton generation2, hot carrier collection3, intermediate band structure4 and tandem architecture5. Of these studies, only tandem PV cells have practically surpassed the Shockley−Quessier limit. The highest PCE of tandem devices achieved by six-junction configuration has exceeded 47% (ref. 6). The basic working principle of tandem PVs is that of stacking different Eg junctions in optical series to mitigate the thermal energy loss of photogenerated carriers and realize the efficient spectral splitting. The wider Eg absorber resides at the top to utilize and filter most of the high energetic photons, while the narrower Eg material at the bottom could absorb the rest low-energy photons, enabling maximum utilization of the solar spectrum (Fig. 1a,b). The most common tandem PV cell is based on a double-junction tandem architecture, which contains a wide-Eg front cell (about 1.5–1.9 eV) and a narrow-Eg rear cell (about 0.9–1.3 eV). Compared with a typical single junction solar cell, the ideal Eg is around 1.4−1.5 eV. The front and rear subcells can also be referred to as the top and bottom subcells, respectively. Double-junction tandem devices typically have two configurations based on how the two junctions are electrically stacked: two-terminal (2-T) tandem and four-terminal (4-T) tandem. Although the fabrication and operation processes of 4-T are relatively simple, the additional two electrodes and corresponding optical loss usually result in a high cost for this configuration. On the other hand, the 2-T (or monolithic) tandem cell is fabricated sequentially on a single substrate with one transparent front electrode and one opaque rear electrode, where the front cell and rear cell are connected by an interconnection layer (ICL). As displayed in Fig. 1d, the ICL is where the recombination of photogenerated charge carriers from the front and rear subcells occurs, to retain the charge neutrality, either in a metallic recombination layer or a band-to-band tunnelling junction layer. Generally, a 2-T tandem PV setup can avoid extra manufacturing costs and spectral loss, which makes it more promising than 4-T tandem cells in practical applications. However, owing to limitations in the fabrication process and the manipulation of electrical coupling, it is much more challenging to fabricate monolithic tandem cells than 4-T tandem cells. The selection of ICL is affected by the surface roughness of the rear cell, compatibility of the solvent and deposition temperature. More importantly, the bottom cell must be stable during the processing of the top cell and ICL, and the Jsc of both cells must be well matched. That said, persistent research efforts have been devoted to achieving high-performance 2-T tandem PVs7,8. Over the past decade, metal halide perovskite materials have been a focus of next-generation PV research owing to their superior optoelectronic properties. The record PCE of single-junction perovskite PVs has surged from an initial 3.8% to 25.5% (refs. 9–15). The general chemical formula of a metal halide perovskite is ABX3, where A refers to a monovalent cation like caesium (Cs+), methylammonium (MA+) or formamidinium (FA+); B refers to a divalent cation like Pb2+ or Sn2+; and X refers to a halide anion (Fig. 1e). The remarkable bandgap tuneability enabled by rich varieties of composition engineering is one of the major advantages of perovskite materials, making them versatile candidates as the subcell materials in a tandem device architecture. Wide-bandgap perovskite materials as front cells

In this section, we discuss wide-bandgap perovskite materials as front cells with composition engineering strategies, then move on to how their crystal growth could be affected and manipulated. Because all of these factors can potentially lead to changes in defect physics and interfacial charge dynamics, defect passivation strategies and interface engineering of wide-bandgap perovskites will also be covered (Table 1). Composition engineering. For lead halide perovskite materials, both the valence band maximum and conduction-band minimum are determined by the orbitals of the lead and halide atoms. Therefore, tuning the composition of perovskites can lead to changes in their bandgaps. For example, MAPbX3-based perovskites have been both theoretically and experimentally investigated to show a shift in the bandgap from about 2.25 eV for MAPbBr3 to about 1.55 eV for MAPbI3 (refs. 16,17). Heo and Im18 used MAPbBr3 as the front cell and MAPbI3 as the rear cell to achieve one of the earliest perovskite-based tandem solar cells with 10.8% efficiency and over 2.2 V Voc. As the front cell, MAPbBr3 successfully split MAPbI3’s absorption spectrum in the visible light region, which allows effective current matching. However, when the rear cell has an even lower bandgap, such as Pb−Sn perovskites (Eg ≈ 1.25 eV), Si (Eg = 1.1 eV), or copper indium selenide (Eg ≈ 0.98 eV), the optimal bandgap region for the front A

cell will be in the range from 1.8 eV to 1.65 eV to maximize current matching. McMeekin et al.19 systematically studied the halide composition tunability in the more thermally stable FAPb(I1-xBrx)3 and FACsPb(I1-xBrx)3 perovskites, in which case, caesium (Cs) is required to achieve continuous and stable perovskite bandgap evolution in the entire Br-to-I range, from around 2.25 eV when x = 1 to around 1.53 eV when x = 0. Otherwise, a ‘yellowing’ (indicating that the material is highly degradable) of the FAPb(I1-xBrx)3 films would occur for compositions of x between 0.3 and 0.6, associated with the transition from a trigonal (x < 0.3) to cubic (x > 0.5) structure (Fig. 2a). With the optimal perovskite composition, FA0.83Cs0.17Pb(I0. 6Br0.4)3, they achieved over 22%-efficiency perovskite/Si heterojunction tandem solar cells. Beal et al. discussed similar effects on mixed halide compositions for inorganic caesium lead halide perovskites from pure CsPbBr3 to pure CsPbI3, and were the first to report a structurally stable 1.9-eV bandgap CsPbI2Br with 6.5% efficiency20, which is so far one of the most promising inorganic wide-bandgap perovskites, whose efficiency has most recently reached 16.79% with the incorporation of CaCl2 (ref. 21). Bush et al. reported FA−Cs mixed-cation systems, where increasing the A-site Cs content could also increase the bandgap while maintaining a constant halide composition. Instead of pursuing higher Br-containing compositions, using relatively more A-site Cs is preferable in optimizing the bandgap, improving the Voc and maintaining photostability (Fig. 2b)22. The reason is that although the goal in tuning the perovskite composition for front cells is to achieve suitable bandgaps, issues could arise such as phase instability and halide segregation in Br-rich systems. In fact, incorporating alkali elements such as Cs and Rb not only stabilizes the perovskite structure in the entire Br-to-I range, but also creates a more homogeneous halide distribution and suppresses halide segregation of these wide-bandgap perovskites, driven by excited charge carriers upon further illumination or current injection23. Crystal growth control. The solution processability of perovskites provides huge capacities to manipulate and optimize their crystal growth kinetics for highly crystalline thin films with low defect densities. Similar to regular perovskite materials, the formation of wide-bandgap perovskites will be affected by precursor-coordinating molecule interactions27, the introduction of antisolvents28,29, annealing conditions30, surface energy of the substrates28, precursor concentration and solvent removal rate31, and so on. Related to the previous discussions on composition engineering, one should expect that changes in composition affect the structural stability: varying the Cs ratio in the precursor solution could lead to an apparent change in film crystallinity, which further affects photostability, trap state distribution, and charge transport and recombination32,33. Dang et al. further utilized in situ grazing incident wide-angle X-ray scattering to demonstrate the synergistic effect of Cs and Rb in an FA-based perovskite on the crystal growth kinetics (Fig. 2d)34. Simultaneous addition of Cs+ and Rb+ suppressed the formation of both the solvated and hexagonal phases, but significantly boosted a direct conversion into a single desired 3C-phase (often known as the α-phase). Using such an FA–MA–Cs–Rb quarto-cation perovskite with a 1.73-eV bandgap, a stabilized efficiency of 17.3% was reported with minimum hysteresis35. On the other hand, several important additives have been found to significantly affect the crystal growth for wide-bandgap perovskites. MACl has been independently reported by Kim et al. and Chen et al. to benefit the perovskite grain morphology to grow larger grains36,37. The latter work also incorporated MAH2PO2 into the perovskite precursor to suppress nonradiative recombination and to passivate the grain boundaries, and achieved 18.6% efficiency with a Voc of 1.19 V and an optical Eg of 1.70 eV. Another additive is Pb(SCN)2, which also assists in growing large perovskite grains38, and has been reported to most effectively improve the performance of perovskite solar cells when combined together with phenylethylammonium iodide (PEAI). Kim et al.39 reported these bimolecular additives in a perovskite/CIGS-stacked tandem to overcome the separate challenges associated with each additive: PEAI could grow two-dimensional (2D) structures at the grain boundaries and balance the excess undesirable Pb2+ introduced by Pb(SCN)2, but limited the grain size and suppressed intergrain charge transport; while Pb(SCN)2 could increase the grain size to improve the overall electrical conductivity (Fig. 2e). As a result, remarkable improvements in the structural and optoelectrical properties—including crystallinity, defect density, energetic disorder, charge mobility and carrier lifetime—were observed. .

Defect passivation and interface engineering. Regardless of theoretically predicted defect tolerance, proper management of defects in halide perovskites is very important to achieve high mobility, long carrier lifetimes, minimized nonradiative losses and thus high-performance solar cells. Defects are also reported to easily capture moisture and oxygen and to become the points where device degradation initiates. In fact, similar to most crystalline semiconductors, defect management in perovskite materials starts from the first step in their processing, which includes composition engineering and crystal growth. Thus, most methodologies introduced in the previous sections could eventually lead to suppressed defect density. Most notably, the above-mentioned PEAI and its derivatives have been reported to show a superior defect passivation effect, although its exact mechanism in interacting with perovskite defects remains debatable. Although some have reported that the PEAI molecule itself passivates surface defects of perovskites42, these molecules could also form 2D/3D heterostructures at the grain boundaries of perovskite films, resulting in a longer carrier lifetime and improved device photovoltage43. Gharibzadeh et al. used this method for a 1.74-eV bandgap (FA0.83Cs0.17Pb[I0.6Br0.4]3) perovskite to achieve 19.4% efficiency with a Voc over 1.30 V (ref. 44). Incorporating potassium or sodium halides into the perovskite has also been reported (Fig. 2f) to be effective in suppressing the formation of electronic defects and improving device stability45,46. Other A-site additives that passivate the perovskite including dipolar cation MA+ and alkali cation K+, both proven effective in reducing defect density and improving device stability46–48. Recently, organic ionic compounds have also been discovered to be effective additives in passivating perovskite materials. Although the bandgap of their perovskite materials needs further widening to serve as a front cell, Bai et al. and Lin et al. independently reported an imidazolium-based ionic liquid and a piperidinium-based ionic solid, respectively, as additives that enhanced the PV performance and device stability49,50. Since these achievements were realized by retarding phase segregation and pin-hole formation, their strategy could be very promising for efficient wide-bandgap perovskite cells as well. Several post-treatments have also been reported to be particularly effective for wide-bandgap perovskite materials. Zhou et al. used benzylamine molecules to treat the surface and grain boundaries of an Eg = 1.73-eV perovskite cell that achieved a 1.24-V VOC and 18.1% efficiency51. Luo et al. reported a secondary grain-growth technique triggered by guanidinium bromide for a 1.62-eV bandgap perovskite that delivered similar PV performance52. Another frequently used method is to introduce an extra passivation layer. Peng et al. inserted an ultrathin polymer-fullerene blended with poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA)/PCBM53. Jaysankar et al. applied an Al2O3 passivation layer, both between the perovskite and the transporting layer54. Bett et al.55 used one of the above methods in a 2-T perovskite-Si tandem device and achieved over 20% efficiency with a record Voc of 1.85 V. To achieve highly efficient wide-bandgap perovskite front cells, energy alignments could be further optimized via interface engineering to improve the device Voc. Narrow-bandgap perovskite materials as rear subcells

Narrow Eg perovskites have received tremendous attention for the part they play in constructing highly efficient and stable perovskite-perovskite tandem PVs58–60. Partial substitution of Pb by Sn to form Sn−Pb alloyed perovskites has until now been the only strategy that can achieve an Eg as low as 1.2 eV, which perfectly complements the wide-Eg perovskite to make full use of the solar spectrum and establish highly efficient all-perovskite tandem cells. The bowing effect of the bandgap enables the narrowest bandgap of perovskite materials to be obtained at a 60% Sn ratio61. However, Sn-based perovskites exhibit inferior semiconducting properties, such as the short carrier lifetime induced by high trap density61 and the high intrinsic carrier density caused by the spontaneous oxidation of Sn2+ ions62. Here we focus on narrow-bandgap Sn-based perovskite used as the rear cell and discuss strategies to improve the efficiency and stability of the solar cells through the suppression of Sn2+ oxidation and defect passivation (Tables 1 and 2). .

m

Suppression of tin (II) oxidation. In the presence of oxygen, Sn-based perovskite PVs deteriorate efficiency and stability owing to the ease of oxidation from Sn2+ to Sn4+, which produces Sn vacancies, leading to a p-type self-doping effect63,64. The introduction of SnF2 to the perovskite precursor has been successful in suppressing the oxidation of Sn2+ by adding excess Sn2+ without the formation of undesired phases (Fig. 3a)65. However, an overdose of SnF2 will induce severe phase segregation on the surface of perovskite thin films. To tackle this problem, various strategies have been developed to coordinate with SnF2 and to prohibit phase segregation, such as pyrazine66 and FACl67. A high PCE of 4.8% and 15.8% was thus achieved for pure Sn and Sn−Pb-mixed perovskites, respectively. On the other hand, adding a second additive can hinder the oxidation reaction of Sn2+ more effectively, such as ascorbic acid68, hypophosphorous acid69, or trihydrazine dihydriodide70, and so on. Hydrazinium chloride, a well known reducing agent, was employed in the solvent as a co-additive with SnF2, where the decomposition of hydrazine-released electrons could effectively retard the oxidation process of Sn2+ (ref. 71). The resulting PCE of the corresponding pure-Sn-based perovskite PV was boosted up to 5.8%. Yan et al. systematically studied the synergistic effect of the sulfonate group, amino group and hydroxyl group on the antioxidant activities by introducing three small molecules—phenolsulfonic acid (PSA), 2-aminophenol-4-sulfonic acid (APSA), and the potassium salt of hydroquinone sulfonic acid (KHQSA)—into the perovskite precursor (Fig. 3b)72. Owing to the strongest interaction of the sulfonate group, amino group and hydroxyl group with Sn2+, KHQSA exhibited the highest antioxidant activity. Hence, the addition of KHQSA, along with SnCl2 serving as the co-additives, significantly enhances the air stability of the Sn-based perovskite PV. After a 500-h exposure to air without encapsulation, the device maintains 80% of its initial PCE. The introduction of larger organic cations such as butylammonium (BA)73, guanidinium (GA)74, phenylethylammonium (PEA)75, and ethylenediammonium (EDA)73 provides an additional approach to preventing the oxidization of Sn2+. Diau et al. demonstrated that the addition of butylammonium iodide (BAI) and 1% of ethylenediammonium iodide (EDAI2) could improve the stability and the PCE simultaneously without formation of the 2D phase73. Recently, Tan et al. employed a small amount of metallic Sn powder into the precursor to prohibit the oxidation of Sn2+ in the precursor solution via a comproportionating reaction (Fig. 3c)76. Through this simple method, the Sn vacancies effectively decreased, and thus the carrier diffusion length was substantially enhanced, resulting in a record PCE of 21.1% in mixed Pb−Sn perovskite PVs. Furthermore, the same group utilized the surface-anchoring zwitterionic antioxidant to push the PCE to 21.7% (ref. 77). Defect passivation. Anion doping, with bromide, chloride or thiocyanate, for example, is an effective method of passivating the defects in mixed Sn−Pb perovskites and enhancing the PV properties. For instance, Yan et al. reported that the incorporation of chlorine into perovskite bulk materials could significantly enlarge the grain size, reduce defect density and achieve a high PCE of 18.4% (ref. 78). Furthermore, the same group found that the addition of bromine could also effectively decrease the vacancies of Sn and I ions owing to the formation of Br-rich grain boundaries after high-temperature annealing79. The substantially reduced Voc deficit, caused by passivated grain boundaries, resulted in a PCE of over 19%. Aside from suppressing the grain boundary defects by incorporating halogens, introducing the thiocyanate anion SCN− can also suppress the formation of defects by regulating the grain growth of perovskites. Owing to the Lewis-base nature of the SCN− group, it can form a strong Lewis-base−acid adduct with Sn2+ to regulate the nucleation of perovskite crystals80. The resulting film shows improved crystallinity and morphologies and the corresponding pure-Sn-based PV device thus exhibits an excellent PCE of 9.41%. A large group of organic ammonium cations, such as PEA+, + FA , GA+ and N-(3-aminopropyl)−2-pyrrolidinone, have also been reported to efficiently passivate the defect80–84. By forming a hydrophobic 2D protecting layer at grain boundaries and surfaces, the target perovskite shows a significantly enhanced photoluminescence lifetime, suppressed defect density and much extended stability. Although it remains ambiguous whether the layered 2D perovskite is detrimental to the charge carrier extraction and transport, the PCEs of PV devices employing large organic cations have approached 10% for pure Sn-based perovskites and 20% for Sn− Pb mixed perovskites with largely enhanced long-term stability. In addition, metallic cations like Cd2+ can de-dope the Sn-containing perovskite by filling the Sn vacancies. The introduction of a small dose of Cd2+ led to an increased diffusion length, which allowed for the fabrication of a thicker perovskite (Fig. 3d), resulting in an enlarged Jsc and PCE85. Recently, incorporating passivation agents with both effective cation and anion (that is, GASCN), has been reported to improve the carrier lifetime to over 1 µs, reduce defect density and manipulate surface morphology. As depicted in Fig. 3e, a PCE of over 20% for the narrow-Eg perovskite PV cell was achieved with an active layer over 1 µm thick82.

Perovskite is the only polycrystalline thin-film PV absorber material that has demonstrated >20% PCE with a wide bandgap (around 1.7 eV or larger). This makes perovskites uniquely fitted for tandem applications, not only for all-perovskite tandem devices, but also for pairing with mature PV technologies (such as Si and CIGS) to further boost their performance86. We focus on the 2-T configuration, as this is most scientifically challenging and regarded as more promising for practical applications. For a 2-T tandem device, the key components include the top subcell, the bottom subcell, the transparent electrode and the ICL. Here we describe the transparent electrode and ICL components. We first review transparent electrode technology, which is mostly independent from specific perovskite-based tandem technologies. A good ICL usually requires voltage addition from two subcells, minimum optical loss, good charge conduction between two subcells, minimum lateral charge conduction, and an effective barrier to protect the underlying subcell during top cell processing. Despite these common requirements, the ICL structure is often tailored for specific bottom subcell technology. We use the evolution of ICL components to discuss the progress of each primary perovskite-based tandem technology. Transparent electrodes. Transparent electrodes are a critical component in perovskite-based tandem devices. Here, the transparent electrode refers to the top transparent electrode of a tandem device stack, where the light is illuminated. In general, a transparent electrode needs to have good transparency as well as electrical conductivity. As a tandem device needs to respond to a wide range of solar spectra, a transparent electrode also needs to have superior transparency in both visible and near-infrared wavelength range. Various transparent electrode candidates were studied for semitransparent perovskite solar cells for potential integration into tandem devices during early-stage development. Examples of transparent electrodes include nanomaterials (Ag nanowire87, carbon nanotube88 and graphene)89, metallic thin films (Au/LiF90, MoOx/Au/MoOx91, MoOx/Au/Ag/MoOx92 and Cu/Au93), transparent conductive oxides (TCOs) (MoOx/IZO94, MoOx/In2O3:H95, MoOx/AZO96, AZO nanoparticles/ITO97 and ZnO nanoparticles/AZO98), and conductive polymers (the PEDOT:PSS/Ag grid98). For example, the optimum bandgap for the perovskite top cell to pair with Si is ~1.75 eV based on SQ modelling117. In addition, the bandgap of a semiconductor generally changes with temperature. The bandgap of Si and perovskite usually follow an opposite trend as the temperature is increased: the bandgap narrows for Si but it increases for perovskite. These factors would shift the devices away from current matching that is optimized for AM1.5 G and room temperature conditions. Hörantner et al. examined this issue with a rigorous optical and device simulation on perovskite/Si 2-T tandem solar cells117. Aydin et al. later experimentally studied the temperature dependence of perovskite/Si 2-T tandem devices, and found that the tandems can easily reach about 60–75 °C when working outdoors118. Calculating through a modified temperature-dependent radiative effective model, the optimal perovskite bandgap is determined to be <1.68 eV (at 25 °C), which is consistent with Hörantner et al117. .

Perovskite/CIGS tandem solar cells. CIGS thin-film solar cells achieve their best efficiency at relatively narrow bandgaps, around 1.1 eV, with the possibility of tuning to near 1.0 eV while still maintaining a high-performance level. This bandgap range is so far beyond the range of low-bandgap perovskites that it provides an attractive option for combining a mature narrow-Eg thin-film PV technology with wide-Eg perovskites to form efficient and stable tandem devices. Todorov et al. demonstrated the first functional 2-T perovskite/ CIGS tandem device with a 10.9% efficiency by using an ITO recombination layer connecting a CIGS subcell with a p-i-n perovskite subcell119. In this study, the zinc oxide (ZnO)-based layers in a standard CIGS device stack were not used, to avoid the incompatibility with perovskite fabrication. Light management is critical in improving the efficiency of perovskite-based tandem solar cells. Due to the refractive index (n) mismatch between the multiple layers in the tandem device stack, the reflection losses at the interfaces between adjacent layers can lead to significant losses of power output. A major optical loss is caused by the reflection loss from the front transparent electrode interfacing with air. A common approach to mitigate this loss is to use an antireflection coating. For example, lithium fluoride (LiF) and magnesium fluoride (MgF2) with low refractive index are widely used31,35,109,111. Anti-reflection foils with textured surface applied on top of tandems were also reported124–126. For perovskite/Si tandem, another major optical loss is from the bottom Si subcell. Owing to the large refractive index difference between the perovskite (about 2) and Si (about 4), the light reflection at the smooth Si top surface is large. Texturing the Si top surface is generally effective in reducing reflection; however, it would be challenging to fabricate a conformal perovskite top subcell on the textured surface. As discussed in the perovskite/Si tandem section, the two general approaches—using either a hybrid two-step sequential perovskite deposition process or a thick perovskite layer to planarize the textured Si substrate—have the potential to enable highly efficient perovskite/Si 2-T tandem configurations on a textured Si subcell31,111,114. These and other light-management schemes as well as detailed optical modelling are expected to enable advances in perovskite-based tandem solar cell development.

Future perspectives

The PCEs of perovskite-based tandem solar cells have been as high as 29.5%, exceeding the record PCEs of all types of single-junction cells under one-Sun illumination. Therefore, improving stability, reducing the cell-to-module gap and lowering manufacturing costs are the most significant aspects for future research aiming to accelerate the commercialization of perovskite-based tandem solar cells. PV industries and consumers expect a commercial product to last for more than 20 years to lower the levelized cost of energy. However, the intrinsic and extrinsic instability of perovskite subcells limits the lifetime of tandem cells, such as the phase segregation of wide-Eg perovskite, and the insufficient thermal stability of the narrow-Eg perovskite. Regardless of the numerous reports on enhanced perovskite stability127, so far, a complete understanding of the degradation mechanisms of halide perovskites is still lacking. Hence, it is necessary to employ advanced techniques to closely monitor the degradation of perovskites and develop more targeted mitigation strategies to further elongate the device lifetime. For example, drive-level capacitance profiling could be applied to map the spatial and energetic distribution of defect states128, which could help to further understand the degradation pathway of perovskites and their interface. On the other hand, damageless techniques such as cryogenic electron microscopy have been developed to study the structural and compositional change of perovskites under external stimuli129. The biggest challenge for scaling up perovskite-based tandems to the module level is to fabricate homogeneous perovskite layers, which require improvements to fabrication methods such as blade coating or vacuum deposition. For perovskite cells integrated with mature industrial Si or CIGS cells, finding a suitable coating method for the ICL and perovskite layer on the rough CIGS or textured Si surfaces remains challenging owing to the submicrometre thickness of the perovskite layer. Enhancing the diffusion length of the perovskite layer by increasing the carrier lifetime and reducing the effective mass can achieve a thicker perovskite film that could fully cover the roughness of the front cell. Reducing the defect density to enhance lifetime is the most frequently used strategy now130. Modifying the effective mass of perovskites by means of rational composition engineering is another possible approach, as yet not fully explored. Although vacuum deposition could realize a conformal thin film on the rough surface, the compositional complexity of wide-bandgap perovskites prohibits the application of vacuum deposition and increases the cost of the entire cell. For all perovskite solar cells—apart from the fabrication issue— the processing of suitable ICLs without damaging the rear cells represents a challenging limitation. Owing to the difficulty of selecting an orthogonal solvent of ICLs, applying a solvent-free deposition method such as ALD or sputtering might enable the fabrication of compact ICLs with large areas. Although 4-T tandem cells often exhibit higher PCE values than 2-T configurations, the extra cost of 4-T tandem cells limits the application of the 4-T configuration in conventional solar technology. However, when considering the stability issues of perovskites, the lifetime of tandem solar cells could be extended in a 4-T configuration simply by substituting the degraded perovskite subcells if the added cost of the replacement were acceptable, because the subcells in 4-T tandem cells are relatively independent. Therefore, the 4-T configuration might be a temporary solution to lowering the cost of perovskite tandem cells and pushing forward the commercialization of perovskite tandems before the stability issues of perovskites are fully resolved. We note that although current efforts focus on two-junction tandem solar cells, perovskite-based triple-junction solar cells could become attractive because of their potential to deliver a higher theoretical efficiency limit. At present, high-efficiency multijunction (with >2 junctions) solar cells are mostly built with group-III to -V compounds (for example, GaAs and GaInP)6. However, high manufacturing costs prohibit commercialization. In this context, perovskites with tuneable bandgaps from around 1.2 eV to 2.3 eV offer an option for developing highly efficient and low-cost multijunction solar cells. According to a recent simulation by Hörantner et al., triple junction solar cells based on all-perovskite and perovskite−perovskite−silicon can reach efficiencies of 37% and 39%, respectively131. Along this direction, several groups—Ballif et al.132, McMeekin et al.133 and Tan et al.134—have performed encouraging proof-of-concept monolithic triple-junction perovskite-based solar cells with Voc higher than 2.5 V. All of these works theoretically and experimentally show the promise of high-efficiency perovskite-based triple-junction PSCs. We strongly believe that most of the above-mentioned issues will be addressed in the near future through the efforts of academia and industry. Perovskite-based tandem solar cells will come to play a significant part in PV technology. .
