Abstract Rechargeable zinc‐ion batteries (ZIBs) are emerging as a promising alternative for Li‐ion batteries. However, the developed cathodes suffer from sluggish Zn2+ diffusion kinetics, leading to poor rate capability and inadequate cycle life. Herein, an in situ polyaniline (PANI) intercalation strategy is developed to facilitate the Zn2+ (de)intercalation kinetics in V2O5. In this way, a remarkably enlarged interlayer distance (13.90 Å) can be constructed alternatively between the V-O layers, offering expediting channels for facile Zn2+ diffusion. Importantly, the electrostatic interactions between the Zn2+ and the host O2−, which is another key factor in hindering the Zn2+ diffusion kinetics, can be effectively blocked by the unique π‐conjugated structure of PANI. As a result, the PANI‐intercalated V2O5 exhibits a stable and highly reversible electrochemical reaction during repetitive Zn2+ insertion and extraction, as demonstrated by in situ synchrotron X‐ray diffraction and Raman studies. Further first‐principles calculations clearly reveal a remarkably lowered binding energy between Zn2+ and host O2−, which explains the favorable kinetics in PANI‐intercalated V2O5. Benefitting from the above, the overall electrochemical performance of PANI‐intercalated V2O5 electrode is remarkable improved, exhibiting excellent high rate capability of 197.1 mAh g−1 at current density of 20 A g−1 with capacity retention of 97.6% over 2000 cycles.

Developing advanced rechargeable battery technologies that enable high energy density coupled with high rate capability, low cost, reliable safety, and long calendar life is of great technological importance for the emerging large‐scale applications such as electric vehicles and energy‐storage grids.[1-4] Despite the commercial success in portable electronics market, Li‐ion batteries are increasingly considered unsustainable to be scaled up due to the limited abundance of Li and high safety risks arising from the use of flammable organic electrolytes.[5-7] In this regard, exploring aqueous rechargeable batteries based on naturally abundant resources is becoming an urgent but appealing task nowadays.[8-10] Among the currently developed aqueous batteries beyond Li‐ion technologies, Zn‐ion batteries have captured the most extensive interest owing to the distinct advantages of Zn metal anodes, such as high natural abundance (i.e., low price), environmental benignity, and relatively low redox potential (−0.762 V vs Zn/Zn2+).[1113] Moreover, the two‐electron (Zn0/2+) redox couple endows a higher theoretical capacity (820 mAh g−1) of zinc‐ion batteries (ZIBs) in comparison with lithium/sodium ion batteries.[14-16] Despite the advantageous prospect over lithium ion batteries, the lack of suitable cathode materials for rechargeable ZIBs has severely hampered the practical applications.[17, 18] Layered materials offering expedite Zn2+ channels are generally recognized as preferable cathodes for ZIBs due to the intrinsic (de)intercalation‐type storage mechanism.[19] At present, several families of layered materials, including polymorphous MnO2 (α‐, β‐, γ‐type, etc.), Prussian blue analogs (CuHCF, ZnHCF, etc.), and vanadium‐based compounds (V2O5, VS2, LiV3O8, etc.),

Page 3

have been developed to store and host Zn2+ in aqueous electrolytes.[20-27] Among them, vanadium pentoxide (V2O5), a common cathode for Li‐ and Na‐ion batteries, are extensively studied as a promising ZIB cathode due to its tunable layered structure and multiple redox states of vanadium.[28-30] However, the interlayer distance in the original V2O5 framework (orthogonal, space group: Pmn2 ) is actually confined for Zn2+, which sterically hinders the ion diffusion kinetics and eventually leads to fast performance degradation over long‐term cycling.[31, 32] Hydrated V2O5 (V2O5·n H2O) adopting a bilayer structure has recently gained attention, for which the pillaring water molecules enlarge the interlayer spacing.[33] Nevertheless, Zn2+ could also coordinate with water molecules in aqueous solution, forming hydrated ions with a larger size of ≈5.5 Å that surely brings about extra difficulty in zinc‐ion insertion and extraction.[34-36] In order to facilitate the kinetic process, an intuitive approach is to introduce intercalated guests, such as Li+, Na+, Ca2+, and Mg2+, for enlarged interlayer distances and thereby enhanced rate and cycling performance.[37-40] Although these achievements are definitely impressive, the repetitive Zn2+ insertion and extraction still inevitably induce structural degradation of host materials during the charging/discharging process.[41] In addition to the interlayer distance, Zn‐ion intercalation kinetics is also limited by the Coulombic interactions between Zn2+ and anions in the host structure.[42, 43] Recent investigations demonstrate that oxide cathodes display much more sluggish kinetics than sulfide counterparts due to the stronger electronegativity of O2− than S2−.[14] For the bilayer V2O5·n H2O, the structural water works as a charge shield for zinc ions that partially reduces their interactions with the host oxygen‐ sublattice.[44, 45] Nevertheless, the loss of structural water during charge/discharge may cause the collapse of layer structure and severe capacity fade.[46] Therefore, for further improving the overall performance of ZIBs, one has to weaken/shield the Coulombic interactions between Zn2+ and the lattice anions of hosts. Taking these factors into consideration, exploration of effective strategies capable for enlarged interlayer distance with excellent structural integrity and simultaneously shielding the Coulombic interactions may hold great promise, which obviously is a great challenge. In this work, we design and facilely synthesize a polyaniline (PANI)‐intercalated V2O5 (designated as PVO) with enlarged interlayer distance to facilitate the Zn2+ insertion/extraction kinetics for high‐performance ZIBs. The intercalation of PANI not only expands the diffusion channels for facilitating Zn2+ diffusion but also maintains the structural stability as interlayer pillars.[47] Especially, its unique π‐conjugated structure serving as electron‐ reservoir simultaneously shields the electrostatic interactions between Zn2+ and the V2O5 host.[48] In situ synchrotron X‐ray diffraction (XRD) coupled with Raman and X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) demonstrates a stable and highly reversible electrochemical

Page 4

process occurred in PANI‐V2O5 during Zn2+ (de)intercalation, which is responsible for the improved battery performance. The incorporation of PANI, as elucidated by first‐principles calculations, remarkably lowers the binding energy between Zn2+ and host oxygen‐sublattice from 2.06 to 1.67 eV, therefore promoting the extraction/insertion process of Zn2+. Moreover, the intercalation of PANI leads to an intermediate energy band across the Fermi level, thereby facilitating the electron transport during electrochemical process.[49] Benefitting from the above features, the superior zinc ion storage performance including high rate performance of 197.1 mAh g−1 at current density of 20 A g−1 and unprecedented cycling stability of up to 2000 cycles with a capacity retention of 97.6% has been achieved in the present PANI‐intercalated V2O5, holding a great promise to high‐rate and long‐life ZIBs. The PANI‐intercalated V2O5 were obtained by in situ hydrothermal reaction based on a dissolution–recrystallization process, as shown in Figure 1a. Under the hydrothermal condition, V2O5 particles were first dissolved to generate free vanadium species, followed by nucleation and growth process. The XRD pattern shown in Figure S1 in the Supporting Information indicated the initial product (curve a) is orthogonal V2O5 (JCPDS card 89‐0612) without any formation of impurities. As the reaction proceeded, protonated aniline inserted into the interlayers of orthorhombic V2O5 and polymerized by chemical oxidation. As observed in the XRD pattern (curve c in Figure S1, Supporting Information), a new diffraction peak appears in the low angle (6.47°), indicating successful insertion of polyaniline. When the reaction time was prolonged to 2 h, orthogonal V2O5 gradually transformed into bilayered V2O5 induced by interlayered polyaniline under hydrothermal reaction. In this work, different concentrations of aniline (5, 60, and 180 µL) were introduced into the synthesis of PANI‐intercalated V2O5 and referred as PVO‐5, PVO‐60, and PVO‐180. All the diffraction peaks of PANI‐intercalated V2O5 samples (PVO‐5, PVO‐60, and PVO‐180) could be assignable to (00l ) Bragg reflections, indicating the coherent lamellar‐ordered layers assembling along the c‐axis (Figure S2, Supporting Information).[50] Specifically, the sharp diffraction peak located at 6.353°, corresponding to the (001) lattice plane of V2O5, suggests a remarkably enlarged interlayer distance of 13.90 Å, which is much larger than that of the hydrate V2O5 (HVO) without PANI insertion (Figure 1b). The crystal structures of V2O5 with and without PANI intercalation are presented in Figure S3 in the Supporting Information. It has been demonstrated that the widened interlayer distance would effectively promote Zn2+ insertion and extraction, therefore leading to an improved electrochemical performance.[51] The success of PANI intercalation in V2O5 is further confirmed by Fourier transform infrared (FT‐IR) spectroscopy and thermogravimetry coupled with differential thermal analysis (TG‐DTA). As displayed in Figure 1c, the peaks at around 3230 and 1615 cm−1 are assigned to the stretching and bending vibrations of N-H bonds,

respectively, while the peaks at 1465, 1303, and 1132 cm−1 are attributed to the stretching vibrations of C-C, C-N, and C-H groups.[52-54] These FT‐IR results firmly demonstrate the existence of PANI in the framework of the as‐synthesized V2O5 nanosheets. In addition, the TG‐ DTA curves clearly reveal an abrupt weight loss of 11.33% accompanied with a continuous endothermal process at 400 °C, indicating the evaporation and thermal decomposition of PANI.[55] Besides, there is another two weight losses of 2.21% and 7.84% below 350 °C, which can be attributed to the evaporation of physically absorbed water and structural water molecules, respectively (Figure 1d). Figure 2a shows the scanning electron microscopy (SEM) image of the as‐prepared PVO‐60 sample, showing a flower‐like nanoarchitecture composed of uniform nanosheets. The morphology of the primary nanosheets is further confirmed by the transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images in different scales (Figure 2b–f). Obvious layered structure with a widened interlayer distance of 13.83 Å can be observed (Figure 2e), which is consistent well with the XRD results. The lattice fringes corresponding to the (312) and (021) planes are also clearly observed from high‐resolution TEM image (HRTEM, Figure 2f). This result as well as the corresponding selected‐area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern (Figure 2g) confirms the high degree of crystallinity for the as‐synthesized samples. Furthermore, the elemental mapping of energy‐dispersive X‐ray spectra (EDS) indicates the uniform distribution of V, O, C, and N elements in the PVO‐60 composite (Figure 2h,i), also confirming the successful intercalation of PANI molecules. Electrochemical performance of the PVO‐60 electrode was examined with typical coin‐type cell at room temperature. The cyclic voltammetry (CV) profiles of PVO‐60 electrode present two pairs of redox peaks located at 1.01/0.906 V and 0.58/0.46 V, corresponding to the continuous redox couples of V5+/V4+ and V4+/V3+, respectively (Figure 3a).[33, 51] It should be noted that the initial three CV curves are roughly overlapped, revealing a highly reversible reaction of PVO‐60 in the aqueous Zn‐ion battery. Figure 3b shows the discharge/charge curves of PVO‐60 for the first 200 cycles at a current density of 1 A g−1. The discharge profiles exhibit a distinct sloping discharge/charge potential plateau, which is assignable to the intercalation of active Zn species. The discharge capacity of 398.8 mAh g−1 is delivered in the initial cycle and remains at 371.2 mAh g−1 after 200 cycles at 1 A g−1 (Figure 3c). With gradually increasing the current rate, the PVO‐60 electrode exhibits average reversible discharge capacity of 375.2, 315.1, 264.6, and 197.1 mAh g−1, at 1, 5, 10, and 20 A g−1, respectively, and meanwhile HVO exhibits capacities of 290.0, 218.9, 180.3, and 116.7 mAh g−1 at the corresponding current densities (Figure 3d). Even at the high current density of 30 A g−1, the cell can still deliver a capacity of 163.3 mAh g−1, indicating a preferable rate capability of PVO‐60 than that of the HVO. Figure 3e shows the galvanostatic discharge/charge profiles of the PVO‐60 electrode at various current densities. It should be noted that the discharge plateau is still visible at high current rate of 30 A g−1 with a slight decline of the platform voltage, revealing the intercalation of active Zn species.

Furthermore, when the current rate is stepwise reset back to 0.1 A g−1, the specific capacity of PVO‐60 restores to 95% of the initial capacity, indicating good layer structure stability and excellent electrochemical reversibility. For comparison, HVO displays poor capacity retention at the same current densities. The long cycling stability of the PVO‐60 electrode was evaluated at a current density of 10 A g−1 (Figure 3f). Remarkably, PVO‐60 manifests excellent long‐term cycle stability with negligible capacity decay at 10 A g −1 (capacity retention of 95.3%). Even at 20 A g−1, the reversible discharge capacity of PVO‐60 electrode still retained at 197.1 mAh g−1 after 2000 cycles, with a capacity retention of about 97.6% (Figure S4, Supporting Information). This result indicates that PANI intercalation well maintains the structural integrity of V2O5 during long‐term cycling. In contrast, much lower specific capacity was delivered for HVO electrodes. Especially at low current density (0.1 A g−1), PVO‐60 electrode displays much better cycling stability than HVO (Figure S5, Supporting Information). The same phenomenon has also been observed in the rate performance shown in Figure 3d. Generally, the reported V2O5 electrodes suffer from fast capacity fading due to irreversible structural transitions at low current density. It should be noted that the insertion amount of PANI could also significantly affect the structural integrity and interlayer distance of V2O5. With increasing the aniline concentration from 5 to 180 µL, the (001) diffraction peaks continuously shift to the lower angle, indicating gradually enlarged layer space (Figure S2 and Table S1, Supporting Information). Clearly, the V2O5 electrode with 60 µL aniline insertions shows better structural integrity than that of V2O5 electrodes with 5 µL aniline insertion (PVO‐5, shown in Figure S6, Supporting Information). The specific capacity of PVO‐5 electrode declines rapidly with cycling especially at low current density (0.2 A g−1) owing to the destruction of the layer structure and dissolution of vanadium. With increasing the amount of PANI, the V2O5 electrodes manifest enhanced rate performance owing to the improved electronic conductivity (discussed later) and larger interlayer distance (Figures S7 and S8, Supporting Information). Impressively, the PVO‐60 electrode exhibits high energy and high power densities as evidenced in the Ragone plot (Figure 3g). A remarkable energy density of 187 Wh kg−1 was delivered based on the mass of the PVO‐60 cathode at a power density of 7.2 kW kg−1, which is superior to most of the recently reported Zn‐ ion batteries (Table S2, Supporting Information). To gain insight into the Zn2+ storage behavior of the PVO‐60 cathode, the electrochemical kinetics was investigated by the CV method reported by Dunn and co‐ workers.[56] Figure 4a shows the typical CV curves of the PVO‐60 electrode at different scan rates from 0.4 to 1.0 mV s−1 between 0.2 and 1.6 V. All the measured CV curves show similar shapes with gradually increased the scan rate. It has been demonstrated that the currents (i ) of CV and the scan rates (ν) meet an empirical equation i = avb , where a and b are adjustable

Page 7

parameters.[57] The b values for the cathodic (b 1, b 2) and anodic (b 3, b 4) peaks were calculated to be 0.93, 0.79, 0.94, and 0.89, respectively (Figure 4b). These results indicate that the Zn2+ storage process of the PVO‐60 electrode is mainly controlled by the mixed mechanisms of capacitive response and diffusion process. In addition, the relative contributions of capacitive effect (k 1v ) and diffusion processes (k 2v 1/2) could be quantitatively determined by the following equation, i = k 1v + k 2v 1/2, where k 1 and k 2 are constants at particular sweep rates.[58] Figure 4c shows the fitted CV curve at a specific scan rate (0.8 mV s−1), in which the shadow area represents the capacitive contribution with a high value of 65.1%. With increasing the scan rate from 0.4 to 1 mV s−1, the capacitive contribution gradually increases from 56.3% to 67.8% accordingly (Figure 4d), indicating that the electrochemical behaviors of the PVO‐60 electrode is mainly dominated by the capacitive effect. The galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) was employed to probe the ion diffusion coefficient of Zn2+ in PVO‐60 (Figure 4e and Figure S9, Supporting Information) and the corresponding diffusion coefficients (D Zn2+) of Zn2+ for PVO‐60 and HVO upon Zn2+ insertion and extraction are presented in Figure 4f,g.[37] It can be observed that the D Zn2+ of PVO‐60 decreases slightly with the Zn2+ insertion/extraction and varies from 1.38 × 10−11 to 1.02 × 10−10 cm2 s−1 and 6.81 × 10−12 to 1.70 × 10−10 cm2 s−1 over the entire intercalation/deintercalation process, while the D Zn2+ of HVO electrode shows apparently decrease during the Zn2+ insertion. This result suggests that the PVO‐60 nanosheets endow fast Zn2+ migration, thus leading to good rate capability. The decent kinetics in PVO‐60 was further confirmed by electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements. As shown in Figure 4h and Figure S10 in the Supporting Information, the PVO‐60 displays much lower charge‐transfer resistance (R ct) than HVO before and after cycling (Table 1). It is worth noting that there is an obvious decrease in the R ct for the two electrodes after ten cycles, which could be ascribed to the activation of materials as the result of small amount of Zn2+ insertion. Moreover, Figure 4i displays the real part of impedance as a function of low frequencies (ω).[59] The much smaller slopes of linear relationship for PVO‐60 indicate a faster ionic diffusion in the electrochemical processes, which is agreement with the GITT results.[60] The lower charge‐ transfer resistance and facile Zn2+ diffusion in PANI‐intercalated V2O5 guarantee the fast electrochemical reaction kinetics. To further elucidate the Zn2+ storage mechanism of the PANI‐intercalated V2O5, in situ synchrotron XRD characterization, capable of tracing the transient states during battery operation, was carried out to identify the structural evolutions of the PVO‐60 electrode during charging–discharging process.[61] The (hkl ) peak shifts in the set of the in situ XRD patterns, based on the Bragg's law, could be tracked for extracting lattice parameters at various voltages, while the phase changes during Zn2+ (de‐)intercalation could be also fingerprinted by identifying the newly emerged or disappeared peaks in the XRD patterns. In this way, the reversibility of structural evolution corresponding to the reversible electrochemical redox reaction could be clearly revealed. Consistent with the ex situ XRD data, the in situ XRD peaks emerged at low

angle accord with the periodic (00l ) distances inserted with PANI, while the peaks at high angle could be assigned to the V–O coherent layers (Figure 5a). During the entire electrochemical process, the Bragg position of the (001) peak shows no obvious shift, but a new set of peaks appears from discharge state of 0.4 V to charge state of 0.9 V (Figure 5b). These peaks also accord with the (00l ) feature but emerged at a higher angle, which might be attributed to the rearrangement of the lamellar ordering upon deep Zn2+ intercalation. Based on the Bragg's law, the evolution of interlayer distance d is estimated to be almost constant with the intercalation and extraction of the Zn2+ (Figure 5c). This result indicates that the electrostatic interaction between Zn2+ and host O2− could be blocked by the insertion of PANI, which facilitates the Zn ions insertion/extraction due to the expedite interlayered channels and thereby benefits the structural stability upon cycling. On the other hand, the peaks of V–O layers emerged at high angle exhibit a reversible feature during the discharge–charge process, which provides evidence for a highly reversible redox reaction of vanadium ions (discussed later). In agreement with the in situ XRD observations, ex situ Raman scattering measurements further substantiate the high reversibility of the PVO‐60 electrode. As depicted in Figure 5d, the Raman spectrum of the PVO‐60 composite conforms well with the normal D2h point symmetry of V–O layers.[62] When discharging to 0.2 V, two Raman bands emerge at 250 and 950 cm−1, accompanied with silence of the D2h bands. Consistent with the in situ XRD results, such behavior could be attributed to the reordering of the V–O layers upon deep Zn2+ intercalation.[63] Remarkably, at the end of charge, the Raman spectrum reverses back to the original D2h features, confirming the reversible structural and electrochemical evolutions of the PVO‐60 composite. On the other hand, XPS measurements were carried out to reveal the reversible electrochemical reduction of V−O layers during cycling. As shown in Figure 5e,f, the peaks located at 516.3 and 517.6 eV can be ascribed to V4+ and V5+ species, respectively, revealing the partial reduction of the V2O5 framework owing to PANI intercalation.[64] Upon Zn2+ insertion, a new peak corresponding to V3+ species appears at 515.4 eV, accompanied with an increase of V4+ intensity and a loss of V5+ intensity. Conversely, V4+ and V3+ are oxidized back to V5+ in the fully charged state, showing a similar peak profile with that of the initial state.[65] In all, the enhanced cycling performance of the PVO‐60 electrode is twofold, the open and stable layered structure upon cycling, as well as the highly reversible redox reaction during charging–discharging process. This could be attributed to the insertion of PANI that enlarges the interlayer distance for facile and reversible Zn2+ diffusion. Even after times of charge/discharge cycles, the PVO‐60 electrode still maintains its layer structure and nanosheet‐like morphology, as shown in Figure 5g,h. The mappings of discharged PVO‐60 cathode (0.2 V) in Figure 5i show the uniform distribution of Zn element with the same distribution of V in the nanosheet, indicating the charge storage of zinc ion.

Density functional theory calculations were performed to uncover the intrinsic influence of PANI intercalation on the Zn‐ion dynamics process. The calculated total density of states (DOS) show that the Fermi level appears at the valence‐band maximum for pristine V2O5 (Figure 6a), while the intercalation of PANI causes the Fermi level moving toward the conduction band (Figure 6c). Notably, for the PANI‐V2O5 there is an intermediated level appearing across the Fermi level, offering a step that facilitates the electron transfer during charging/discharging process. In order to address the bonding behavior of Zn ions, differential charge density was calculated for both pristine (Figure 6b) and PANI‐V2O5 (Figure 6d) as a single Zn2+ is inserted into the frameworks. Substantial charge depletion and accumulation can be recognized around Zn and adjacent O atoms, respectively. The charge redistribution patterns are very similar between Zn and the four adjacent O atoms for both systems. Bader charge analysis indicates that Zn acts as an electron donor and becomes largely ionized (respectively losing 1.35 and 1.29 |e|).[66] Specifically, charge accumulation is also visible around the next‐nearest O atoms in bulk V2O5, explaining the stronger binding interaction of Zn and sluggish Zn2+ diffusion in the pristine V2O5. In the case of PANI‐V2O5, notable redistribution can be seen around the nearest C-H bond of PANI. The repulsion between positively charged Zn and H should be responsible for the weakened Zn binding strength. The binding energies (E b) for Zn ions intercalated within V2O5 layers are calculated as E b = (E Zn/sam – E sam – n × E Zn)/n , where n is the number of Zn ions, E Zn/sam and E sam are total energies of V2O5 samples with and without Zn2+ intercalation, respectively, and E Zn is the energy of a single Zn atom derived from its bulk state. The calculated binding energy for Zn ion inserted in PANI‐V2O5 is remarkably smaller than that in pristine V2O5 (1.67 vs 2.06 eV) (Figure 6f), suggesting that it is much easier for Zn ions (de)intercalation in PANI‐ V2O5. The blocked electrostatic interactions between Zn2+ and the V2O5 framework as a result of PANI intercalation could be responsible for the facile Zn2+ kinetics and therefore effectively improve the electrochemical performance of PANI‐intercalated V2O5. In summary, we report an in situ PANI‐intercalation strategy to optimize the kinetics of zinc ion extraction/insertion in V2O5. The intercalated PANI molecules serve as interlayer pillars that enlarge the Zn2+ diffusion channels, which ensures expedite Zn2+ diffusion channels for rapid electrochemical kinetics. Moreover, the unique π‐conjugated structure of PANI served simultaneously alleviates the electrostatic interactions between Zn2+ and oxygen‐sublattice, which significantly lowers the binding energy between Zn2+ and V2O5 host. Coupled with the conductive nature of PANI, promotions of both electronic and ionic transports as well as an unprecedented cyclic stability have been achieved in the present PANI‐intercalated V2O5. As most ZIBs suffer from instinct kinetic problems associated with Zn2+ (de)intercalation, our
