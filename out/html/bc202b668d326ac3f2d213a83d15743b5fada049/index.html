Abstract: Metal-air batteries have much higher theoretical energy density than lithium-ion batteries, and are frequently advocated as the solution toward next-generation electrochemical energy storage for applications including electric vehicles or grid energy storage. Yet they have not fulfilled their full potentials as limited by challenges associated with the metal anode, air cathode and electrolyte. These challenges would have to be properly resolved before metal-air batteries can become a practical reality and be deployed on a large scale. Here we survey the current status and latest advances in metal-air battery research for both aqueous (e.g. Zn-air) and non-aqueous (e.g. Li-air) systems. The general technical issues confronting their developments are overviewed, and our perspective on possible solutions is offered.

Keyword: Electrochemical Energy Storage; Zn-air; Li-air; Na-air; Lithium oxides

Electrochemical energy storage devices are essential components in the future energy network to buffer the unpredictable energy generation and supplies from renewable sources. Of the many available options, lithium-ion batteries play the most important role, and have deeply penetrated every corner of our life since its advent. In spite of their great success, there has been a call to continuously improve their energy and power density. This need becomes increasingly urgent in most recent years as driven by the demand to electrify transportation and promote grid-scale stationary energy storage.1,2 Unfortunately, conventional lithium-ion technology based on intercalation chemistry is approaching their performance limit. It is now generally believed that further improvements on Li-ion battery can bring in at most additional 30% increase in energy density.3 Such an upper limit means that it would be difficult to achieve long driving ranges (e.g. 500 km) when conventional lithium-ion batteries are used to power electrical vehicles (EVs). New chemistry and systems are therefore being actively sought with a targeted energy density of 500 Wh/kg and a cost of <$100/kWh. Among several potential candidates, metal-air batteries (which broadly include those running on oxygen or air feedstock) have gained revived interest. They are assembled from a metal anode and an air-breathing cathode in a proper electrolyte. The metal anode can be alkali metals (e.g. Li, Na and K), alkaline earth metals (e.g. Mg) or first-row transition metals (e.g. Fe and Zn) with good electrochemical equivalence; the electrolyte can be aqueous or non-aqueous – depending on the nature of the anode employed; the air-breathing cathode often has an open porous architecture that permits continuous oxygen supply from surrounding air. Metal-air batteries combine the design features of both conventional batteries and fuel cells. They have large theoretical energy density about 3~30 times higher than those of lithium-ion batteries (Figure 1). Research on metal-air batteries commenced much earlier than lithium-ion batteries. The first 2

Page 3

primary zinc-air battery was designed by Maiche dating back to 1878,4 and its commercial products started to enter market in 1932.5 Following that, aqueous iron-air, aluminum-air and magnesium-air batteries were developed in 1960s.6-8 Non-aqueous metal-air batteries first emerged about two decades ago, initially for Li-air, and more recently for Na-air and K-air.9-11 Despite their early beginning, the development of metal-air batteries has been hampered by problems associated with metal anodes, air catalysts and electrolytes. None of them at present are at a stage for large-scale industrial deployment. Their viability to replace lithium-ion batteries for future EV applications also remains unclear. In this Perspective, we do not intend to provide a comprehensive overview of metal-air batteries (excellent review articles on these topics can be found in recent literature12-21), but instead aim to highlight major technical hurdles that need to be overcome before meta-air technologies can become a practical reality. Since the electrochemistry differs considerably for aqueous and non-aqueous systems, we structure our discussion into the following connected parts.

Aqueous metal-air batteries Metals such as Zn, Fe, Al and Mg are thermodynamically unstable in the aqueous medium, but their surfaces can be passivated by corresponding oxides or hydroxides under certain circumstances, and thus are compatible with aqueous electrolytes to some extent. These metals have been investigated for aqueous metal-air batteries. Above electrochemical reactions may be reversed upon recharge with metals plated at the anode and O2 evolving at the cathode. In many regards, aqueous metal-air batteries are considered as a special type of fuel cell running on metal fuels. The oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) taking place at the air cathode is similar to the ORR in hydrogen fuel cells. As a result, these two electrochemical systems share several common design principles and criteria. Of the four listed types of aqueous metal-air batteries, Zn-air so far has received the most favorable consideration. In what follows, we will focus on this particular system as an example to illustrate some general challenges confronting the development of aqueous metal-air batteries and their possible solutions. Zn-air has a theoretical energy density of 1353 Wh/kg (excluding oxygen), a theoretical working voltage of 1.65 V and potentially very low fabrication cost. However, the practically attainable energy density of Zn-air is usually between 350-500 Wh/kg. Their commercial products have inadequate rate capability as limited by the inefficiency of air catalysts, and are mostly intended for low power applications such as miniature hearing aids. Furthermore, rechargeable Zn-air batteries suffer from poor energy efficiency of <60% caused by the substantial overpotentials at the air cathode. The cycle life of rechargeable batteries is also far from satisfactory due to the lack of stable bifunctional electrocatalysts and cycleable metal anode. It further aggravates when batteries are deeply cycled under large currents. Solutions to these problems lie at the rational design and engineering of both cathode and anode materials. Optimization of the air catalyst and cathode architecture plays the decisive role to improving battery performance. Air cathode is the performance-limiting electrode in metal-air batteries because ORR is a highly sluggish reaction. At the air cathode, this reaction mainly takes place at the triple4

Page 5

phase boundary where the solid electrode is simultaneously interfaced with liquid electrolyte and gaseous O2. As a consequence, developing active air catalysts to expedite the ORR kinetics and designing proper electrode architecture to enlarge the triple-phase boundary would greatly benefit the battery discharge performance. Fortunately, existing knowledge accumulated for alkaline fuel cells can be readily transplanted to aid the research of Zn-air batteries. Air catalysts proven successful for the former are also promising candidates for the latter. Over the past several decades, a large variety of materials, ranging from precious metals (e.g. Pt and Ag), to non-precious metal oxides (e.g. MnOx and CoOx) or even metal-free carbonaceous materials (e.g. nitrogen-doped carbon nanotubes or graphene), have been investigated as the air catalyst.15-17 It is worth noting that in the concentrated alkaline electrolyte (e.g. 30 wt% KOH) typically used for Zn-air batteries, the ORR activity of Pt — which commonly serves the benchmark material — is somehow suppressed due to its high surface coverage of OH adspecies,22 and surpassed by many non-precious metal based materials. For example, we demonstrated that CoO nanocrystals selectively grown on N-doped carbon nanotubes outperformed 20 wt% Pt/C at the same catalyst areal loading in 6 M KOH (Figure 3).23 A sufficiently large pool of potential ORR catalyst materials now is available to provide the necessary material basis for primary Zn-air batteries. As for electrically rechargeable metal-air batteries, the air cathode should be able to efficiently catalyze both ORR and its reverse reaction — oxygen evolution reaction (OER). Many researchers therefore emphasize the importance of developing bifunctional oxygen electrocatalysts. Despite the much narrower material selection, a dozen of high-performance bifunctional electrocatalysts have been successfully developed in just the last decade.17,21,24-26 Alternatively, the bifunctionality of the air cathode can also be achieved via the proper combination of multiple functional components.23 This 5

Page 6

second approach allows for more flexibility in the air cathode design since researchers can individually optimize ORR and OER catalysts, and then combine them together. Nevertheless, it should be pointed out that even with the combination of best available ORR and OER catalysts, the polarization of air cathode between charge and discharge is still so large that the round-trip energy efficiency of rechargeable Zn-air batteries would unlikely go beyond 65% at current densities pertinent to practical applications. It presents a serious drawback of metal-air batteries in comparison with conventional lithium-ion technology (>90%). Another problem with the air cathode of rechargeable metal-air batteries is the poor cycling stability. Even though individual ORR and OER catalysts can be made sufficiently durable under respective operation condition, many of them had low tolerance toward alternating reductive-oxidative environments during discharge-charge cycles. The cycling is especially detrimental to the ORR active component. Increasing evidence has been revealed that upon battery recharge, the harsh oxidative potential can cause irreversible damage to ORR active sites at the cathode as well as the electrochemical corrosion of the carbon support.17,23,27 As far as we are aware, there are very few bifunctional air cathodes with both required high activity and cycling stability. One possibility to completely circumvent this issue is to adopt a bi-cathode configuration.17,23,26,27 In this way, ORR and OER catalysts are physically decoupled, and loaded on two separate cathodes for discharge and charge, respectively. Much improved cycling performance has been achieved with such a design, of course, at the unavoidable price of bigger battery size and lower energy density. Furthermore, the metallic anode needs to be specifically engineered so as to suppress its corrosion and non-uniform dissolution/deposition. Without properly resolving this issue, the long-term cycling stability of rechargeable Zn-air batteries would not be achieved, but so far it is relatively overlooked. 6

Page 7

Common strategies to tackle this problem is to alloy Zn with other metals (e.g. Pb, Cd, Bi and In) or introduce additives (e.g. metal oxides, organic acids, silicates, surfactants and polymers).17,21,28-30 They can, to some extent, stabilize the Zn anode, slow down its parasitic hydrogen gas generation, reduce the solubility of its discharge product, and suppress dendritic Zn deposits at charge. But none of them represent an ultimate remedy. An alternative approach to this long-standing issue is to use mechanical recharging (where Zn fuel and electrolyte are periodically replenished), or to adapt the design of flow batteries (where an electrolyte suspension of Zn particles circulates in and out the battery). Mechanically rechargeable Zn-air batteries or Zn-air flow batteries operate only in the discharge mode, therefore are also not restrained by the availability of stable bifunctional air cathode. Spent materials collected can be regenerated outside the battery. In 1990s, there were serious efforts in US and Europe to develop mechanically rechargeable zinc-air batteries for EV applications.17,31 Zn-air flow batteries are now being actively pursued by ZincNyx Energy Solutions, Inc. In addition to Zn-air, other aqueous metal-air batteries are confronted with similar challenges. Feair batteries are also electrically rechargeable, but their practical energy density is much lower — typically in the range of 60-80 Wh/kg — barely comparable to current lithium-ion technology.14 They are suitable for stationary energy storage due to their long cycle life (>1000 cycles), low-cost (<$100/kWh) and environmental benignity, but obviously out of consideration for pure EV applications. Al-air and Mg-air batteries have significant theoretical energy density, but more than often only a very small fraction can be utilized due to the severe parasitic corrosion of their metallic anodes in contact with the aqueous electrolyte.13,32 They can only be mechanically recharged. Non-aqueous metal-air batteries Metals such as Li, Na and K are too reactive in aqueous solution. Only when they are intimately protected with an ionic conductive film (e.g. NASICON-type glass 7

Page 8

ceramic, which permits the ion transport between the metal anode and aqueous electrolyte) can their corresponding metal-air batteries operate in aqueous electrolytes.18,19 Such protected aqueous batteries are not our focus in this part because their fabrication process is too complicated and fabrication cost is too high. Most metal-air batteries of Li, Na and K use aprotic electrolytes. Importantly, ORR in aprotic electrolytes proceeds through a mechanism drastically different from that in aqueous electrolytes (see Figure 2b and the following equations). It is now generally accepted that the reaction involves an initial one-electron reduction of O2 on the catalyst surface to form a superoxide anion O2-, which then reacts with alkali metal cation to form peroxide MO2. As for Li+, its small size is unable to stabilize O2-. LiO2 subsequently disproportionates to form peroxide Li2O2 as the discharge product.19,3335

Larger cations (Na+ and K+) can more effectively stabilize the superoxide anion according to hard-

soft acid-base theory.36 Accordingly, the discharge product of Na-air batteries is usually a mixture of Na2O2 and NaO2,10,20 and that of K-O2 batteries is predominantly KO2.11 These superoxides or peroxides have limited solubility in the electrolyte, and are deposited on the air cathode. Their accumulation results in the gradual blockage of the available cathode surface area, and eventually shuts off the battery. As a result, the discharge capacity of non-aqueous metal-air batteries is determined by the storage capacity of the air cathode toward the discharge product, and is far smaller than the theoretical value. Compared to aqueous metal-air batteries, non-aqueous metal-air batteries are still in their infancy. They suffer from even more serious challenges, which will be exemplified using the Li-air system in the 8

Page 9

following discussion. First, suitable electrolytes for non-aqueous metal-air batteries are yet to be identified. This is because the superoxide or peroxide product is oxidative and would cause the decomposition of most common electrolytes used in conventional lithium-ion batteries.18,19 When Abraham and coworker introduced rechargeable Li-air batteries in 1996, they used carbonate-based gel electrolyte.9 However, this Li-air prototype failed within just a few cycles. It is now agreed based on both theoretical and experimental findings that organic carbonates are highly susceptible to the nucleophilic attack by superoxide or peroxide species. For example, evidence was shown that reaction between superoxide/peroxide and propylene carbonate led to the ring opening of the heterocycle and the subsequent formation of lithium alkyl carbonates, Li2CO3, CO2 and H2O instead of Li2O2 as the discharge products at the cathode.37 The charging capacity observed in early Li-air batteries using carbonate-based electrolytes was therefore mainly contributed from the electrolyte decomposition, not the reversible oxidation of Li2O2. At present, research efforts are focused on ether-based solvents as the electrolyte, with the most popular choice being tetraethylene glycol dimethyl ether (TEGDME) or dimethyoxyethane (DME) thanks to their poor electrophilic characteristics.18,19,38 Other electrolytes of interest include dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and acetonitrile.39 Nevertheless, even these electrolytes are insufficiently stable, and their decomposition occurs during battery cycling.40,41 The search for better electrolytes continues. It is essential to the eventual realization of the long-term cycling stability (>1000 cycles) of any non-aqueous metal-air batteries. Second, to improve battery discharge performance would require the integral optimization of the electrolyte/cathode couple. It is suggested that the solvent donor number (DN) greatly impacts its solubility toward the reaction intermediate (LiO2), and hence the discharge characteristic of Li-air 9

Page 10

batteries.34,42 In high-DN solvents, LiO2 has greater solubility in the electrolyte; its subsequent solution-mediated disproportionation leads to the precipitation of large Li2O2 particles on the cathode. In low-DN solvents, LiO2 readily adsorbs on the electrode; its surface-mediated disproportionation instead results in Li2O2 thin films with thickness up to 5~6 nm. It is therefore not surprising that batteries using high-DN solvents generally have specific capacities >3 times greater than those using low-DN solvents.34 One can envisage that the optimized cathode structure for the solution growth of Li2O2 should have large pore volume to accommodate as many Li2O2 deposits as possible, and that for the surface growth of Li2O2 should have high surface area commensurate with pores of sufficient size to permit mass transport within the pores. When the pore size is too small, the pore entrance would be easily blocked, rendering the electrode inner surface area inaccessible and causing the premature discharge termination. Third, reversible Li2O2 decomposition would need to be achieved at a lower overpotential (<3.5 V) so as to promote the battery energy efficiency and cycle life. Most Li-air batteries discharge well even using commercially available carbon black as the ORR catalyst.43 The real challenge comes from recharging. A pronounced overpotential (>1 V) is commonly required in order to reverse the reaction and electrochemically decompose Li2O2 at the cathode.18,19 This not only lowers the energy efficiency of Li-air batteries to <75%, but, more importantly, also may cause the electrolyte or electrode degradation, and hence very poor battery cycle life. The majority of current Li-air cathodes use carbon as the catalyst or catalyst support. Its insufficient electrochemical stability is a general concern. Carbon would get oxidized at such positive potentials, leading to the accumulation of Li2CO3 on the cathode surface, which passivates the electrode and is extremely difficult to decompose.44,45 To avoid this problem, Bruce and coworkers used carbon-free nanoporous gold as the catalyst together with a DMSO 10

Page 11

electrolyte for Li-air batteries.39 They observed that the battery capacity was dominated by Li2O2 formation and decomposition, and achieved marked capacity retention of 95% after 100 cycles. This improvement was exciting, but gold obviously is not suited for any practical application. Moreover, efforts are also devoted to developing catalysts to facilitate the reversible Li2O2 decomposition. A number of materials including Pt, Co3O4, MnO2 and RuO2 have been investigated, and some of them are proven to be effective.18,19,46 The use of redox mediators may also be beneficial. Soluble mediators can better access Li2O2, and mediate the electron transfer to accelerate its dissociation at lower potentials.47,48 The disadvantage, however, is the parasitic shuttle effect between anode and cathode. At last, the rechargeability of Li-air batteries might also be dramatically improved if its reaction pathway could be altered. Even though Li2O2 is the thermodynamically stable discharge product, we recently demonstrated that crystalline LiO2 was selectively formed as the discharge product by using an iridium-graphene hybrid catalyst (Figure 4).49 LiO2 could be more facilely decomposed during recharge due to its half metallic nature and the catalytic effect of Ir. As a result, the battery was repeatedly charged and discharged with a very low charge potential (< 3.5 V) and great cycle life. In addition to aforementioned challenges, there are several other issues such as anode stabilization, O2 crossover suppression and CO2 management that need to be carefully addressed in order to make Li-air technology a practical reality. All these problems in fact are not specific to Li-air, but general to other non-aqueous metal-air batteries including Na-air and K-air, which so far have not received as much research attention. Compared to Li-air, the lower charging overpotentials of Na-air and K-air batteries are obviously attractive (because their discharge products are mainly superoxides), but they suffer from even more problematic cycle life.10,11 It remains unclear at this moment whether or not their superoxide electrochemistry would be a unique promise and advantage over Li-air. 11

For future electrochemical energy storage, it is desirable that the battery of choice should have an energy density in excess of 500 Wh/kg and a cycle life over 1000 cycles at a cost lower than $100/kWh. With an energy density of 500 Wh/kg, a battery pack including necessary ancillaries weighed no more than 300 kg would provide sufficient energy to drive EVs for at least 500 km. IBM in 2009 first launched the “Battery 500” project, ambitiously aiming to develop Li-air batteries that could ensure a 500 mile (800 km) driving range. But it becomes increasingly transparent that the subject is significantly more complex than initially thought. At present, no metal-air technologies － aqueous or non-aqueous － are able to meet the above set target despite their great potentials. In our opinion, aqueous metal-air batteries, particularly Zn-air batteries, might be a short-term solution, and are closer to replace conventional Li-ion batteries if given proper policy support. Current primary Zn-air batteries on the consumer market already have an energy density approaching 500 Wh/kg. Driven by the recent development of better ORR catalysts and the optimization of cathode architecture, we can confidently foresee that their energy density and power density will be further enhanced in the near future. Comparatively speaking, non-aqueous metal-air batteries with their large theoretical energy density may present a long-term solution for large-scale electrochemical energy storage if all of their current technical challenges can be effectively solved. However, based on their development trajectory, it seems to us that even rechargeable non-aqueous metal-air batteries are eventually realized, they might not be suited for high-power applications (such as EVs) because the ORR rate in aprotic solvents is orders of magnitude slower than in aqueous electrolytes. This deficiency can be complemented by other high-power batteries or supercapacitors. One notable new direction for non-aqueous metal-air batteries is the development of their close systems. Instead of the gaseous O2 ↔ solid superoxide/peroxide phase transformation that involves 12

Page 13

large structural reorganization and hence huge energy barrier, batteries based on the more rapid solid superoxide ↔ peroxide ↔ oxide phase transformation (because of their similar atomistic environments) have recently emerged. They are an exciting extension of existing metal-air technologies in that only “solid oxygen” is involved without the participation of external gaseous oxygen, and therefore can be made closed. Using the interconversion among Li2O ↔ Li2O2 ↔ LiO2 as an example, this approach was demonstrated to effectively breach the kinetic and stability bottleneck of non-aqueous metal-air batteries: remarkable cycling performance with only 1.8% after 130 cycles as well as small round-trip overpotential of only 0.24 V was reported (Figure 5).50 Due to the lack of gaseous oxygen, these batteries were found fully compatible with low-cost carbonate electrolytes. A shuttling mechanism of self-generated radical species soluble in the carbonate electrolyte was further observed to automatically protect from O2 gas release and overcharging. Moreover, using the close system circumvents the moisture and CO2 management issue that have been long troubling conventional nonaqueous metal-air batteries. We anticipate more activities along this direction to develop the sodium and potassium versions in years to come. The development of metal-air batteries is both of fundamental and practical importance. Despite their current unsatisfactory performance, there is no reason to doubt about their tremendous potentials and bright future.
