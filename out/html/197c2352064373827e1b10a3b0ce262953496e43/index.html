The water–gas-shift (WGS) reaction is an industrially important source of pure hydrogen (H2) at the expense of carbon monoxide and water1,2. This reaction is of interest for fuel-cell applications, but requires WGS catalysts that are durable and highly active at low temperatures3. Here we demonstrate that the structure (Pt1–Ptn)/α-MoC, where single isolated platinum atoms (Pt1) and subnanometre platinum clusters (Ptn) are supported on α-molybdenum carbide (α-MoC), catalyses the WGS reaction even at 313 kelvin, with a hydrogen-production pathway involving direct carbon monoxide dissociation identified. We find that it is critical to crowd the support surface with Pt1 and Ptn species, which prevents oxidation of the support that would cause catalyst deactivation, as seen with gold/α-MoC (ref. 4), and gives our system high stability and a high metal-normalized turnover number of 4,300,000 moles of hydrogen per mole of platinum.

The size of active metal species can strongly affect a catalyst’s activity. In the case of the water–gas shift (WGS) reaction, some studies have suggested that non-metallic gold (Au) and platinum (Pt) single sites promote the reaction in the low-temperature range of 400–573 K (refs. 1,5–8); others9 have suggested that the active sites of Pt/silicon dioxide (SiO2) and Pt-sodium (Na)/SiO2 catalysts are Pt particles rather than single Pt atoms; and recent work has suggested that atomic-layered Au clusters on an α-molybdenum carbide (α-MoC) support are highly active4. But irrespective of remaining debates about the metal species dimension that optimizes WGS activity, there is general agreement that maximizing the density of the metal/support interface increases catalytic activity10–12. However, this can prove challenging to maintain and thereby limit catalyst stability. For example, the metal atoms of single-site catalysts tend to agglomerate on the support when running the reaction at the necessary temperatures13–16. In the case of the supported layered Au cluster system4, stability issues arise from oxidation of the support that deactivates the catalyst when using it above 473 K. It remains a grand challenge to construct a catalyst that can achieve a high loading of surface atomic species of noble metals with high mass-specific activity and the strong anti-oxidation capability for preventing deactivation during the WGS reaction. Herein we have developed a method to create abundant surface sites for anchoring Pt atomic species over an α-MoC surface, producing a

robust, efficient and stable (Pt1–Ptn)/α-MoC catalyst for the WGS reaction, where both isolated Pt atoms (Pt1) and subnanometre platinum cluster (Ptn) species co-exist in a high density. We demonstrate that the Pt1 species have a higher intrinsic WGS activity, one order of magnitude higher than the best catalyst reported so far4,7,11,17,18, while the formation of crowding Pt1 and Ptn surface species over α-MoC is critical for a high mass-specific activity. The atomic Pt species can effectively prompt the turnover of oxygen species on the adjacent α-MoC surface with carbon monoxide (CO) adsorbed on Pt, which keeps the α-MoC substrate from deep oxidation and deactivation. As a result, the (Pt1–Ptn)/α-MoC catalyst shows an ultrahigh turnover number of 4,300,000 mol hydrogen (H2) per mol Pt, one order of magnitude higher than the Au/α-MoC catalysts4. A series of Pt/α-MoC catalysts with 0.02–8 wt% Pt loading was prepared using a modified incipient wetness impregnation method (Methods)19. As shown in Fig. 1a, except for the 8 wt% Pt/α-MoC sample where weak and broad diffraction peaks of Pt were resolved (associated with nanometre-size Pt particles), no X-ray diffraction (XRD) peaks associated with Pt were observed in the samples, indicating that the Pt species were highly dispersed on α-MoC for Pt loadings as high as 5 wt% (Supplementary Fig. 1). The high-resolution STEM Z-contrast images (Z is the atomic number) of 0.2 wt% Pt/α-MoC (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Fig. 2) show that all the Pt species are atomically dispersed on the α-MoC support, with a surface density of about 9 Pt1 per 100 nm2 (Fig. 1b). With increasing Pt loading, the density of surface Pt species increases and their structures also evolve (Fig. 1d, e, Supplementary Figs. 3–7). Notably, the density of Pt1 species increases to around 42 Pt1 per 100 nm2 and 80 Pt1 per 100 nm2 for 2 wt% and 8 wt% Pt loadings, respectively (Fig. 1b). Furthermore, a small amount of subnanometre Pt clusters (Ptn) emerges at 2 wt% Pt loading (Fig. 1d, Supplementary Figs. 3, 4). When the Pt loading further increases to 8 wt% (Fig. 1e, Supplementary Figs. 5, 6), nanometre-size Pt particles are observed in addition to the high-density Ptn clusters and Pt1 species, as also detected by XRD (Fig. 1a). The better dispersion of the current Pt/α-MoC catalysts compared with our previous reports19 is attributed to the creation of more surface sites for anchoring the low-dimensional Pt species by the improved preparation method. The appearance of Pt clusters and nanoparticles with increasing Pt loading is in good agreement with the Pt L3-edge extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) analysis, where the averaged Pt–Pt coordination number increases from 0 to 3.8 to 5.6 for Pt loadings of 0.02 wt%, 2 wt% and 8 wt%, respectively (Fig. 1h, Supplementary Fig. 8, Supplementary Table 1). On the basis of the structural analysis results (Supplementary Figs. 2–9), we denoted the 0.02–0.2 wt%, 2 wt% and 8 wt% Pt/α-MoC

catalysts as Pt1/α-MoC, (Pt1–Ptn)/α-MoC and (Pt1–Ptp)/α-MoC, respectively, where Pt1, Ptn and Ptp stand for atomically dispersed Pt1 species, subnanometre Pt clusters and nanometre-size Pt particles, respectively. We evaluated the WGS performance of the Pt/α-MoC catalysts under product-free and full reformate gas feed. As shown in Fig. 1g, the pure α-MoC and commercial copper/zinc oxide/aluminium oxide (Cu/ZnO/ Al2O3) catalysts are both inactive for the WGS reaction below 373 K. However, considerable CO conversion was observed over 0.2 wt% Pt1/α-MoC in the 313 K to 373 K range. The CO conversion increased drastically when the Pt loading was increased to 2 wt%, showing an exceptional CO conversion of 22% at a low reaction temperature of 313 K and almost full conversion at 373 K. Importantly, CO conversion of 90–100% can be achieved over a wide reaction-temperature window, from 393 K to 673 K, under high space velocity (product-free gas feed, gas hourly space velocity 180,000 h−1) using this 2 wt% (Pt1–Ptn)/α-MoC catalyst. This is in sharp contrast to the 2% Au/α-MoC catalyst where the CO conversion declines drastically when the temperature goes above 523 K (ref. 4). The results show that the 2 wt% (Pt1–Ptn)/α-MoC catalyst is more stable than the Au catalysts over a wide temperature window. We measured the mole-specific activity (mol H2 per mol Pt per second) of the catalysts in the kinetic region under full reformate gas feed (393–523 K) and product-free gas feed (313–353 K) to compare their intrinsic activity. WGS catalysts in the literature, such as Ir1/FeOx (ref. 20), Au1/CeO2 (refs. 6,21), Au1-Na/mesoporous silica (MCM-41) (ref. 7), Au1-K/KLTL-zeolite (ref. 7) and Au/α-MoC (ref. 4) catalysts, our Pt/α-MoC catalysts have even higher activity and a wider reaction temperature window (Figs. 1h, 2, Supplementary Table 2). Specifically, the 0.2 wt% Pt1/α-MoC catalyst shows a remarkably high Pt-normalized activity of 0.11, 2.2, 4.7, 15.5 and 21.2 mol H2 per mol Pt per second at 313, 393, 423, 473 and 523 K, respectively, much higher than any of the catalysts reported so far. The mole-specific activity of the 0.02 wt% Pt1/α-MoC catalyst was similar to that of the 0.2 wt% Pt1/α-MoC (Fig. 1h), indicating that they have similar active site constructs, both with Pt1 species supported

over α-MoC. In comparison, the 2 wt% (Pt1–Ptn)/α-MoC and 8 wt% (Pt1–Ptp)/α-MoC catalysts exhibited a considerable decline in activity, due to the formation of Ptn clusters and Ptp particles. While Pt1 and Ptn or Ptp species all contribute to the overall activity, the Pt atoms in the interior of the clusters and particles are not accessible to reactant molecules and cannot directly participate in the catalytic process. Figure 1h shows that the mole-specific activity drops with the increasing Pt–Pt coordination number at higher Pt loading. Nevertheless, the intrinsic activity of the Pt1/α-MoC and (Pt1–Ptn)/α-MoC catalysts still far exceeds that of any of the catalysts in the literature (Supplementary Table 2). In fact, to the best of our knowledge, these are the only catalysts reported so far that meet the WGS catalyst 2004 technical targets set by the US Department of Energy for transportation fuel cells3 (see Fig. 2 and the associated calculation of WGS catalyst targets in the Methods). Mass-specific activity is another important measure of the catalyst performance, as it reflects the total power for a catalyst to convert the feeds. Apart from the excellent intrinsic activity, our 2 wt% (Pt1–Ptn)/α-MoC catalyst has a high mass-specific activity that surpasses that of other catalysts (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table 2). To better understand the outstanding catalytic efficiency of our Pt/α-MoC catalysts, we conducted near-ambient-pressure (NAP) X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) experiments (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Figs. 11, 12). After activation, the 2 wt% (Pt1–Ptn)/α-MoC catalyst shows characteristic C and Mo peaks from α-MoC22. A peak at 530.3 eV is observed in the O 1s XPS, which is ascribed to the residual oxygen on the surface of α-MoC23. After dosing 5 mbar water into the catalyst at room temperature, besides gaseous water (534.8 eV)24 and residual oxygen23, a new peak at 532.3 eV is clearly resolved (Fig. 3a), which arises from OH species on the surface of α-MoC25. Parallel water treatment experiments via transient kinetic analysis (TKA) confirmed the hydrogen production (Supplementary Figs. 13, 14). The results suggest that the 2 wt% (Pt1–Ptn)/α-MoC can dissociate water at room temperature, generating H2 and surface OH species on α-MoC (Fig. 3d, route 1; the asterisk represents the sites on α-MoC for water adsorption/dissociation)4,19,26:

After the formation of surface OH species at room temperature, gas-phase water was pumped out and CO was introduced into the system at various temperatures. With the introduction of 5 mbar CO at 303 K, two new peaks at 537.6 eV (gas-phase CO) and 533.8 eV (absorbed CO) were resolved. The intensity of surface OH species decreased substantially, starting from 323 K (Fig. 3a). Meanwhile, parallel TKA experiments showed that carbon dioxide (CO2) and H2 were produced at 323 K after dosing CO into a surface pre-adsorbed with water (Supplementary Fig. 15a, b). This shows that the reaction between CO adsorbed on Pt species and surface OH species can readily occur at 323 K, producing CO2 and H2. However, even after reaction with CO at 373 K, there is still a substantial amount of OH species on α-MoC, which can hardly be further reacted even at 423 K (Fig. 3a). This demonstrates that there are two types of OH species on the α-MoC surface: those close to Pt species with adsorbed CO and those remote from Pt sites. The OH adjacent to Pt could be eliminated by CO adsorbed on Pt species, which recovers the surface site for further water dissociation, while OH species far from the Pt species may remain as spectators during the reaction. TKA experiments using 13C16O and H218O as feeds were performed to differentiate oxygen and carbon from different sources (H2O versus CO, and CO versus α-MoC). After the activated catalyst reached 423 K in argon (Ar), the feed was switched to a mixture of 13C16O and H218O. Owing to the difference in diffusion, 13C16O arrived at the catalyst surface at 100 s and its consumption formed a Gaussian peak before 125 s (regime I in Fig. 3b), while water arrived at the catalyst with a 25-s delay (Fig. 3b, regime II). Notably, the turnover number reached an ultrahigh value of 4,300,000 mol H2 per mol Pt in 263 h (Supplementary Table 3). As discussed earlier, oxygen species adsorbed on the surface of α-MoC can readily react with CO adsorbed on adjacent Pt sites, recovering surface sites for further turnover, while OH species far from surface Pt species will not participate in the reaction and could cause deep oxidation of the α-MoC surface and deactivation. Therefore, crowding surface Pt species, including the Pt1 atoms and Ptn clusters, can help to eliminate excess surface oxygen species, and is the key for the extraordinary stability we observed in the 2 wt% (Pt1–Ptn)/α-MoC catalyst. This argument is further supported by the in situ XPS and XRD data shown in Fig. 4b–d. Whereas the bulk α-MoC structure was maintained in both catalysts after reaction (Fig. 4c, d), the surface of the 0.02 wt% catalyst with a very low density of Pt1 species was mostly oxidized (Fig. 4b). In contrast, the surface of 2 wt% (Pt1–Ptn)/α-MoC catalyst remained unoxidized after 8 h of reaction, and the surface Pt species were well retained even after 263 h in the WGS reaction (Fig. 1f). These results demonstrate that the presence of crowding surface Pt species is indeed pivotal for improving the catalyst stability.

Methods Preparation of catalysts Synthesis of α-MoC. Ammonium molybdate tetrahydrate (AMT, (NH4)6Mo7O24·4H2O) was purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent. The grinded powder of AMT was calcined in the muffle oven to 773 K at a rate of 10 K min−1 to synthesize the precursor MoO3. Typically, 1 g MoO3 was loaded into the tubular quartz reactor and placed in a vertical furnace. The powder was heated to 973 K in ammonia (160 ml min−1; pre-purified gas) at a rate of 5 K min−1 and kept at 973 K for 2 h. After the sample was cooled to room temperature, the gas was switched to a methane (CH4)/H2 mixture (150 ml min−1; 20/80 v/v) and the temperature was increased to 973 K at 5 K min−1 and held at 973 K for 2 h. Finally, the sample was passivated at room temperature using a 1% oxygen/Ar gas mixture. Synthesis of Pt/α-MoC catalysts. Pt was deposited onto α-MoC (100 m2 g−1) using an incipient wetness impregnation (IWI) method. Before the loading of Pt precursor, the α-MoC substrate was reduced in 10% H2/nitrogen (N2) (20 ml min−1) at 523 K for 60 min, which is expected to create more surface anchoring sites for Pt species compared with our previous reported method19. Appropriate amounts of H2PtCl6·6H2O (Sigma-Aldrich) were dissolved in 200 μl deaerated deionized water. The Pt precursor solution was finely mixed with 50 mg α-MoC powder, followed by drying in a vacuum oven at 323 K. Before catalytic evaluation, the samples were mildly reduced at 623 K for 1 h under H2 flow at a flow rate of 60 ml min−1 first. Then the stream was switched to 15% CH4/H2 (100 ml min−1), and the temperature was increased at 5 K min−1 to 863 K and then held at this temperature for 2 h to reactivate the samples. The samples were then cooled to room temperature in 15% CH4/H2. Cu/ZnO/Al2O3 catalyst. The Cu/ZnO/Al2O3 catalyst (HiFUEL W220) was purchased from Alfa Aesar. Before testing, the sample was treated at 523 K in 20% H2/Ar (100 ml min−1) for 2 h.

Evaluation of catalytic performance The catalytic performance was tested in a continuous flow fixedbed quartz reactor at atmospheric pressure. The reactor was a 4-mminternal-diameter quartz tube in which the catalyst (40–60 mesh) was sandwiched between quartz wool layers in the tube reactor. Before performing the experiment, the Pt/α-MoC catalysts were pre-treated in 15% CH4/H2 at 100 ml min−1 at 863 K for 2 h (heating rate: 10 K min−1). Following the pre-treatment, the catalysts were exposed to the reformate gas (11% CO, 26% H2O, 7% CO2 and 26% H2 balanced by N2 for the full reformate gas; or 10.5% CO, 21% H2O and 20% N2 balanced by Ar for the product-free reformate gas; unless otherwise specified). Water was fed to the system using a high-performance liquid chromatography pump and mixed with the dry gas stream in a vaporizer maintained at 313 K (for low-temperature reaction below 393 K) or 393 K. For reaction below 393 K, the reaction gas feeds were 3% CO, 6% H2O and 20% N2 balanced by Ar for the product-free reformate gas or 3% CO, 6% H2O, 2% CO2 and 10% H2 balanced by N2 for the full reformate gas. A small air condenser was incorporated before the reactor to prevent overheating of the catalyst. The reactor effluent gas was passed through an ice-bath condenser to remove most of the H2O, and the gas composition was analysed by gas chromatography (GC7890II, Tianmei) with a TDX-01 column and thermal conductivity detector. The composition of the outlet gas was determined using an N2 internal standard method. The coefficients of CO, CO2 and CH4 were determined using standard samples.
