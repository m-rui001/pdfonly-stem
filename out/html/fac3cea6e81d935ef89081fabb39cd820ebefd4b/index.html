Abstract Perovskite solar cells have made tremendous progress using laboratory-scale spin-coating methods in the past few years owing to advances in controls of perovskite film deposition. However, devices made via scalable methods are still lagging behind state-of-the-art spin-coated devices because of complicated nature of perovskite crystallization from a precursor state. Here we demonstrate a chlorine-containing methylammonium lead iodide precursor formulation along with solvent tuning to enable wide precursor processing window (up to ~8 min) and rapid grain growth rate (as short as ~1 min). Coupled with antisolvent extraction, this precursor ink delivers high-quality perovskite films with large-scale uniformity. The ink can be used by both spincoating and blade-coating methods with indistinguishable film morphology and device performance. Using blade-coated absorber, devices with 0.12-cm2 and 1.2-cm2 areas yield average efficiencies of 18.55% and 17.33%, respectively. We further demonstrate a 12.6-cm2 four-cell module (88% geometric fill factor) with 13.3% stabilized active-area efficiency output.

Introduction Perovskite solar cell (PSC) technology has made stunning progress in performance in the last several years,1-8 making it a potentially transformational technology and disruptive addition to the emerging photovoltaic (PV) market. The rapid improvement in PSC efficiency is largely a result of developments in solution-chemistry processing of high-quality perovskite absorbers. In general, solution deposition of perovskites includes coating a wet precursor film on the substrate, evaporating/drying the solvent, and forming perovskite or an intermediate solid film, which are often followed by thermal annealing to fully crystallize the perovskite film.9 Perovskite nucleation, growth, and coarsening often occur with significant overlap in one or several of these steps, which complicates the perovskite formation process.10 Most academic groups have focused on using spin coating, because it requires small amounts of liquid (although it is wasteful); can easily be done in a controlled environment; and is easy to use. Unfortunately, the spin-coating method cannot be scaled for the high throughput and large substrate sizes desired for module production. Toward future module production, several scalable techniques have been reported for perovskite deposition,11,12 such as blade coating,13-15 slot-die coating,16 spray deposition,17 inkjet printing,18,19 and electrodeposition.20,21 However, these scalable methods have generally produced devices with lower efficiencies than spin-coated devices. Blade coating is attractive because it can easily be scaled up and translated to slot-die coating for sheet-to-sheet or roll-to-roll fabrication. Different approaches have been adopted in blade coating, such as onestep deposition (mostly mixture of PbCl2 and CH3NH3I or MAI with molar ratio of 1:3),14,22 twostep sequential deposition,13 and hot casting.15,23 Because of the complexity of film formation under specific environments, different solution-coating routes often result in significantly different film growth mechanisms, film morphologies, and microstructures.24 Therefore, it is generally accepted that solution chemistry/processing has to be redeveloped when transitioning from spin coating to scalable deposition. It is worth noting that device architectures also affect perovskite deposition. Encouraging results have been obtained for both mesoporous13,25,26 and planar structures27,28. Mesoporous structure can display high stability29 and easy perovskite deposition4, whereas planar structures typically have a simpler architecture for processing30. During the past two years, one of the most successful solution-processing approaches is antisolvent dripping/washing,5,31 which has been widely adopted by the perovskite community to produce high-quality perovskite thin films.6,32 Moreover, additives such as MACl can be used in

simple spin-coating methods to control perovskite morphology and coverage33,34. It is commonly accepted that applying an antisolvent during spin coating of the precursor film causes rapid supersaturation, leading to the formation of highly uniform perovskite thin films. However, a challenge for this method is the very narrow time window (seconds) for adding the antisolvent,31 which makes it a difficult transition to any of the mentioned scalable methods.11 Given the sensitivity of the perovskite formation dynamics, there is need to design the perovskite precursor formulation to enable a controllable process window. Considering state-ofthe-art antisolvent-extraction approaches and the role of excess halide in the precursor in film formation, we demonstrate a general chlorine-containing methylammonium lead iodide precursor formulation along with solvent tuning to enable wide precursor processing window and rapid grain growth rate for the planar device structure. Perovskite films were deposited either by blade coating or by spin coating, followed by solvent bathing (solvent-solvent extraction),35 and finally, annealed in air. Proper manipulation of solvent composition extends the precursor-processing time window from seconds to several minutes. Using excess MACl in our ink not only enhances perovskite grain size and film crystallinity, it also increases the perovskite grain growth rate with a significant reduction in the thermal annealing requirements (e.g., 1-min air annealing at 150oC). Shortened annealing will be beneficial for increasing manufacturing throughput. Using a bladecoated absorber in a TiO2-based planar device structure with fullerene interfacial modification, champion solar cells with 0.12 cm2 and 1.2 cm2 areas yield stabilized efficiencies of >19% and 17.5%, respectively. It is noteworthy that spin-coating using the same precursor chemistry generates comparable perovskite film morphology and device performance. Using blade-coated perovskite absorber, we further demonstrate a 12.6-cm2 four-cell module with 88% geometric fill factor (11.09 cm2 active area) and 13.3% stabilized active-area efficiency output. This new precursor ink chemistry bridges the processing gap between spin coating (preferred by most academic groups) and blade coating (geared toward large-scale manufacturing), enabling laboratory-scale advances that are easily translatable to scalable deposition techniques. As mentioned earlier, for spin-coating-based antisolvent-dripping deposition, the timing for adding the solvent to extract the precursor solvent is very critical to the final film quality and device performance. If the solvent is applied too early, then the film will be full of pinholes and/or a spiral pattern. If applied too late, then a rough surface will form after drying. To obtain a good-quality film, there exists a narrow processing window—between these too-early and toolate times—in which the antisolvent must be applied. For most solvents used in the perovskite community so far, the processing window is very short, usually just seconds. Such a short time window makes the reproducibility of high-quality perovskites difficult. The stringent requirement of the solvent-solvent extraction step also makes it very challenging to be implemented in scalable processing approaches. Therefore, a large processing window for several minutes is highly desirable for both laboratory- and large-scale fabrication of efficient PSCs. We show that this challenge can be addressed by tuning solvent properties. Figure 1a illustrates that the processing timing window can be considerably extended using different solvent compositions, as evidenced by the evolution of the average absorbance (450–700 nm) as a function of the processing delay time between the precursor film being blade coated and the solvent bathing step. When NMP:DMF is used, the device PCEs are independent of processing delay time for a wide range (0.5–8 min). In contrast, the PCE drops much more rapidly when using the other two solvent compositions (especially pure DMF), which agrees with the extremely limited time window before the precursor films undergo drastic changes in their optical properties. Once the perovskite precursor ink is casted on the substrate, there is still a large amount of solvent in the wet film. The rate of solvent evaporation ultimately determines how long this wet-film stage exists. The drying process of solvents is strongly correlated with the vapor pressure of the solvent. As shown in Supplementary Table 1, DMF has the highest vapor pressure among commonly used solvents, which explains why DMF quickly evaporates during or after coating. Other solvents, such as DMSO and NMP, have a much lower vapor pressure; so the mixing of these solvents with DMF will greatly extend the time of the wet-film stage. Viscosity of the solvent also plays an important role in the film formation. An additional benefit of using solvent mixtures is that blending solvents allows for some tuning of the viscosity (Supplementary Figure 2). NMP has the lowest vapor pressure, but its relatively high viscosity often results in heterogeneous traces after blade coating and poor device performance (Supplementary Figure 3). It is worth mentioning that precursor with mixed DMF/NMP solvent has a good wetting on c-TiO2 coated FTO substrate (Supplementary Figure 4). In addition to the drying control, there are critical interactions between solvent and salt (mostly PbI2). NMP was reported to form PbI2 (NMP) complex to help form high-quality perovskite films.36

Another important processing parameter for scale-up to future roll-to-roll fabrication is the thermal annealing time. Most solution-processed perovskite films require annealing with various durations. The standard MAPbI3 perovskite using the conventional antisolvent-dripping approach requires annealing for about 5–10 min at 100oC, whereas the mixed-halide approach (PbCl2+3MAI) requires about 45 min of annealing at 100oC.3,37 The longer the annealing time, the longer the required time in the oven heating will need to be for roll-to-roll processing, which could be cost inhibitive. Therefore, it is ideal to have a fast annealing process with flexibilities in the annealing requirement to allow for the highest yield and throughput. Because of the substantial difference in processing conditions and material properties often observed between spin coating and blade coating (or other scalable techniques) as discussed before, it has generally been accepted that solution chemistry and processing controls cannot be readily transferred from spin coating to scalable processing. In contrast, when we use the new precursor chemistry with solvent composition tuning, no apparent difference of the perovskite film morphology/microstructure was found between spin coating and blade coating. This can be attributed to the extended precursor-film processing window. Figure 4a compares the SEM images of the perovskite films prepared by blade coating (top row) and spin coating (bottom row) using various amounts of excess MACl (0%–100%). For both coating approaches, when the amount of MACl is increased, we clearly see an increase of the grain sizes, ranging from 130–400 nm for 0% MACl to 600–2,400 nm for 30% MACl and further to 2,400–3,200 nm for 70% MACl. Despite the general trend of enlarged grain sizes with MACl, using too much MACl is found to cause significant deterioration of the film morphology. Supplementary Figure 8 shows the SEM images at a lower magnification for both spin- and blade-coated perovskite films using 50%, 70%, and 100% MACl. At 50% MACl, the films show large grain sizes with a compact film morphology and minimum amount of pinhole formation. However, a large number of pinholes/voids appear for both spin- and blade-coated films when 70% MACl is used. With 100% MACl, the void space is increased and the surface of the perovskite films also becomes very rough. The formation of the voids is likely caused by the release of excess MACl during annealing. A higher MACl content is expected to cause more voids, and therefore, rougher films. The morphology evolution with different chlorine ratios is similar to previous report using PbCl228, indicating tremendous role of chlorine in morphology formation. Regardless of the clear impact of MACl on film morphology, it is striking to see that there is no difference observed between the spin- and blade-coated films, indicating that the precursor chemistry presented in this study enables a seamless transition between spin coating and blade coating. It has been reported that by using fullerene-based materials (e.g., C60 and PCBM), the electron extraction can be effectively enhanced, and often leads to reduced hysteresis.45-47 Here, we tested coating the compact TiO2 layer with PCBM before blade-coating the perovskite layer. The J–V curves in Figure 6a produce a PCE of 19.06% under reverse scan and 18.54% under forward scan, yielding a hysteresis < 3%. Conclusion In summary, we have demonstrated successful blade coating with antisolvent treatment by formulating solvents to achieve a prolonged (~ 8-min) wet-film stage. Excess MACl was employed to achieve high-crystalline film with a very wide thermal process window. The wide time window for both wet precursor-film processing and post-deposition thermal annealing is attractive for future scalable device fabrication. This approach decouples the precursor-film casting and perovskite formation, enabling similar perovskite morphology and device efficiency for both spin and blade coating, which can facilitate knowledge transfer from academia (preferring spin coating) to industry (relying on scalable methods). Further interface optimization demonstrates solar cells with stabilized efficiencies of >19% for 0.12-cm2 area (17.5% for 1.2 cm2 area) and negligible hysteresis using this scalable ink formulation and thin film deposition method. Using blade-coated perovskite absorber, we obtain a 12.6-cm2 four-cell module with 88% geometric fill factor (11.09 cm2 active area) and 13.3% stabilized active-area efficiency output. The connection between lab and industrial scale enabled by our new solution chemistry and demonstration with a readily scalable processing approach should be of significant general appeal to other fast crystallization processes, such as vacuum flash,48 air blade,49 in addition to the antisolvent treatment. For blade coating, 30 wt% and 45 wt% equimolar ratio MAI and PbI2 precursors with different amounts of MACl additive (15%, 30%, 50%, 70%, 100% molar ratio) in mixed solvent (NMP/DMF 9/8 volume ratio) were used. Typical recipe for 45 wt% precursor with 30% MACl additive is 0.2 g MAI, 0.580 g PbI2, 0.025 g MACl, 0.524 g NMP and 0.429 g DMF. Blade coating was performed on a customized easy-coater station using a ZEHNTNER ZUA 2000 blade at room temperature in air. The gap between the blade and top substrate is fixed at 120 μm. For 1 square-inch and 2.5×1.5 inches substrates, 15 μL and 25 μL precursor were used respectively. Once the precursor ink was dispensed onto the substrate with blade coating, the substrate was transferred into 100 mL DEE bath within 30 s unless otherwise specified. Perovskite film crystalized in ether bath in 1 min. After drying of the bathed film with Nitrogen flow, a further thermal annealing was conducted at 150oC with a petri dish covered for 2–3 min unless otherwise specified. Final perovskite film thickness was controlled by varying precursor-ink concentration and blade-coating speed. 30 wt% precursor inks with various coating speed (0.2, 0.4, 0.8 m min-1) gives ~70 nm-, ~130 nm-, ~200 nm-thick film, and 45 wt% precursor inks with 0.4 m min-1 and 0.8 m min-1 yield the thickness of ~500 nm and ~650 nm, respectively. For spin coating, the same precursor ink is used as in blade coating. Substrate with precursor was spin-coated at 4000 rpm for 25 s, followed by the same ether bathing and thermal annealing as used for blade coating. Blade coating was conducted in ambient air with typical relative humidity of 10%–20% at about 20 oC. Spin coating was conducted in a drybox filled with compressed air. All thermal annealing of perovskite was performed inside the same drybox. Device fabrication. Fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) substrate (TEC 15, Hartford Glass Co) was patterned using hydrogen-evolution etching method (zinc powder and 5M HCl solution). Pre-patterned FTO was cleaned in base bath (0.2 M NaOH in ethanol) and then deposited with a thin compact TiO2 layer by spray pyrolysis using 0.2 M Ti(acac)2OiPr2 in a 1-butanol solution at 450oC. Sprayed film was annealed at 450oC for 1 h. For optimized large-area device, 100-nm thick Au busbar was deposited before TiO2 spray pyrolysis to reduce series resistance. The perovskite film was subsequently deposited using the aforementioned blade- or spin-coating methods. For some devices with PCBM treatment before perovskite deposition, 20 mg mL-1 PCBM in dichlorobenzene was spin-coated at 2,000 rpm for 35 s in N2-filled dry box, followed by annealing at 100oC for 1 h. A hole transport layer (HTL) was spin-coated at 4,000 rpm for 35 s with a HTL solution consisting of 80 mg Spiro-OMeTAD, 30 μL Li-TFSI stock solution (500
