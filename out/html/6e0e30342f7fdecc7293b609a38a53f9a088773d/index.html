Here we report using controllable high temperature shockwaves to synthesize and stabilize single atoms (HT-SAs) at record-high temperatures (1500–2000 K), achieved by a periodic on-off heating featuring a short on-state (55 ms) and a 10-times longer off-state. The high temperature provides activation energy for atom dispersion by forming strong metal-defect bonds, while the off-state critically ensures the overall stability especially for the substrate. The resultant HT-SAs exhibit a superior thermal stability as durable catalysts. The reported shockwave method is facile, ultrafast, and universal (e.g., Pt, Ru, and Co single atoms; carbon, C3N4, and TiO2 substrates), which opens a general route for single atom manufacturing that is conventionally challenging. Single atom catalysts, offering maximized atom-use efficiency and unique coordination environments, are of great interest for catalytic performance enhancements for many reactions including oxidation,1–3 hydrogenation,4–7 electro-catalysis,8–12 and so on.13–16 However, the stability of single atom catalysts remains a grand challenge due to the thermodynamically driven atom aggregation, especially at high temperatures.17–21 In general, atomic dispersions can be achieved by the confinement and coordination of metal atoms to the substrate in wet chemical synthesis to prevent such an aggregation at mild temperatures (< 773 K).1,4,6,10,22 Recent studies have sought to improve the thermal stability of single atoms by enhancing the metal-substrate absorption,17,23–26 using kinetic or spatial confinement,27–29 or forming strong metal-substrate bonds by annealing at 800–900 °C.2,5,8,30,31 The successful synthesis of single atom catalysts at higher temperatures naturally grants higher thermal stability, but high temperature synthesis (> 1000 °C) is typically challenging to achieve and incompatible with many temperature-sensitive methods and materials. In this work, we report the use of high temperature shockwaves to synthesize and stabilize single atoms at record high temperatures (e.g., 1500–2000 K). The high temperature shockwave is achieved using a programmable, periodic on-off heating pattern featuring a short on-state (e.g., ~1500 K for ≤ 55 milliseconds) and a 10-times longer off-state (near room temperature). The on- state provides the activation energy for single atom dispersion by forming metal-defect bonds that can naturally sustain high temperature annealing, as we confirmed with in situ microscopy as well as theoretical simulations. The longer off-state critically ensures the overall dispersion stability by preventing extended heating-induced metal vaporization and particularly substrate deterioration. The high temperature synthesized single atoms (HT-SAs) demonstrate superior

stability in direct methane conversion for 50 h and are also hydrothermally stable after steam treatment at 973 K. Shockwave synthesis of thermally stable single atoms As a demonstration, we began by synthesizing Pt HT-SAs on CO2-activated carbon nanofiber (CA-CNF) substrates. As schematically shown in Fig. 1a, ethanol-based salt precursors (H2PtCl6) are loaded onto the defective CA-CNFs (loading 0.01 µmol/cm2, normalized to the geometric area) with good wetting. The precursor-loaded CA-CNF film was then subjected to a shockwave heating via an electrical Joule heating process that can be easily programmed in terms of temperature, on-off durations, and repeated cycles (Fig. S1). The thermal images captured by a high-speed camera show a uniform spatial temperature distribution during the shock heating process (Fig. S2). Fig. 1b shows the temperature evolution of a pulse heated to ~1500 K for 55 ms and then rapidly quenched by directly cutting off the input current for 10 times longer, leading to an average temperature of ~400 K for the overall process. Fig. 1c shows the temperature profile with 10 heating cycles over a 6 s period, demonstrating the relatively stable temperature that can be repeatedly achieved during the heating process. In addition, we can achieve a temperature up to ~3000 K (Fig. S2c), allowing us to synthesize thermally stable HT-SAs at record high temperatures. Fig. 1d-e shows the high angle annular dark field (HAADF) images of Pt dispersed on the CA-CNF substrate after 1 and 10 heat shocks at 1500 K. For the single heat shock, the surface of the CA-CNFs was dispersed with a high-density single atoms, though Pt clusters were also visible (Fig. 1d and Fig. S3a). However, after 10 shocks, the substrate displayed a mostly uniform single atom distribution (Fig. 1e and Fig. S3b), indicating the further disassembly of clusters into single atoms during the continuous shockwave heating. We further confirmed the

Page 5

single atom dispersion by macroscopic extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) (Fig. 1f) and X-ray near edge structure (XANES) analysis (Fig. S3c). The EXAFS spectrum of the 1- cycle sample shows a weak peak at ~2.5Å corresponding to the Pt-Pt bonding, while a dominate peak at ~1.5Å indicates the Pt-substrate bonds (Pt-C bond, as discussed later), revealing a structure of Pt nanoclusters mixed with single atoms. After 10 cycles, nearly no Pt-Pt bonds remain, suggesting the dominance of single atom dispersion by disassembling the remaining clusters. The CA-CNF substrate has a surface area of ~56 m2/g and the Pt loading is measured to be ~0.24 wt %. We have also varied the metal loading and found that nanoclusters form at a higher loading (see Fig. S4 for the cases of 0.05 and 0.1 µmol/cm2) owing to limited stabilization sites.

To understand the dispersion mechanism, we have designed control samples using different heating strategies (Fig. S5a-c). Low temperature synthesis (573 K, 1 h) lacks the activation energy to effectively disperse and bond these atoms to the substrate (i.e. poor dispersion and stability), while high temperature annealing (1500 K, 10 min) leads to a serious particle agglomeration due to overheating induced (1) graphitization of the carbon substrate (i.e., losing of defects)32 and (2) long-range atom diffusion. Therefore, the shockwave heating creatively utilized high temperature for HT-SAs synthesis but is short enough to avoid the deterioration to the substrates, thus maintaining the stability of the single atom dispersion. In addition, the defects are important to help binding these mobile single atoms onto the substrate and improving their structural stability (Fig. S5d-f). Experimentally, shockwave synthesis on the relatively crystalline CNF (without CO2 activation) yields nanoclusters mixed with single atoms due to limited defective sites. In contrast, after activation, the improved defect concentration and presence of micro-pores (i.e., carbon vacancies) on CA-CNFs (Fig. S6-S7) lead to a high-density single atom dispersion. Accordingly, a more defective substrate can accommodate high density single atoms. Therefore, these control experiments illustrated the critical role of high temperature, shockwave heating, and defects on the substrate for effective single atom dispersion and stabilization. The HT-SAs naturally possess superior structural stability since they were synthesized at a high temperature of 1500 K, which we confirmed by in situ scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) from room temperature up to 1273 K (equipment limit). The sample was stabilized for at least 30 minutes before taking images. As shown in Fig. 2a, the Pt HT-SAs display a uniform and high-density single atom dispersion at each temperature up to 1273 K after holding for 60 minutes. In addition, we also confirmed the stability of the Pt HT-SAs by

Page 8

performing ex situ thermal annealing at 1073 K for 1 h in a furnace with Ar flow (Fig. S8). As the shockwave method can produce a temperature up to 3000 K (Fig. S2c),33 this could enable the synthesis of HT-SAs at an even higher temperature. As an example, we synthesized HT-SAs at 1800 K and 2000 K (using a less loading of 0.005 µmol/cm2) and observed the stable single atom dispersion proved by both STEM and EXAFS measurement (Fig. 2c and Fig. S9). For comparison, in Fig. 2d we summarize previous reports of single atoms synthesized using various methods. Most wet chemical approaches use a mild temperature (< 773 K) and the resulting single atoms are vulnerable under subsequent high temperature annealing, especially when there is no proper bonding or coordination with the substrate.17,19,27,34 Furnace heating or annealing at 1073–1173 K can substantially increase the thermal stability of single atoms by creating strong and stable metal-substrate bonding through atom trapping and substrate anchoring.2,5,8,30 However, the much higher temperature of our shockwave method surpasses furnace heating with the ability to synthesize single atoms upto 1500–2000 K. In addition, a higher temperature synthesis provides ultrafast kinetics for single atom dispersion with extremely shorter processing durations (< 10 s) and higher efficiency compared to low temperature methods.

Fig. 3a shows a typical EXAFS fitting of Pt 1500K HT-SAs, rendering a bond distance of 1.931 Å and a coordination number of 2.8 (indicating a Pt-X3 bond configuration). The experimentally derived bond length is considerably shorter than the literature reported Pt-O (2.01-2.05 Å)10,35 and Pt-N bonds (~2.3 Å)36, but is in good agreement with a Pt-C bond having a calculated bond length of 1.93 Å.37 Moreover, based on this Pt-X3 bond model, we calculated the bond distance of Pt-C in Pt-C3, Pt-N in Pt-N3 and Pt- O in Pt-O2C2 using density functional theory (DFT).38–41 The calculated Pt-C bond in Pt-C3 configuration shows a bond distance of 1.940 Å which is very close to the experimentally fitted result, while Pt-N and Pt-O bonds all have much larger bond distances (Fig. 3b), confirming the Pt-C bond structure in our samples. The DFT result also reveals that the bonding energy of Pt-C bonds is surprisingly higher than Pt-N bonds under the similar bond configurations (Pt-X3 and Pt-X4) (Fig. S11a). In addition, the charge density difference diagrams further illustrate the difference between Pt-C and Pt-N bonds: in Pt-C bonds, large amount of charge transfer appears between Pt and C atoms and the charge density is mainly concentrated in the center, indicating strong covalent bond nature; while in Pt- N bonds, the transferred electron is mainly circulated around the Pt and N atoms, which denotes an ionic bond (Fig. S11b). Therefore, the Pt-C bonds demonstrate a higher binding energy and a unique covalent nature compared with Pt-N bonds in a similar bond configuration.

High temperature dispersion and stabilization

To understand the atomistic origin of the high temperature induced dispersion and stabilization, we performed molecular dynamic (MD) simulations using the reactive force field (ReaxFF) potential42,43 (details in the SI Methods section). The defects on graphene contain randomly etched carbon vacancies to mimic the surface of CA-CNFs. Fig. 3c clearly depicts the dispersion of Pt clusters into single atoms with increasing heating cycles at 1500 K (from I to III), as well as the dispersion stability upon thermal annealing at 1500 K (IV). We observed both the agglomeration and atomization of Pt atoms, while the atomization can only be stabilized when energy-favorable Pt-C bonds are formed (type 4-10 in Fig. 3d). Fig. 3e shows the evolution of bond structures before and after shockwave at 1500 K, where all the initial weak bonds (Pt-Pt and type 1-3 Pt-C) change into strong Pt-C bonds (type 4-10) with higher binding energies, illustrating the atomic origin of why the HT-SAs are more thermally stable than Pt clusters. The simulation clearly reproduced our synthesis process where the initial Pt clusters are weakly attached with the substrate, but with more shockwave heating, the clusters are disassembled into

Page 12

single atoms by forming strong Pt-C bonds that can sustain high temperature annealing. This also indicates defects are essential to stabilize HT-SAs and determine the dispersion density. In the process, high temperature is critical to provide sufficient activation energy for atom diffusion and overcome the energy barrier for bond formation. As an example, when a single Pt atom deviated from a Pt-30 cluster to form a Pt-C bond, kinetically there is an energy barrier (e.g. 1.48 eV) hindering the dispersion, which can only be overcome at a high temperature (Fig. 3f and Fig. S12). We also simulated the shockwave dispersion at other temperatures: 500 K, 1000 K, 2000 K, and 2500 K (Fig. S13). Although similar shockwave patterns (on/off ratio and cycle numbers) were applied, low temperatures (500 K and 1000 K) fail to fully disperse the Pt cluster within the given repeated cycles. In contrast, higher shockwave temperatures have an improved kinetics and achieve single atom dispersion much faster, i.e. a much higher dispersion efficiency (Fig. 3g). Moreover, the Pt-C bond distribution in these HT-SAs shows increasing proportion of more stable type 5-10 bonds at higher synthesis temperatures, indicating improved thermal stability (Fig. 3h). Therefore, high temperature synthesis is critical for single atom dispersion and stabilization by providing the essential activation energy, accelerating the dispersion process, and promoting more stable bond formation.

Generality of the shockwave synthesis and catalytic study The simple shockwave synthesis method can be generally applied to other metals and substrates, in which the high temperature enables the atom dispersion by forming stable metal- defect bonds while the shockwave heating helps maintain the overall stability. Since the shockwave temperature is sufficiently high compared with the thermal decomposition temperatures of most metal precursors, our method can be used to produce single atom dispersions of most metals, as demonstrated of Ru and Co HT-SAs on CA-CNFs (Fig. 4a and Fig. S14). In addition, the shockwave method can be extended to other substrates, such as conductive reduced graphene oxide, semiconductor C3N4 and oxides like TiO2, but using a different heating method (Fig. S15) and forming different bonds with the substrates (Fig. S16). Radiative shockwave heating can be used for powder samples by depositing precursor-loaded powders beneath a carbon film that was joule heated in a shockwave pattern. The high temperature achieved in the film also heated the C3N4 powder and induced a high-density single atom dispersion (Fig. 4b). In addition, the shockwave heating maintains the structural integrity of C3N4 which otherwise would be easily carbonized by prolonged heating (Fig. S17). The non-contact radiative heating can also be easily scaled up for continuous production (Fig. S18). As oxides have poor thermal conductivity for effectively radiative heating, alternatively, we achieved Pt HT-SAs on TiO2 substrates by depositing a thin layer (~2.5 nm) of TiO2 on the nanofibers in CA-CNF film via atomic layer deposition (Fig. S15c). The shockwave heating of the CA-CNF film also heated the TiO2 layer through conductive heating and induced single atom dispersion on TiO2 (Fig. 4c). These results demonstrate the generality of the high temperature shockwave

Page 15

method for synthesizing thermally stable single atom dispersions, which suggests great potential for scalable nanomanufacturing. To test the stability of our HT-SAs, we first performed the in situ hydrothermal test for Pt HT-SAs on CA-CNFs in an environmental TEM (ETEM) at a partial H2O pressure of 10-3 mbar from 300 K to 773 K (upper limit to avoid equipment corrosion), with each studied temperature held for at least 30 minutes. As shown in Fig. S19, there was no nanocluster emerging during the in-situ measurement up to 773 K, proving the HT-SAs are stable. We further confirmed the performance stability of the Pt HT-SAs in the CO oxidation reaction before and after hydrothermal treatment using 5% H2O at 973 K for 4 h, corroborating the hydrothermal stability of the Pt HT-SAs (Fig. 4d). The Arrhenius plots (Fig. 4e, measured by kinetic studies) also show that the apparent reaction energies of the Pt single atom catalysts before and after the steam pretreatment are very close (50.7 kJ/mol vs 53.2 kJ/mol). In addition, we performed multiple cycles of the CO oxidation measurements (Fig. S20), as well as stability test at 493 K for 50 h, both confirming the high stability of the Pt HT-SAs (Fig. 4f). Additionally, we have demonstrated the HT-SAs for reductive catalytic application of direct methane conversion, where single atom catalysts exhibit good performance due to the coke resistance by preventing catalytic C-C coupling.44,45 A control sample was synthesized via conventional impregnation (i.e. IMP) method by using the same material but thermally reducing at 573 K for 1 hour (Fig. S21). Fig. 4g and Fig. S22 display the performance of Pt IMP (Ts=573 K) and Pt HT-SAs (Ts=1500 K) for direct CH4 conversion at 973 K to various products, such as ethylene, ethane, propylene, propane, butene, and benzene over the total hydrocarbon products. The product distribution shows a high selectivity for C2H4, C2H6, and C6H6 (> 90%) for Pt HT-

SAs and no coke formation. In contrast, the Pt IMP sample shows severe coke formation even

Page 16

during the first hour of reaction, which could be ascribed to the continuous ensembles and aggregation of Pt sites at a high temperature (973 K). Fig. 4h shows the superior stability of the Pt HT-SAs in CH4 conversion with a stable turnover frequency (TOF) for 50 h, in contrast to the quick drop of TOF for Pt IMP sample due to its dispersion instability at high temperature. Note that our HT-SAs demonstrate roughly 1000-times higher TOF than Pt-nanocluster-supported on SiO2 catalysts reported in the literature under similar conditions,44 manifesting the criticalness and great efficiency of single atom catalysts. These practical results demonstrate the superior thermal stabilities of HT-SAs synthesized by high temperature shockwaves and their great potential for catalytic applications.

Conclusion We report a high temperature shockwave method for synthesizing and stabilizing high- density single atoms at record high temperatures with a great efficiency. The shockwave design enables high temperature synthesis (1500–2000 K) during the on-state, however, the average temperature is significantly lower (e.g., 400 K) as a result of the extended off-times, making it compatible with various substrates and manufacturing. Control experiments and simulation results reveal the critical roles of high temperature synthesis, which provides the activation energy and ultrafast kinetics for single atom dispersion and promotes more stable bond formation with the defects on the substrates. We confirmed the high temperature stability of the HT-SAs by in situ (stable up to 1273 K) and ex situ STEM (shockwave stability up to 1500–2000 K). We also demonstrated the stability of HT-SAs in CO oxidation and in direct methane conversion for 50 h. The shockwave method is facile and general for synthesizing various thermally-stable single atoms and onto different substrates, which provide a route for single atom manufacturing toward high temperature catalytic reactions.

Materials and substrates. All the salt precursors H2PtCl6 hydrate (99.995%), RuCl3·xH2O (>98%), and CoCl2·6H2O (98%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich without further purification. Carbon nanofiber (CNF) substrates were derived from polyacrylonitrile (PAN, Sigma-Aldrich) by electrospinning an 8 wt% PAN in dimethylformamide (DMF, Sigma-Alrich) solution at a voltage of 15 kV, a spinning distance of 10 cm, and a feed rate of 1 mL/h. The nanofiber mat was first stabilized in air at 533 K for 5 h and carbonized at 1273 K for 2 h in Ar to create the CNF sample. The CNF material was then thermally annealed under a CO2 atmosphere (80 mL/min) at 1023 K for 3 h (CA-CNF). Reduced graphene oxide was synthesized according to modified hummer’s method46 and then thermally reduced at 300°C for 2 hours in an Ar protected tube furnace. The C3N4 powder substrate was synthesized by pyrolysis of melamine powder at 550 °C for 2 hours in a muffle furnace. The TiO2 coated CA-CNFs were fabricated using atomic-layer-deposition (Beneq TFS 500 ALD) of alternating Ti precursor and H2O for 50 cycles (estimated TiO2 thickness of 2.5 nm). Shockwave synthesis. The salt precursors were dissolved in ethanol-based solutions at dilute concentration (0.0005 mol/L), and the solutions were directly dropped onto the suspended CNF films with a loading and left to dry (room temperature, 30 minutes). The precursor-loaded CNF films were then subjected to the shockwave synthesis in an Ar-filled glovebox using a programmable electrical pulse (Keithley 2425). The pulse temperature, duration, and on-off ratio can be easily adjusted by programming in the Keithley 2425 system (the sweep function) or by an external control software. A high-power DC power source (Volteq, 1200 W) was also used when high input power was needed. The programmable pulse heating on the high power electric source is controlled by Keithely through a relay. To estimate the temperature evolution during the shockwave process, a homemade color ratio pyrometer measurement was performed using a Vision Research Phantom Miro M110 highspeed camera. Detailed temperature calculations can be found in our previous paper.47 MATLAB was used to extract raw pixel values to calculate the temperatures. For the figures that show the temperature of a single sample as a function of time, only unsaturated pixels above the black level and within the error threshold were used to report the mean and median temperature of the frame for a contiguous area of at least 10 acceptable pixels. MD Simulations We used the large-scale atomic/molecular massively parallel simulator to perform molecular dynamic (MD) simulations.43 To consider the bond interactions between the Pt and carbon atoms, the ReaxFF potential was used to describe the interaction between atoms in the Pt/graphene system.42 The simulation was performed on a canonical ensemble (NVT) and the Nosé-Hoover thermostat with 0.25 fs time step. We first equilibrated the Pt/graphene system at 300 K for 2.5 ps. Then, we performed repeated heating to 1500 K for 25 ps and cooling to 300 K for 12.5 ps for a total of 50 times (mimicking the shockwave process). Finally, we slowly increased the temperature of the system to 1500 K for 100 ps after single Pt atoms formed to observe the stability of single Pt atoms on the graphene sheet (mimicking the thermal annealing process). In the simulation, the graphene sheet was 10 nm×10 nm in size, and the defects were created by removing carbon atoms randomly. 80 Pt atoms on the graphene sheet were also located randomly. DFT Simulations Density functional theory (DFT) implemented in the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP)39,41 is performed to determine the atomic interaction between lithium and graphene. The generalized gradient approximation (GGA) of the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional40 are used for exchange and correlation interaction. The Brillouin zone is sampled by a 2×2×1 Monkhorst and Pack38 grid. The cutoff energy in our calculation is 400 eV. All the calculations are relaxed to minimize the total energy of the system until the atomic forces are converged to 0.01eV/Å. Period boundary conditions are applied in both in-plane and interlayer directions. To eliminate the interaction between periodic images of atoms, a 12.75Å×12.49Å cubic supercell is used to investigate the interaction of Pt atoms with graphene and a vacuum space of 15 Å is perpendicular to the graphene plane. Considering the existence of suspending bond, all our calculations are added the spin polarization. For single atom dispersion on C3N4 and TiO2, the simulation (boundary and convergent conditions) was set using similar but adaptive rules. Catalytic studies. Catalytic CO conversion was conducted in a fixed-bed flow reactor at atmospheric pressure. Typically, 15 mg of catalyst (Pt HT-SAs on CA-CNFs) was loaded into a quartz tube reactor (7 mm i.d.). Prior to the reaction, the catalyst was pretreated in He (50 mL/min) for 2 h, then cooled down to 50 oC. At 50 oC, the gas flow was switched to the reactant gas feed (50 mL/min) containing 1% CO and 4% O2, balanced by He. The reaction was then carried out at various temperatures, which was increased stepwise from 50 oC to 300 oC. We allowed the system to reach steady state before product analysis. To determine the conversion of the reactants and the product yields, an Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectrometer (Nicolet 6700, Thermo Scientific) equipped with a long path (5 m) gas cell and an MCT detector (with a resolution of 8 cm-1) was used to analyze CO (2173 cm-1). Kinetic measurements were carried out below 200 oC and with higher GHSV of 400 L gcat-1 h-1, ensuring that the reaction condition is within the kinetic zone. The long-term stability test was measured at 220 oC. Catalytic nonoxidative conversion of CH4 was conducted in a fixed-bed flow reactor at atmospheric pressure. Before reaction, a pretreatment was applied: 50 mg catalyst (Pt HT-SAs on ALD CA-CNF) (40–60 mesh) was loaded into a microflow quartz reactor (7 mm i.d.), heated to 110 oC at a rate of 5 oC/min under He (50 mL/min) and held at 110 oC for 1 h. After pretreatment, the temperature was increased to 700 oC under He and the gas flow was then switched to 90% CH4/He (20 mL min-1, space velocity = 24 L/(gcat h)). The reaction temperature was kept at 700 oC, and the reaction was run for 20 h. To determine the conversion of the reactants and the formation of products, a gas chromatograph (GC-2010 plus, Shimadzu) equipped with a SH-Rt-Q-BOND column and a BID detector were employed. All of the lines between the reactor outlet and GC sampling loop inlet were heat-traced to 90 oC to prevent product condensation. The methane conversion, hydrocarbon product selectivity, and coke deposition selectivity were calculated according to the mass balance.
