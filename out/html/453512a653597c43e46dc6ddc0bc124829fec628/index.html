Abstract Iridium-based electrocatalyst is still the only practical anode catalyst for proton exchange membrane (PEM) water electrolyzer due to its excellent stability under acidic oxygen evolution reaction (OER), but is greatly limited by its high cost and low reserves. Here we report a nickel stabilized ruthenium dioxide (Ni-RuO2) catalyst, a promising alternative to iridium, with high activity and durability in acidic OER for PEM water electrolysis. While pristine RuO2 showed poor acidic OER stability and degraded within a short period of continuous operation, the Ni-incorporation can greatly stabilize the RuO2 lattice and extend its durability by more than one order of magnitude. When applied on the anode of a PEM water electrolyzer, our Ni-RuO2 catalyst demonstrated over 1000-hour stability under the water splitting current of 200 mA cm-2, suggesting its great potential for practical applications. Density functional theory studies coupled with Operando differential electrochemical mass spectroscopy analysis confirmed the adsorbate evolving mechanism on Ni-RuO2 as well as the critical role of Ni dopants in stabilizing the surface Ru and subsurface oxygen for improved OER durability.

Main Text Water electrolysis using renewable electricity is widely recognized as a promising and sustainable route to producing clean hydrogen (H2) fuels1-3. Until today, the dominant alkaline water electrolysis (AWE) technology suffers from challenges including the crossover of product gases, high ohmic resistance, limited current density and low operating pressure, mainly because of employing a diaphragm and a liquid electrolyte in the AWE device4, 5. Using a polymer-based proton exchange membrane (PEM) for efficient proton transfers, PEM water electrolysis (PEM-WE) technology can effectively tackle the above challenges with much enhanced performance, and thus attracts broad research interests4-7. However, the large-scale deployment of PEM-WE device is largely obstructed by the lack of active, stable and low-cost acidic oxygen evolution reaction (OER) catalysts5, 8-15 however present sluggish reaction kinetics in acids5, 8, 11, 16. More importantly, they suffer from severe degradations under the strong acidic and oxidative conditions5, 6, 9, 17. It is highly desired but still remains a big challenge to develop active and durable acidic OER electrocatalysts. . Most of existing OER catalysts with excellent performance in alkaline solutions Until today, iridium (Ir) based catalysts such as IrO2 are generally considered as the only practical OER electrocatalysts in PEM devices due to their robust durability and good activity9, 10, 14, 17-20. However, the high cost and extremely low earth abundance greatly limit its large-scale applications5, 9. Ruthenium (Ru) element has been recognized as an attractive alternative to Ir for acidic OER (~ 7.5 times cheaper than Ir)12, 21-24. However, while its oxides (e.g. RuO2) typically present good acidic OER activities, Ru-based catalysts are still greatly challenged by their OER long-term stability in acids or PEM reactors12, 13, 24-26. In recent years, great efforts have been made to improve Ru’s acidic OER performance via strategies such as multi-metal oxides or doping (Sr0.90Na0.10RuO322, Cr0.6Ru0.4O223, Mn- doped RuO227, Ni/Co-doped RuO228, 29), morphology and structure tuning (ultra-thin RuO2 nanosheets30, single atom Ru-N-C26), strain effect (Ru1-Pt3Cu12), reconstruction (A2Ru2O7, A = Y, Nd, Gd, Bi31), etc., resulting in enhanced OER activities and stability compared with commercial RuO2 nanoparticles in acids. Nevertheless, the stability of most Ru-based catalysts reported so far was still limited within tens of hours under a small current density of ~ 10 mA cm-2, which is far from industrial application requirements12, 23, 27, 32. More

importantly, the stability performance obtained under standard three-electrode setup with acidic liquid electrolyte may not necessarily be translated into a practical PEM reactor due to their significantly different reaction environments9. Generally, the acidic OER stability challenge of Ru-based catalysts mainly stems from the following two reasons. One is related to the oxidative release of the lattice oxygen, which could cause over-oxidation of Ru species into dissolvable RuO4 under OER potentials9, 12, 33-35. The other mechanism is the direct demetallation of surface Ru, which can also result in crystal structure collapse of Ru-based catalysts35-37. Therefore, simultaneously stabilizing lattice oxygen and surface Ru in RuO2 catalysts under OER conditions could be a feasible way to boost its stability in acids. Here we report an exciting result that, by incorporating nickel (Ni) dopants into the RuO2 nanocrystals, we were able to dramatically stabilize the RuO2 lattice for an extended durability under acidic OER conditions. The impact of Ni incorporation on RuO2 stability is impressive: while the pristine RuO2 showed a poor OER stability of less than 40-hour operation under a 10 mA cm-2 OER current on a rotation disc electrode (RDE), our Ni doped RuO2 (Ni-RuO2) catalyst showed a stable operation of over 200 hours without obvious degradation. More impressively, once coupled with the commercial Pt/C hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) catalyst in a practical PEM electrolyzer, our Ni-RuO2 catalyst showed a stable water electrolysis of over 1000 hours at 200 mA cm-2, suggesting its great potential for practical water splitting applications. Density functional theory (DFT) studies and Operando differential electrochemical mass spectroscopy (DEMS) analysis confirmed the adsorbate evolving mechanism (AEM) for RuO2 catalysts, and revealed the Ni dopants can obviously enhance the lattice stability of surface Ru and subsurface oxygen, which is responsible for its improved OER durability. Additionally, our Ni-RuO2 catalyst also exhibited a much- improved activity, which needs a small overpotential of 214 mV to deliver 10 mA cm-2 OER current and a cell voltage of 2.10 V (without iR compensation) to deliver 1.5 A cm-2 overall water-splitting current under room temperature. Synthesis and characterizations of catalysts. We developed a three-step process to incorporate Ni into RuO2 lattice for Ni-RuO2 catalysts preparation (Methods; Fig. 1a). First, a wet impregnation of metal precursors was adopted on the carbon black support, which was followed by H2/Ar annealing reduction to obtain Ru3Ni alloy nanoparticles supported

on carbon black (Ru3Ni/C, Fig. 1b, Supplementary Figs. 1a and 2). The carbon support is important during this process to prevent particle aggregation38. Second, the obtained Ru3Ni/C was annealed in air to convert the alloy nanoparticles to Ru3NiOx and meanwhile to remove the carbon supports (Supplementary Figs. 1b and 3). Finally, the obtained Ru3NiOx underwent an acid-leaching process to remove unstable Ni species and get the final catalyst, i.e. Ni-RuO2. The air annealing and acid-leaching processes greatly enhanced acidic OER performance (Supplementary Fig. 4a), and the optimized air annealing temperature was at 450 °C (Supplementary Fig. 4b). The choice of Ni incorporation over other transition metals (Supplementary Fig. 5), and the Ru to Ni precursor molar ratio of 3:1 (Supplementary Figs. 6-11) were made via a rigorous performance optimization process. The X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern of Ni-RuO2 showed the characteristic peaks of rutile RuO2 (JCPDS 71-2273) with the dominant (110) peak at 28.1° (Supplementary Fig. 1c), suggesting the Ni incorporation did not affect the RuO2 lattice structure. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) images (Figs. 1c-e and Supplementary Fig. 12) clearly showed that the Ni-RuO2 nanoparticles were uniform in size with an average diameter of ~ 4.0 nm (the inset of Fig. 1c). This ultrafine nanoparticle size inherited from its precursor (i.e. Ru3Ni/C) where the Ru3Ni nanoparticles had an average size of only 1.7 nm (Fig. 1b). High-magnification HAADF-STEM images clearly revealed that the Ni- RuO2 nanoparticles exhibited high crystallinity, with distinct lattices observed (Fig. 1d, and Supplementary Figs. 12b-d). By focusing on an individual nanoparticle, we observed well-defined lattice fringes that can be ascribed to the {110} planes of RuO2 (Figs. 1e, f). Other TEM image also showed a series of RuO2 lattices including (110) and (210) (Supplementary Fig. 13). The successful Ni incorporation was confirmed by the energy dispersive spectroscopic (EDS) elemental mapping (Fig. 1g and Supplementary Figs. 14, 15). The Ni distribution is uniform across the whole RuO2 matrix. We also synthesized the RuO2 nanoparticle without Ni incorporation (i.e. RuO2) as a control sample. The RuO2 catalyst showed similar morphology to Ni-RuO2 with an average nanoparticle size of ~4.5 nm (Supplementary Fig. 16), as well as high crystallinity as confirmed by XRD

Electronic properties of the catalysts. The X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) survey spectrum (Supplementary Fig. 18a) revealed the Ni atomic ratio of 1.4 at.% (Ni : Ru ratio at around 1 : 21) in Ni-RuO2. There were four peaks in high-resolution Ni 2p XPS spectrum (Fig. 2a); the peaks at 856.0 eV and 873.9 eV were assigned to Ni 2p3/2 and Ni 2p1/2 of Ni2+, respectively, and the peaks at 861.8 eV and 881.6 eV were ascribed to two accompanying satellites39, 40. For high-resolution Ru 3d XPS spectra, two sets of doublet peaks were observed between 280 and 290 eV (Fig. 2b), which could be attributed to the doublet peaks of Ru4+ 3d5/2, 3d3/2 and their satellite peaks23, 24. The binding energy of Ru 3d5/2, and 3d3/2 for Ni-RuO2 was positively shifted (~ 0.1 eV) compared with those of RuO2, possibly suggesting that Ru in Ni-RuO2 catalyst presented a slightly higher oxidation state than that in RuO2 (see the X-ray absorption near-edge spectroscopy (XANES) spectra below for additional evidence). Previous studies have revealed that the Ru species with higher oxidation state could benefit its OER activity30, 41. In high-resolution O 1s XPS spectra (Supplementary Fig. 18b), three peaks at 530.1, 531.3, and 532.9 eV were found to be Ru-O bonds, OH groups, and H2O, respectively42, 43. Compared with RuO2, the Ru-O bonds, and OH groups in Ni-RuO2 were slightly negative shifted, respectively. We then performed X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) on both Ni-RuO2 and RuO2 (Figs. 2c-i). The Ni K-edge XANES suggested the Ni oxidation state in Ni-RuO2 was very close to NiO of +2 (Fig. 2c). The corresponding extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) spectrum of Ni-RuO2 at Ni K-edge showed a dominant peak at around 1.6 Å (Fig. 2d), which was assigned to the Ni-O coordination. No obvious NiO or Ni metal nanoparticles were formed in Ni-RuO2 as suggested by the distinct EXAFS spectrum compared to the NiO or Ni reference, respectively, which indicates a high dispersion of Ni in the RuO2 lattice. This Ni-O coordination environment in Ni-RuO2 was further confirmed by the wavelet transform (WT) of its Ni K-edge, where one strong peak intensity was observed (Figs. 2g-i). We also analyzed the XANES and EXAFS spectra of Ni-RuO2 and RuO2 at the Ru K-edge. While these two Ru K-edges appeared to be very close to each other (Fig. 1e), a zoom-in XANES spectra revealed Ni-RuO2 showed a slightly higher near- edge absorption energy and white line peak than those in RuO2 (Supplementary Fig. 19), suggesting the relatively higher Ru oxidation state in Ni-RuO2, which agrees well with the

Electrochemical characterizations on RDE in acidic electrolyte. Following the OER standard measurements12, 13, 23, 24, we first evaluated OER activities of Ni-RuO2 catalyst and control samples on a RDE setup. The control samples were as-prepared RuO2 and commercial RuO2 nanoparticles (Com-RuO2, see Supplementary Figs. 17b and 21 for characterizations). Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) curves showed that our as- synthesized RuO2 presented better OER activity than Com-RuO2, mainly due to the particle size effects (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 22). The overpotential for Com-RuO2 to deliver 10 mA cm-2 was 330 mV, consistent with previous reports12, 30. The Ni incorporation showed an obvious enhancement to the OER activity of RuO2: the overpotential for Ni-RuO2 to reach 10 mA cm-2 was 214 mV, lower than that of RuO2 (227 mV). This overpotential difference was quickly enlarged when to deliver higher current densities, due to the significantly improved Tafel slope of Ni-RuO2 (42.6 mV dec-1) compared with RuO2 (52.9 mV dec-1) and Com-RuO2 (60.7 mV dec-1) (Fig. 3b). The electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) of Ni-RuO2 at 1.45 V showed the lowest charge transfer resistance (Fig. 3c), suggesting a much facile OER kinetics compared with RuO2 and Com-RuO224, 30. The superior activity of Ni-RuO2 can be reliably repeated (Supplementary Fig. 22a), and is among the best compared to other reported acidic OER catalysts (Supplementary Table 1). To better understand the origin of high OER performance of Ni-RuO2, we first tested the electrochemical double-layer capacitance (Cdl) to calculate the electrochemically active surface area (ECSA) and the roughness factor (Rf) for activity normalization (Fig. 3d, Supplementary Figs. 23a-c, and Supplementary Table 2). Obviously, our prepared Ni- RuO2 and RuO2 with ultrafine nanocrystal structures showed higher Cdl and ECSA than those of Com-RuO2 (Fig. 3d, and Supplementary Table 2). Particularly, Ni-RuO2 had the highest Cdl and ECSA which are nearly twice of those in RuO2, suggesting the Ni incorporation can significantly improve the density of active sites. Considering the ECSA- normalized OER activity, the specific activity still followed an order of Ni-RuO2 ˃ RuO2

While achieving high OER activity is important, a good stability may play an even more important role in practical water splitting applications9, 17. We studied the OER durability in the acidic electrolyte at 10 mA cm-2 on RDE (Fig. 3f), a widely adapted benchmark criteria in literature10, 12, 24. Although our synthesized RuO2 showed improved stability compared with the Com-RuO2, it still degraded at a fast rate and could last no longer than 40 hours. The excellent durability of Ni-RuO2 outperforms the-state-of- the-art non-Ir-based acidic OER catalysts reported in RDE tests (Fig. 3g and Supplementary Table 1). The excellent OER durability of Ni-RuO2 compared with pristine RuO2 clearly indicates the critical role of Ni incorporation. Mechanism understanding. We performed DFT simulations to understand the effects of Ni dopants on the OER performance, especially the stability, of RuO2 catalyst. Consistent with our XRD observation (Supplementary Fig. 1c), RuO2 crystal was modelled to have a rutile lattice structure. Moreover, we chose to study the catalytic properties of fully oxidized (110) surface of RuO2 (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 34a), because it was predicted to be the most stable surface in our RuO2 catalyst annealed at 450 °C 21, 45, 46. In

8

Page 11

the outermost layer of RuO2 (110) surface, there are two types of Ru sites, i.e. the coordinatively unsaturated site (CUS) and the fully coordinated bridge site (BRI). The Ru atom has five coordinated O atoms at a CUS Ru site whereas binds to six O atoms at a BRI site47. Our DFT results indicate it would be energetically favorable for a Ni dopant to replace the BRI Ru instead of the CUS site (Supplementary Fig. 35). Additionally, the simulated EXAFS based on this proposed atomic structure agrees well with our experimental tests (Supplementary Fig. 36). We thus adopted such a Ni-doped RuO2 (110) surface to examine the impacts of Ni incorporation on the OER process (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 34b). As depicted schematically in Fig. 4c, a 4e- OER on RuO2 (110) starts from H2O adsorption at a CUS Ru site, followed by two sequential deprotonation steps to form *OH and then *O. Subsequently, the reaction pathway bifurcates into two possible mechanisms, i.e. lattice oxygen mechanism (LOM) and AEM12, 15, 24, 36. Following LOM, the surface adsorbed *O further reacts with a lattice O to produce a O2, whereas the generated O vacancy is refilled by another H2O. Following AEM, the surface adsorbed O further reacts with another H2O in the electrolyte to form *OOH and then the final product of O2. We noticed that hydrogen bonds between H and an adjacent O helped stabilize OOH adsorption at the CUS Ru site on RuO2 (110) surface (Supplementary Fig. 37)47, 48. Supplementary Fig. 38 shows the DFT predicted free energy evolution for OER via AEM on RuO2 (110) surface. The OER limiting potential, defined as the lowest potential under which the free energy evolution becomes downhill, is about 1.89 V on RuO2 (110) surface. Additionally, the rate determining step for OER on RuO2 (110) surface was predicted to be the step of O transformation to OOH (Supplementary Note 1), consistent with the previous result24, 27. In contrast, the limiting potential for OER via LOM was predicted to be 3.76 V (Supplementary Fig. 39), which is 1.87 V higher than that of AEM. This result suggests the OER on RuO2 (110) surface would be thermodynamically more favorable to follow the AEM mechanism rather than the LOM, consistent with a previous study36. PEM-WE device performance. In summary, we developed a non-iridium-based acidic OER catalyst that can deliver at least 1000-hour stability under a 200 mA/cm2 water-splitting current in a PEM-WE device. The findings in this work not only provided an effective method and new understanding to improve the catalytic performance of RuO2 catalyst, but also demonstrated its great potential to be implemented in practical PEM cells in the future for large-scale clean H2 generation. While there is still a gap between our Ni-RuO2 catalyst’s stability performance and a typical industrial requirement (delivering over 1 A cm-2 current for over 10 years at 80 °C of operation), this encouraging milestone stability we achieved using Ru-based catalyst clearly suggests the possibility of replacing Ir-based catalysts in the future. More studies can be done in the future to further improve the stability of Ru-based catalysts, such as temperature effects, multi-element doping, etc.

Synthesis of catalysts. In a typical procedure, 0.1721 g of RuCl3 and 0.0656 g of NiCl2·6H2O (i.e. RuCl3 and NiCl2 with a molar ratio of 3: 1) were mixed in 150 mL of 1 M HCl and sonicated for 2 h. 0.4 g of carbon black (Cabot, BP2000) was then added and dispersed by stirring for 18 h under room temperature to ensure the uniform distribution. Next, the mixture solution was dried by a rotary evaporator, and the remaining powder was collected. The powder was annealed in a flowing Ar/H2 (5% H2) atmosphere at 900 °C for 2 h and then annealed in air at 250-550 °C (450 °C as the optimized temperature) for 3 hours. After annealing, 50 mg of product was added in 20 mL 1 M HCl and mixed under room temperature for 12 h to perform acid leaching, and then centrifuged and washed with deionized water for 3 times. Finally, the sample was dried at 80 °C in an oven for 12 h to obtain the Ni-RuO2 catalyst. The RuaNibOx-AL samples with different Ni dopant amounts were also prepared following the same procedure, except that the amount of precursor was tuned in the first step. Besides, RuO2 without Ni incorporation was also prepared in a similar method by using RuCl3 as precursor. In addition, the M-RuO2 (M = Fe, Co, Zn, Cu) samples with different metal dopants were also prepared using the same procedure, except that the different precursors were used in the first step, including FeCl3, CoCl2, Zn(NO3)2·6H2O, and CuCl2·2H2O. Of note, the weight ratio of total metal content in precursors to carbon black support was fixed at 1:4 in all of catalyst synthesis.
