In both inorganic and organic semiconducting materials, trap states and localized electronic states in the forbidden gap caused by material imperfections are one of the main limiting factors to high performance optoelectronic devices, such as solar cells, ultra-fast photodetectors, field effect transistors, light-emitting diodes, among others. Suppressing these trap states in semiconductor thin films through a simple and efficient approach is thus critical to the enhancement of optoelectronic device performance. A new generation of photovoltaic materials, namely, organic-inorganic halide perovskites (OIHPs),[1] have been shown to possess unique defect tolerant properties, which can give rise to very long carrier diffusion length, significantly longer than the optical absorption length in both single crystals and polycrystalline films. The power conversion efficiency (PCE) of solar cells based on OIHP has skyrocketed in the past few years, [2-7] in part due to negligible charge recombination in the grain interior.[5, 8-10] On the other hand, the polycrystalline films still have several orders magnitude higher density of traps than in single crystals, suggesting the presence of large density of charge traps at grain boundaries (GBs) or film surface.[11-13] A recent direct optical study revealed strong surface recombination in single crystalline MAPbI3, the most studied OIHP material thus far for solar cells. [14] Thus, lowering the carrier recombination at the GBs of perovskite polycrystalline thin films and contacts between perovskite active layer and electrodes is critical to achieving high efficiency perovskite solar cells. To date, many research efforts have been made to enlarge grains of the perovskite polycrystalline films for

reducing the total GB area;[15-19] For example, large-aspect-ratio (up to 7.9) polycrystalline perovskite grains have been grown on non-wetting surface,[15] resulting in over ten-fold reduced charge recombination in the devices. Now that the photo-generated carriers can flow through perovskite films with negligible charge recombination, minimization of the charge recombination at the contacts becomes increasingly important to produce highly efficient perovskite solar cells. Thus, efficient passivation techniques to lower the recombination at perovskite film surfaces are critically needed for achieving very high device efficiency.[20-24]

Due to the low thermal stability of MAPbI3 and MAI can be lost from the crystal, unavoidable thermal annealing (usually at temperature above 80 °C) in the deposited process of MAPbI3 film can cause non-stoichiometry in composition and also decompose MAPbI3 to PbI2 on the surface and at the GBs of polycrystalline films, [25-27] where unsaturated or under-coordinated Pb ions are most frequently found. The theoretical modeling and experimental studies have shown that these under-coordinated Pb atoms, or/and Pb clusters in some cases, can act as the electronic trap states within the perovskite material [28, 29] and also as one of main surface traps that greatly limit the performance of MAPbI 3-based devices. The under-coordination of the Pb atom can result in the formation of net positive charges on the atom, which would then create favorable conditions for coordination or bond formation with electron-rich Lewis bases.[22, 24]

Herein, for the first time, we introduce a π-conjugated Lewis base with n-type semiconductor property to the MAPbI3 based solar cells as an interlayer between MAPbI3 and cathode. The π-conjugated Lewis base is essentially a type of organic semiconducting molecules attached with Lewis base blocks. It has multifunction that can combine trap-passivation and charge-extraction, which can increase both photovoltage and photocurrent. The Lewis base can effectively passivate the Lewis acid traps (e.g. under-coordinated Pb ions and Pb clusters) on the surface and at the GBs of MAPbI3, and the n-type π-conjugated material possesses chemical properties that promote electron extraction and electron transport. The principle of engineering MAPbI3 surface by π-conjugated Lewis is illustrated in Figure 1a, where the formation of Lewis adducts (Figure 1b) can passivate the trap states (Figure 1c) of MAPbI3 polycrystalline films. The organic n-type π-conjugated molecules can extract the electrons generated from MAPbI3 and then transport them to device cathode (Figure 1a). Finally, the MAPbI3 based solar cells with low temperature solution-processed IDIC (a π-conjugated Lewis base, molecular structure in Figure S1 and Figure 2a) layers result in a lower trap density, enhanced stabilized PCEs up to 19.5% with a higher open-circuit voltage (VOC) of 1.11 V, more stable power output, and higher device stability compared to the control devices without IDIC layers.

Like several solution processed fullerenes derivatives,[20, 30-32] such as [6]-phenyl-C61-butyric acid methyl ester (PCBM), which are most commonly applied to the planar heterojunction perovskite solar cells as electron-transporting layers, the organic semiconductor IDIC[33] possess low LUMO (-3.9 eV) and HOMO (-5.7 eV) energy levels (Figure S2), and the electron transporting property with an electron mobility of 1.1 × 10-3 cm2 V-1 s-1.[33] Thus, the photo-induced electrons can transfer from MAPbI 3 to IDIC at the MAPbI3/IDIC interface, and then can be collected efficiently by the device cathode. However, compared to isotropic packed PCBM, IDIC thin films show stronger self-aggregation and better π-π stacking, which can be supported by grazing incident wide-angle X-ray diffraction (GIWAXS) analysis of IDIC (Figure 2b and 2c) and PCBM (Figure S3) neat films. From GIWAXS analysis, as-cast IDIC shows longer π-π stacking coherence length of 3.5 nm than that of PCBM, which is ~2.5 nm derived via Scherrer equation[34]. The good π−π stacking of IDIC is also expected to give rise to a small energy disorder of electronic states, and then small VOC loss in MAPbI3/IDIC based solar cells.[35] The π−π stacking in the out-of-plane direction of the as-cast IDIC thin films is obviously stronger than that in the in-plane direction, which indicates that the IDIC molecules have predominantly face-on orientation. The vertical π−π stacking should facilitate the electron transport between MAPbI3 and the cathode.

Furthermore, unlike the Lewis acid fullerenes which can passivate the Pb-I antistite defect,[21] the IDIC provides the Lewis base carbonyl (C=O) and cyano (C≡N), as electron-withdrawing end-groups. Figure 2a shows an image of electrostatic potential (ESP) surface computed by using a density functional theory (DFT) method. The IDIC shows a planar π-conjugated main chain with a dominated continuous positive ESP, and the N and O atoms of IDIC concentrate negative charges. Thus the carbonyl and cyano groups are expected to passivate the under-coordinated Pb of MAPbI3 through coordination between Lewis acid and base.[36] To confirm the formation of Lewis adducts of Pb and O or N, we firstly measured the UV-Vis absorption spectra of IDIC neat solution and IDIC: PbI2 mixed solution. Generally, the formation of coordination bond between metallic ions and organic ligands will change the conjugated structures of chromophore, and then shift its absorption peaks.[37] IDIC in N, N-Dimethyl formamide (DMF) solution exhibits broad absorption in the 400–700 nm region with two peaks at 496 nm and 636 nm (Figure 3a); IDIC: PbI2 mixed in DMF solution shows obviously stronger and blue shifted absorption (two peaks at 485 nm and 611 nm) relative to IDIC neat solution, which indicates the existence of relatively strong interaction between PbI2 and IDIC. Then to further confirm the mechanism of interaction, the Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy measurement was conducted to analyze IDIC powder and IDIC: MAPbI3 blend. For the neat IDIC powder, the peaks of 1698 cm-1 and 2215 cm-1 are assigned to the symmetric stretching vibrations of C=O bonds (ν(C=O)) and

To distinguish differences between IDIC and IDIC: MAPbI3 easily, we normalize their FTIR maximum peak (1540 cm-1) (Figure S4) assigned to stretching vibrations of C=C bonds aromatic nucleus, which is thus insensitive to Pb ions. The FTIR spectroscopy of the blended IDIC: MAPbI3 powder shows weakened vibration strength for ν(C=O) and ν(C≡N) by 39% and 45%, respectively (Figure 3b), which confirmed the presence of interaction between MAPbI3 and the C=O and C≡N of IDIC. It should be noted that the samples for absorption spectrum measurement were prepared at room temperature without any thermal annealing, which indicates that the passivation of MAPbI3 by IDIC can bypass the widely used thermal annealing treatment in most of reported passivation processes.

DFT calculation was also undertaken to investigate the function of IDIC, particularly the calculation of the density of states with and without IDIC on top of a MAPbI3 surface with partial loss of MAI. As shown in Figure 3c, the deep trap state (at -4.5 eV) induced by under-coordinated Pb clusters is lowered, and becomes much more shallow upon the adsorption of C=O and C≡N of IDIC. We also found direct evidences for the passivation effect of IDIC by measuring the trap density of the devices with and without IDIC. We further investigated the charge transfer between the MAPbI3 films and IDIC by photoluminescence (PL). Here we confined light attenuation region close to the surface of perovskite layer by choosing short wavelength excitation light of 532 nm which has a penetration length of 80 nm into MAPbI3, much less than the thickness (i.e. ~500 nm) of the perovskite films. The neat MAPbI3 film showed the PL peak at 770 nm (Figure 4a), and then a PL quenching of MAPbI3 by 87% was observed by the contacting of IDIC with MAPbI3, larger than the observed quench ratio (70%)[20] by PCBM. This indicates IDIC yields higher efficiency of electron extraction from perovskite layers than PCBM. Meanwhile, MAPbI3 covered with IDIC showed a 5 nm blue-shifted PL peak compared to neat MAPbI3 film, which agrees well with that expectation the IDIC can passivate the traps at the MAPbI 3 surface by filling the deep traps and/or shifted deep traps to shallow traps.[20] The time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) study of MAPbI3 and MAPbI3/IDIC films also revealed efficient charge extraction from MAPbI3 to IDIC. Samples were excited with 404 nm pulsed diode laser with a pulse width of 45 ps. As shown in Figure 4b, the neat MAPbI3 film had a decay time of 96 ns calculated with a single exponential decay function fitting. The quick portion of the exponential PL decay of the MAPbI3/IDIC film was fitted with a decay time of 9.7 ns. Both PL and TRPL analyses demonstrated that IDIC is an efficient electron acceptor for MAPbI3.

Since IDIC combines excellent trap passivation and good electron extraction of MAPbI3, the application of IDIC as the electron transport layer (ETL) is expected to yield high performance devices. Thus, to demonstrate its potential, we used MAPbI3 made by interdiffusion method[41] with solvent annealing treatments[42] as the active layers and IDIC as a trap-passivation and ETL to fabricate p-i-n perovskite solar cells with a device structure (Figure 5a) of ITO/poly(triaryl amine) (PTAA)/MAPbI3/IDIC/C60/BCP/Cu, where PTAA was chosen as the hole transporting layer because of the large perovskite grain sizes formed on the non-wetting surface of PTAA. The top-view (Figure S5) and cross-section (Figure 5b) scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images show the grain sizes of MAPbI 3 is in the micrometer range. The grains connect the electrodes and the GBs are perpendicular to the substrate for MAPbI3 films to minimize the GB energy, which minimizes the charge recombination in the perovskite films. We characterized the morphology of IDIC thin-film using optical microscope and SEM. Although as-cast IDIC has better molecular stacking than as-cast PCBM in molecular scale, it is easy to form a continuous thin film on perovskite surface by spin-coating, as shown optical (Figure S6) and SEM images (Figure S7). There was no “black dots” caused by self-aggregation on the surface of IDIC thin film. Due to the coverage of MAPbI3 surface by less conductive IDIC, the GBs of the perovskite films in Figure S7 became misty compared with the uncoated MAPbI3 film shown in Figure S5. This good IDIC coverage is important for complete trap-passivation effect in the perovskite

After storage for 30 days, the device PCE with IDIC keeps almost stable around 90% of original value, which is better than that (ca. 80%) of the control devices without IDIC layer. In conclusion, we introduced a π-conjugated Lewis base (IDIC) into perovskite solar cells as an interlayer between perovskite and cathode. The IDIC can extract electron from perovskite layers because of its n-type semiconducting property, meanwhile the Lewis base end-groups of IDIC can passivate the traps on the perovskite films at room temperature. MAPbI3/IDIC based solar cells yield a 45% PCE enhancement relative to the control perovskite solar cells without IDIC. This study also points a new route to design interlayer materials based on new π-conjugated Lewis bases by combing various organic π-conjugated structures (e.g. aromatic fused-rings, oligothiophenes etc.) and some typical Lewis base substituent groups (e.g. thiocyanato, sulfhydryl etc.); and then π-conjugated Lewis bases can be broadly applicable to highly efficient perovskite solar cells with no hysteresis and stable power output, and many other high-performance optoelectronic applications, such as ultra-fast photodetectors, lasers and light-emitting diodes.

The fabrication and measurement of solar cells. The methods of cleaning ITO glass, methylammonium iodide (MAI) synthesis [19] and IDIC synthesis[33] can be found elsewhere. The spin coating process was conducted in glovebox with oxygen level lower than 100 particle per million (PPM). The PTAA was dissolved in toluene with a concentration of 2 mg mL-1, and spun-coated on the ITO/glass substrates at the speed of 5000 rpm. The spun PTAA films were thermally annealed at 100 °C for 10 min. The perovskite films were made by interdiffusion method, where lead iodide (PbI2) and MAI solutions were sequentially spun onto the substrates, followed by a thermal annealing driven diffusion process. The concentration of PbI2 and MAI was 650 mg mL-1 in DMF and 65 mg mL-1 in isopropanol, respectively. The spun films were annealed at 100 °C for 60 min on hotplate under the cover of a glass petri dish. 20 µL DMF solvent was added at the edge of the petri dish during annealing process. After annealing, IDIC were deposited on top of MAPbI3 by solution spin-coating from chloroform solution (2 mg mL-1 ) at a speed of 6000 rpm. After the deposition of IDIC layer, 20 nm thickness of C60 was thermally evaporated with a deposition rate of 0.5 Å/s. The devices were finished by the evaporation of 8 nm 2,9-dimethyl-4,7-diphenyl-1,10-phenanthroline (BCP) and 80 nm copper (Cu) electrode. The devices area was defined as the overlap of ITO and Cu electrode. The device area was 7.5 to 12 mm2. The photocurrent-voltage (J-V) curves of the devices were measured in glovebox with oxygen level below 100 PPM. AM 1.5G irradiation (100 mW cm-2) with a

Passivation Simulation Method

Our first-principles calculation for the passivated structures was carried out in the framework of DFT as implemented in the VASP program. [5, 6] The generalized gradient approximation (GGA) in the form of Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) was used for the exchange–correlation functional. The ion–electron interaction is treated using the projector-augmented wave (PAW) method. [7] Since PbI2 surface was likely to dominate the surface geometry of the sample used in this study, surface slabs were modelled as a PbI2-terminated symmetric (001) slab with the tetragonal structure, and 7 PbI2 and MAI layers in total. About 30 Å vacuum was added on top of the slab surface to minimize the interaction between the adjacent slabs. To simplify modelling of the passivation effect of the end group of IDIC molecule to the trap state induced by Pb clusters, we used structures of the simplest Pb cluster, i.e., a Pb atom on PbI2-terminated surfaces with or without the Lewis base end group of IDIC. The 2×2 periodicity was used in the xy plane.
