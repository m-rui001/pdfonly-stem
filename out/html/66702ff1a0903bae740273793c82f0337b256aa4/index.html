Another important strategy relies upon recovering some portion of this waste heat to do useful work. A variety of broad strategies exist for thermal energy conversion, including phase-change cycles such as Rankine cycles (steam engines), singlephase cycles based on gases (e.g., Carnot engines) or liquids (e.g., Malone engines), and solid-state heat engines such as those based on the thermoelectric (TE) effect. The thermoelectric effect describes the conversion of a temperature gradient, typically across a doped semiconductor or metal, into an electrical voltage (Seebeck effect) or vice versa (Peltier effect). While TE energy conversion devices represent a potentially promising strategy for recovering energy from waste heat sources across a broad range of temperatures, many of the semiconductors that currently deliver the best TE effciencies are costly to manufacture and are based on elements that are relatively scarce and/or toxic. As examples, the most widely used thermoelectric materials are inorganic semiconductors and their alloys, including Bi2Te3, PbTe, and Sb2Te3.[3] As such, the cost per Watt of delivered electricity is prohibitively high and there is currently no major commercial market for TE energy conversion devices. Other important potential applications for TE devices include the generation of electricity in remote locations (e.g., military feld deployments) and/or powering small mobile wireless devices that require low power (e.g., health sensors for athletes or emergency responders). In these applications, device weight and form factor (e.g., fexible and conformable devices) become just as important as cost and effciency. Conversion of waste heat to voltage has the potential to signifcantly reduce the carbon footprint of a number of critical energy sectors, such as the transportation and electricity-generation sectors, and manufacturing processes. Thermal energy is also an abundant low-fux source that can be harnessed to power portable/wearable electronic devices and critical components in remote off-grid locations. As such, a number of different inorganic and organic materials are being explored for their potential in thermoelectricenergy-harvesting devices. Carbon-based thermoelectric materials are particularly attractive due to their use of nontoxic, abundant source-materials, their amenability to high-throughput solution-phase fabrication routes, and the high specifc energy (i.e., W g−1) enabled by their low mass. Single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNTs) represent a unique 1D carbon allotrope with structural, electrical, and thermal properties that enable effcient thermoelectric-energy conversion. Here, a detailed review of the progress made toward understanding the fundamental thermoelectric properties of SWCNTs, nanotube-based composites, and thermoelectric devices prepared from these materials is provided. This progress illuminates the tremendous potential that carbon-nanotube-based materials and composites have for producing highperformance next-generation devices for thermoelectric-energy harvesting.

The primary challenge in optimizing materials for thermoelectric energy conversion is the fact that each of these three material properties (σ, α, and κt) are inter-related and do not have the same dependence on carrier density, as shown in Figure 1b. As the carrier density increases, the electrical conductivity increases (benefcial for zT) but the Seebeck coeffcient decreases (detrimental to zT). This complex interdependence begets three guiding principles for the rational development of thermoelectric materials—(1) the peak value of zT at a given temperature for a given material is found at a particular value/range of carrier density, (2) since each critical material property has its own temperature dependence, the peak value of zT for a given material is also found at a particular temperature, and (3) zT can often be increased by strategies that help to decouple or weaken the interdependence of σ, α, and κt.

Effect of SWCNT Doping on σ and α Since all three parameters comprising zT typically depend on the carrier density in a given semiconductor (Figure 1b), the ideal strategy to probe this dependence would employ methods to sensitively tune the carrier density over a large range while probing α, σ, and κ. This is diffcult in many organic and inorganic TE systems, however, due to a variety of factors. In inorganic semiconductors, it is often diffcult to fnd the proper conditions and atomic dopants with appropriate characteristics that enable precise tuning of the carrier density without encountering structural deformations and phase segregation. In typical organic polymers, molecular dopants can be used to tune carrier density via ground state charge transfer interactions that inject holes or electrons into the polymers.[23] However, the addition of these dopants to polymer solutions can create phase segregation and/or completely different morphologies in the subsequently deposited solid-state polymer/blend.[24] In both cases, the expected improvements to conductivity (via increasing the carrier density) can often be counteracted by deleterious structural changes that reduce the electronic coupling between domains and hinder long-range charge carrier transport. SWCNTs represent a unique system with some clear advantages for using charge transfer dopants to tune the carrier density. First, SWCNT networks tend to have both porosity and very high surface areas, so SWCNT surfaces are typically quite accessible for adsorbing redox molecules (Figure 6a–c). Second, the morphology of a SWCNT thin flm is essentially “locked in” once it is deposited. In other words, the network can be immersed in a wide variety of solvents and dopant-containing solutions without leading to any changes in morphology. Third, the sensitivity of the SWCNT pi electron system to surfacemediated redox reactions means that no covalent bonds need to be formed to generate large swings in the carrier density injected by physisorbed molecules. These traits imply that SWCNT networks can be doped simply by soaking in solutions of redox molecules, without any appreciable changes to the network morphology (e.g., SWCNT connectivity, bundle size, porosity, etc.) or SWCNT atomic or electronic structure (e.g., no creation of sp3 defects, no modifcation of intrinsic band structure, etc.). Within a single nanotube, electron and hole mobilities are typically very high, whereas the junctions between SWCNTs typically have appreciable resistance. The markedly higher junction resistance gives rise to a positive temperature coeffcient for the electrical conductivity (negative temperature coeffcient for resistivity) of SWCNT mats, where increasing the temperature provides the thermal energy necessary to surmount the barriers at tube-tube junctions (Figure 11a,b). This behavior is often modeled within a framework of fuctuation-induced tunneling or variable range hopping.[22a,b,50] Many early studies report a transition to a negative temperature coeffcient for conductivity above a transition temperature, indicating phonon-limited transport at higher temperatures that is more representative of metals.[50b] As other studies have noted, this transition to “metallic” conductivity may be convoluted with the temperature-induced desorption of the molecules (oxygen or intentionally added, thermally labile dopants) that impart the p-type character of these flms.[22a,b] Doping SWCNTs with excess charge carriers “fattens out” the increase of conductivity with temperature, suggesting that the dopants ameliorate to some degree the barriers that charge carriers encounter at tube-tube junctions (Figure 11a,b).[22a,b] For most doped organic semiconductors, the Seebeck coeffcient increases with increasing temperature, with the dependence varying from linear (degenerately doped and/ or relatively ordered) to sublinear (lightly doped and/or relatively disordered).[4c,49a] Reports of temperature-dependent

thermopower in SWCNT networks demonstrate that the Seebeck coeffcient increases with increasing temperature, both below and above room temperature (Figure 11c,d).[16a,19a,b,20a,50b] This behavior appears to hold for individual SWCNTs and networks comprised of metallic, semiconducting, and mixed SWCNTs at a variety of doping levels. Temperature-dependent thermal conductivity has been studied for a number of SWCNT systems. Below room temperature, the thermal conductivity of single tubes has been observed to increase with rising temperature.[44b] Above room temperature, κ was observed to decrease with rising temperature for a single tube, generally consistent with Umklapp phonon–phonon scattering (Figure 11e).[44f ] For s-SWCNT networks, κ is observed to decrease (Figure 11f) or remain relatively constant with increasing temperature.[16a,44g] While much work remains to be done in using temperature-dependent transport to uncover transport mechanisms relevant to thermoelectric energy harvesting in SWCNT networks, the results summarized here are encouraging. Notably, for s-SWCNT networks, the electrical conductivity and thermopower both tend to increase with increasing temperature, whereas the thermal conductivity tends to decrease. These trends imply that zT should continue to increase above room temperature. Indeed, Avery et al. recently observed that zT for doped s-SWCNT networks was ≈2.5 times higher at 350 K than at 300 K.[16a] Performance at elevated temperatures (with associated studies of thermally stable doping/ encapsulation strategies) should be a fruitful research direction for realizing the true potential of SWCNT-based materials and devices.

Recycling the heat dissipated by the human body could potentially charge the batteries of small electronic devices simply by embedding a fexible organic thermoelectric device into an article of clothing.
