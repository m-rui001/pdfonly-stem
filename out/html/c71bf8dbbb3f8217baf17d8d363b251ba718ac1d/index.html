Abstract Although the superoxide of lithium (LiO2) is believed to be a key intermediate in Li-O2 batteries leading to the formation of lithium peroxide, LiO2 has never been observed in its pure state. In this work, we provide evidence that use of a cathode based on a reduced graphene oxide with Ir nanoparticles in a Li-O2 battery results in a LiO2 discharge product formed by single electron transfer without further electron transfer or disproportionation to form Li2O2. Differential electrochemical mass spectroscopy (DEMS) and high energy X-ray diffraction (HE-XRD) patterns indicate the presence of LiO2 with no evidence of Li2O2 or Li2O. The HE-XRD studies as a function of time also show that LiO2 can be stable in its crystalline form after one week of aging in the presence of electrolyte. The results provide evidence that LiO2 is stable enough that it can be repeatedly charged and discharged with a very low charge potential (~3.2 V) and may open the avenue for a lithium superoxide-based battery. 1

Batteries based on sodium and potassium superoxide have recently been reported1-3. However, there have been no reports of a battery based on lithium superoxide (LiO2), despite much recent research4-10 into the lithium-oxygen (Li-O2) battery because of its potential high energy density. Several recent studies11-16 of Li-O2 batteries have found evidence of LiO2 being formed as one component of the discharge product along with lithium peroxide (Li2O2). In addition, theoretical calculations have indicated that some forms of LiO2 may have a significant lifetime.17 The recent reports of the existence of LiO2 as one component in the discharge product would suggest it might be formed by itself for use in a battery. However, solid LiO2 has been difficult to synthesize in pure form18 because it is thermodynamically unstable with respect to disproportionation to Li2O2 19,20. Here we show for the first time that crystalline LiO2 can be stabilized in a Li-O2 battery with no evidence for the presence of Li2O2 by using a suitable cathode material. In this work we find that it is possible to promote growth of LiO2 without further electron transfer or disproportionation to form Li2O2, as is generally found. A novel templating growth mechanism involving the use of Ir nanoparticles on the cathode surface may be responsible for the crystalline LiO2 growth. Our results demonstrate that the LiO2 formed in the Li-O2 battery is stable enough that it can be repeatedly charged and discharged with a very low charge potential (~3.2V). We anticipate that this discovery will lead to future research into methods to synthesize and stabilize LiO2. This can open the avenue for high-energy density lithium superoxide-based batteries as well as other potential uses such as a source of oxygen storage. The

The voltage profile is shown in Fig. 3(c) for this discharge process, along with that of the initial discharge process (to 1000 mAh/g). In contrast, no significant capacity for discharge in Ar is observed for the rGO cathode when a similar procedure is done. We have also carried out EPR measurements and find a signal that is consistent with the presence of LiO2 (Fig. S7) in the initial discharge product. Thus, the HE-XRD measurements indicate that the crystalline LiO2 formed with the Ir-rGO cathode is surprisingly stable for a relatively long period of time. Raman spectra (Fig S8) measured as a function of time exhibit a decreasing intensity of the peak at 1123 cm -1 as a function of time, which is consistent with the HE-XRD results. In addition, LiO2 is still the dominant discharge product on the 20th discharge cycle indicating that LiO2 is stable enough that it can be repeatedly charged and discharged for about 40 cycles with a very low charge potential (~3.2 V) and may open the avenue for a lithium superoxide-based battery. An explanation for the formation mechanism and the stability of LiO2 found in this study requires an understanding of the growth and nucleation process, which is quite complex and beyond the scope of the present study. However, the results are consistent with mechanisms proposed in other recent studies. Some insight can be obtained from a postulated mechanism for the nucleation and growth of discharge products from various size-specific Ag clusters decorating carbon cathodes in a Li-O2 cell.10 In that case, the results are explained by a through-solution growth mechanism with a electron transfer site for oxygen reduction that is separate from the nucleation site. Other researchers have also reported evidence for through-solution mechanisms2629

. In addition, the results of the Ag cluster study,10 which is also based on a TEGDME

electrolyte, suggests that fast oxygen reduction will result in a through-solution nucleation and growth of LiO2 followed by disproportionation to Li2O2. Since both Ir and rGO are considered good ORR materials,29,30 the Ir-rGO-based cathodes should result in a similar mechanism, i.e. initial formation of LiO2. The HE-XRD finding that rGO results in both LiO2 and Li2O2 in the discharge product can be accounted for by slow disproportionation. This explanation is supported by a recent theoretical study17 that has shown the possibility of fast and slow disproportionation processes for LiO2 depending on the LiO2 cluster size. In addition, experimental evidence for slow disproportionation has been found in studies of Li-O2 cell discharge products based on an activated carbon cathode.14 In contrast, the HEXRD results showing that only LiO2 is present in the case of the Ir/rGO-based cathode material suggests disproportionation is suppressed. The formation of only LiO2 in the case of the Ir-rGO cathode may be due to some aspect of the Ir-rGO cathode that favors nucleation and growth of largely the crystalline LiO2 phase, which prevents disproportionation (see theoretical calculations below). In the case of Ir-rGO, we noticed the formation of an Ir3Li intermetallic compound on the large Ir agglomerates seen in the backscattering image (Fig. S1), as shown Figure 3(f)3(g). We also noticed that some nanoparticles (needle or rod-like) are formed on the surface of these agglomerates during the 1st discharge (Fig. 3(e)). It is interesting to point out that the Ir3Li intermetallic compound has an orthorhombic lattice, a similar crystallographic lattice32 to that of LiO2. It is possible that it may act as a template for growth of the crystalline LiO2 such as has been found in template-controlled nucleation and growth for other crystalline materials33,34. We carried out density functional theory (DFT) calculations on the interface between LiO2 and Ir3Li and found that some crystalline faces had good lattice matches (Fig. S13), as would be required for epitaxial growth results in the crystalline LiO2 growth. There may be other intermetallic compounds that exhibit similar behavior and will be the subject for further study. The schematic in Fig. 4 summarizes the novel templating process that may be responsible for the LiO2 discharge product found for the Ir-rGO cathode material and the subsequent electrochemical reactions that it can undergo, i.e. either further lithiation or further oxygen reduction. In contrast, the rGO cathode, which does not include Ir nanoparticles, probably has a different nucleation and growth mechanism resulting in a discharge product composed of both LiO2 and Li2O2. The kinetic stability of crystalline and amorphous LiO2 was investigated using ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) and DFT calculations with the results shown in Figure 4. The disproportionation rate will depend on several factors. One factor is the rate at which the O2 leaves the surface. The DFT results in Figure 4(b) indicate that the initial step of O2 leaving the clean surface into vacuum has a barrier of ~0.9 eV based on one of the low energy LiO2 surfaces. Figure 4(c) shows that crystalline LiO2 surfaces (i.e. (101) and (111)) are thermally stable in vacuum at room temperature. For an 7

Page 8

amorphous surface, the barrier is less (~0.3 eV) than for the crystalline surface (Fig. 4(d)). From AIMD simulations, the presence of some solvent molecules adsorbed on the amorphous LiO2 surface reduces O2 desorption (Fig. S14). This suggests that solvent on the LiO2 surface could further suppress disproportionation of the crystalline phase. The other factor controlling the disproportionation rate is the rate at which Li2O2 is formed from the resulting excess Li at the surface. The electrolyte effect on disproportionation was investigated by allowing a sample from a 1000 mAh/g discharge to age for 24 hours in vacuum. Characterization of the sample by Raman, discharge in Ar, and charge potential shows a significant decrease of LiO2 signature after aging in vacuum, indicating kinetics plays an important role in stabilizing the LiO2 (Fig. S15) The Ir-rGO cathode also exhibits a low charge potential, which may be due to several factors. As shown in Figure 4(e), crystalline LiO2 is a half-metal based on density functional calculations and, thus, will have good electronic conduction in contrast to insulating bulk Li2O2. Another factor is that Ir is known to be a good oxygen evolution catalyst31 and interacts strongly with LiO2 to form a good interface for electrical contact. These properties may explain why the discharge product formed on just rGO has a large charge potential, i.e., it lacks the Ir nanoparticles. The Li-O2 cell based on IrrGO cathode material also can cycle for 40 or more cycles (Fig. S9) before failure, similar to what has been found for Li2O2 based Li-O2 cells, indicating that the lithium superoxide is not any more reactive towards the electrolyte than lithium peroxide. In addition, the low charge potential will lead to less side reactions. The failure of the cell could be due to oxygen crossover to the anode resulting in the anode being converted to LiOH as evidenced by the corrosion of the anode (Fig. S10(a)) and, possibly the poisoning of Ir metal catalyst with cycling. When the cycled Li anode is replaced by a new anode, the cell cycles for another 30 cycles (Fig. S10(b)). The evidence presented in this paper indicates that a Li-O2 electrochemical cell based on a lithium superoxide discharge product is possible with a reasonable cycle life, very high efficiency, and a good capacity. The characteristics of the cell performance based on lithium superoxide are comparable to that of previously reported electrochemical cells based on potassium superoxide 3 and sodium superoxide1, 8

Issues with electrolyte stability and decomposition most likely still remain, similar to the electrolytes used for other LiO2 systems, but they do not seem any worse. discharge product is gone after charging. In summary, we have reported evidence it is possible have a one-electron discharge process that forms only lithium superoxide in a Li-O2 electrochemical cell. This is different from the previous studies13-15,35-39 that have provided evidence for both LiO2 and Li2O2 in the discharge product of Li-O2 batteries with some cathode and electrolyte materials and the studies40-42 that have shown LiO2 can be present in solution during discharge. The evidence for the existence of the LiO2 comes from DEMS, HE-XRD data with no evidence for Li2O2 being present. The results of TEM and density functional calculations indicate that a novel templating growth mechanism involving the use of Ir nanoparticles may be responsible for the crystalline LiO2 growth. The LiO2 formed in this way is stable enough to be repeatedly charged and discharged with a very low charge potential. Methods Material Preparation Graphene oxide (GO) was prepared by a modified Hummers method [1, 2]. Pristine rGO was produced by reducing GO in polyol [3]. Specifically, a GO dispersion (1 mg ml1

) in ethylene glycol (EG) was first prepared with the aid of horn sonication for 1 hr. The

solution pH was then adjusted to pH 13 with NaOH (2.5 M in EG). The reaction temperature was increased to 120 °C, and a NaBH4 reducing agent dissolved in EG was injected slowly. The solution remained reacted for 1 hr at this temperature and 17

Page 18

cooled to room temperature. The precipitates were filtered, washed and dried under vacuum. The Ir-rGO composite was synthesized by a hydrothermal method. The IrCl3•H2O was added to 100 ml aqueous GO solution (0.67 mg ml -1) and the mixture was stirred for 2 hrs. The resulting solution was transferred to the Teflon-lined autoclave and there reacted hydrothermally at 180 °C for 12 hrs. The precipitation was filtered, washed and dried under vacuum. Note: During hydrothermal treatment, Ir nanocrystals are obtained with simultaneous reduction of GO into rGO, without addition of strong base. After synthesis, the Ir-rGO and rGO powders were dried at 80 ℃ under vacuum for 24 hrs. To prepare the oxygen electrode, the Ir-rGO and rGO were intimately mixed in a N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) solution and a polyvinylidene fluoride binder (PVDF) with a weight ratio of 8:2. The slurry was coated on a gas-diffusion layer (TGP-H-030 carbon paper, Torray) and dried for 12 hrs at 100 °C under vacuum to remove the residual solvent. Electrochemical testing Electrochemical testing was carried out using a Swagelok-type cell composed of a lithium metal anode, electrolyte (1M LiCF3SO3 in tetraethylene glycol dimethyl ether (TEGDME) impregnated into a glass fiber separator), and a porous cathode (7/16 inch diameter). The cells were sealed except for the Al grid window that exposed the porous cathode to 1 bar O2 pressure. The electrochemical measurements were carried out using a MACCOR cycler. The discharge-charge performance was conducted in the voltage range of 2.2-4.5 V at a constant current of 100 mA/g, and the cell was maintained in 1 bar O2 atmosphere to avoid any negative effects of humidity and CO2. Characterization techniques (the DEMS technique is described with Figure S18) The phase structures of the discharge products were identified using high energy X-ray diffraction (HE-XRD) with a wavelength of 0.11165 Å, performed at beamline 11ID-C of Sector 11 at the Advanced Photon Source (APS) of Argonne National Laboratory. The X-ray specimens were sealed with Kapton tape as a protective film in the glove box to avoid any side reactions from the air. The HE-XRD patterns were collected in the 18

Page 19

transmission mode. During the course of the measurements, a high-energy X-ray beam hit the sample horizontally, and a 2D detector (Perkin Elmer large area detector) was used to collect the X-ray diffraction profiles using transmission mode. The 2D patterns were then integrated into conventional 1D patterns (intensity vs 2θ) for final data analysis using the Fit2d software. Scanning transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEOL JEM-2100F FEG FasTEM with an accelerating voltage of 80 kV) was employed to evaluate the morphology and particle size of the Ir catalysts and the discharge products on the porous cathodes. Spherical and chromatic aberration correction enables the microscope to reach the information limit better than 0.1 nm (measured by Young’s fringes) at 80 kV. To prepare the TEM specimens, a dilute suspension was prepared by ultrasonically dispersing the samples in ethanol for 5 min, and a drop of the suspension was placed onto a copper grid and dried. Particle size histograms were generated from the TEM images using software ImageJ. Field-emission scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Hitachi S-4700) coupled with backscattering electron imaging (BSE) was employed to determine the morphology and estimate the particle size of Ir catalyst and discharge products. Raman spectra of the discharged cathode were obtained using a Renishaw 2000 or inVia microscope spectrometer with a HeNe laser at exciting wavelength of 633 nm. The sample was loaded inside of a glove box to a gas-tight Raman cell with glass or quartz window. Raman spectrum collection was set up in a 180° reflective mode. Roughly 10% of the maximum 13 mW laser intensity was applied. Collection time constant setting varied from 30 to ~100 sec. Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) spectra were collected on a Bruker Avance III 500 MHz (11.7 T) spectrometer in deuterated tetrahydrofuran (THF-d8) or deuterium oxide (D2O). Battery samples were cycled the appropriate number of times before disassembly and rinsing with a deuterated solvent. For the samples in THF-d8, NMR samples were prepared entirely under an inert atmosphere. For samples in D2O, the battery was disassembled under an inert atmosphere, removed from the glove box, immediately rinsed with D2O, and sealed in an NMR tube. All 1H-NMR collections were sampled for 128 scans, manually phased, and baseline corrected prior to peak 19

integration. Data collection was performed using the Bruker Topspin software (v 3.1). Data analysis utilized either TopSpin (v 3.1) or SpinWorks (V4.1.0.0). Theoretical Methods To study the stability of LiO2 system (i.e. crystal, crystalline surfaces, amorphous-like thin film),its interface with a solvent and its surface growth on a intermetallic substrate (i.e. Ir3Li), we carried out Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations with plane wave basis sets as implemented in the VASP code [4, 5]. All the calculations were spinpolarized and carried out using the gradient corrected exchange-correlation functional of Perdew, Burke and Ernzerhof (PBE) [6] under the projector augmented wave (PAW) method, with plane wave basis sets up to a kinetic energy cutoff of 400 eV. The PAW method [7] was used to represent the interaction between the core and valence electrons, and the Kohn-Sham valence states (i.e. 1s for H, 2s for Li, 2s 2p for C and O, 6s 5d for Ir) are expanded in plane wave basis sets. For the geometry optimization and Nudge Elastic Band calculations, the convergence criterion of the total energy was set to be within 1x10-5 eV for the K-point integration, and all the atoms and geometries were optimized until the residual forces became less than 1x10 -2 eV/Å. For the LiO2 crystal, the calculation is based on a mesh of 9x9x9 in K-point grid. For both the crystalline and amorphous-like LiO2 thin film surfaces, the K-point grid of 6x6x1 were used. For the Ab Initio Molecular Dynamics (AIMD) simulations, all the calculations were carried out with the convergence criterion of the total energy set to be within 1x10 -4 eV in kinetic energy cutoff of 300 eV. For the simulations of LiO2 surface with some solvent molecules on it, the Van der Waals method of Grimme (i.e. DFT-D2) [8] is used throughout both the DFT and AIMD calculations. For the simulation of the solvent, a smaller ether solvent molecule, i.e. dimethoxy ethane (DME) is used instead of TEGDME in order to reduce the computational cost. To investigate the thermodynamic stability of the system at room temperature, all the structures from the DFT optimizations were then thermally equilibrated at T = 300 K using ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations based on an Nose-Hoover NVT-ensemble with a time step of 1 fs.

For the ab initio prediction of Electron Paramagnetic Resonance (EPR) g-tensor of the crystalline solids of LiO2 and NaO2, we have used the DFT planewave code Quantum 20

Page 21

Espresso simulation package [9] in which the Gauge-Including Projector Augmented Plane Wave (GIPAW) approach [10] has been implemented. To be consistent with our VASP calculations, we employed the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof functional (PBE) functional and Martins-Troullier norm-conserving pseudopotentials generated by Davide Ceresoli. Throughout the simulation, the planewave energy cutoff 150 Ry and k-point mesh (9x9x9) was used.

To predict the g-tensor that observed from our experimental EPR spectra for LiO2 solid at low temperature, only the NaO2 solid in Pnnm orthorhombic phase [11] that is thermodynamic stable at low temperature was considered in our DFT EPR calculation. For the ab initio prediction of EPR g-tensor of the LiO2 and NaO2 molecules, all the calculations were obtained using Gaussian09 code. In that cases, the accuracy of the theoretical predictions are systematically tested against using PBE and B3LYP functional and 6-31+G(2df,p) and aug-cc-PVQZ basis sets with direct comparison to the experimental LiO2 and NaO2 EPR g-tensor reported [12, 13].

Differential electrochemical mass spectroscopy (DEMS) measurements for charge DEMS measurements were carried out to examine the type of discharge product by measuring the evolved oxygen (O2) molecules during the charging process as well as to calculate the e-/O2 ratio.[17-19] The experiments were performed using an HPR40 (Hiden Analytical) instrument directly connected to a customized Swagelok battery setup.[17] The DEMS working pressure was < 5×10-8 torr. The charge DEMS experiment was performed using the setup configuration described in the calibration section below. At first, the Swagelok battery cell consisting of a fresh Ir-rGO coated GDL as a cathode, a Li chip as an anode, and a glass carbon fiber filter paper saturated in 1M LiCF3SO3 in TEGDME electrolyte was assembled in an Ar filled glovebox. The cell was discharged up to 1000 mAh/g under identical experimental conditions as discussed in the experimental section (i.e., 100 mA/g current density) in 1 atm O 2 (99.99% research grade). Next, the Swagelok battery was flushed with Ar for 15 minutes to remove excess O2 and other impurities from the cell and then connected to the DEMS. The DEMS was calibrated with 1, 2, 5, and 10% O2 mixed with Argon to quantify the evolved oxygen molecules during the charging process. Prior to the charging experiment, the DEMS was operated for a long time (e.g., two hours) to stabilize the 44

Page 45

mass spectroscopy measurement. Secondary electron multiplier (SEM) detection mode was selected for the experiments as it offers higher sensitivity and accuracy in comparison to the Faraday detection mode. Hydrogen, O 2 and CO2 signals were monitored simultaneously online. In steady state conditions, the H 2, O2 and CO2 signals remained constant confirming that the excess O2 was completely removed from the battery system. Next, we performed the charging cycle by applying current densities of 1000mA/g (125 µA.cm-2) and 640mA/g (80 µA.cm-2) for one and 1.6 hours, respectively, and continuously monitored the H2, O2 and CO2 molecules signals online for a real time measurement (Fig. 2(d) and supplementary Fig. S18)). (The higher current densities for charge than discharge (100 mA/g) were used because at the lower current density it was difficult to accurately detect the amount of oxygen released.) An immediate rise was observed in the O2 signal (see left side of Fig. 2(d) and Fig. S18) confirming the evolution of O2 molecules as a result of discharge product decomposition from charging. However, we did not notice any change in the SEM signals for H 2 and CO2 throughout the experiment. The results were observed for the entire charging process until the maximum capacity (1000 mAh/g) was achieved at the potential of 3.60 and 3.43 V for 1000 mA/g and 640 mA/g current densities, respectively. Based on DEMS O2 calibration data, our analysis of the data in Fig. S18 indicates that an average of 8.3×10-10 mole/s O2 for 640 mA/g was evolved during the charging process, which is equivalent to 1.00 electrons per O2 molecule. An average of 1.3 × 10-9 mole/s of O2 formation resulting in a 1.00 e/O2 ratio was found for the charge at higher current density (1000mA/g) in Fig 2d. Moreover, calculations indicate that 182.2 and 181.8 µg of LiO2 were decomposed during the charge process for 1000 and 640 mA/g, respectively.
