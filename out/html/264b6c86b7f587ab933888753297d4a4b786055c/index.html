ABSTRACT: We present a brief overview of current developments in the field of polymer electrolytes, with the focus on single-ion conducting polymers. The latter have significant advantages relative to electrolytes with dual-ion conductivity for use in energy storage devices. First we discuss various mechanisms of ionic conductivity in polymers and fundamental limitations imposed by these mechanisms. We also emphasize the role of ion-ion correlations in conductivity that are not negligible in concentrated and semi-dilute ionic systems. Next we discuss several classes of polymer electrolytes (gels, block copolymers, composites) that are already employed or have good potential to be employed in current battery technologies. At the end we discuss perspectives for the design of dry single-ion conducting polymer electrolytes with required room temperature conductivity.

KEYWORDS: Polymer electrolytes, ionic conductivity, polymerized ionic liquids

1. INTRODUCTION Polymer electrolytes represent a broad class of materials with long polymeric chains and relatively high ion concentrations. Interest in these materials has a long history due to their potential applications in many current and future technologies.1-19 These include energy storage (e.g., regular and flow batteries), 3-8 energy conversion (e.g., fuel cells), 9-12 electrochromic devices,13-15 sensors, and actuators, 16 separations (e.g., gas separation) and water desalination/purification, 1719 and many other technologies.

A significant portion of polymer electrolyte research is related to their use in batteries. Most current batteries are based on organic liquid electrolytes that are flammable and toxic, and have a limited electrochemical stability window and no resistance to dendrite growth.20-22 Solid electrolytes can mitigate most of these problems and drawbacks.22-23 Moreover, they can enable some novel battery chemistry24-25—for instance, high-energy solid cathodic and anodic materials (e.g., Li-S)—which is not possible with liquid electrolytes.26 There are many superionic ceramics (glassy and crystalline) that provide sufficient Li+ or Na+ conductivity.27-28 However, they are brittle, have poor contact with electrodes, and often fail after a few charge/discharge cycles.22, 29-30 Solid polymer electrolytes present potentially the best solution for batteries because of their flexibility, good mechanical properties, adhesion to electrodes, and potential to compensate for electrode volume changes.3, 31-33 However, despite several decades of study, the conductivity of dry polymer electrolytes remains below the level required for many applications, ~10-3 S/cm,3, 22, 34 which strongly limits the battery power. Work on polymer electrolytes started in the 1970s with the discovery of ionic conductivity in poly(ethylene oxide) (PEO) with added salt.35-36 Since that time, PEO has been considered a benchmark for other polymer electrolytes. Its advantage is its very high solubility for salts without phase separation. But it has the disadvantage of crystallization at temperatures below ~60C; and at ambient temperatures, even the amorphous phase provides ionic conductivity several orders of magnitude lower than is required.1, 3, 37 Moreover, PEO usually has a very low Li+ transport number (not to be confused with transference number 38)—i.e., the fraction of the current transported by the ion, tLi+ ~0.2–0.3 1, 3—indicating that small Li ions in PEO move slower than much larger anions, such as PF6 or bis(trifluoromethane)sulfonamide (TFSI). Indeed, analysis of the single-ion conducting polymers collected in a recent review46 revealed that most of them exhibit high Li transport numbers, tLi+ ~ 0.8–0.95. To accelerate segmental dynamics at ambient temperature, significant efforts have been related to the development of polymer electrolytes with low glass transition temperatures, Tg. Indeed, the required rate of segmental relaxation can be achieved in the low-Tg polymers, such as poly(dimethyl siloxane) (PDMS) and 1,4-polybutadiene. These polymers, however, cannot dissolve salts. Any modification of the polymers, and addition of ions, leads to a significant increase in Tg and slowing of segmental relaxation at ambient temperature. Thus, employing the liquid-like mechanism has fundamental limitations and is unlikely to provide the required level of ionic conductivity in dry polymer electrolytes at ambient temperature. Strong acceleration of polymer segmental dynamics can be achieved by adding small molecular plasticizers. Only gel polymer electrolytes currently provide the required room-temperature conductivity, and they are already used in some commercial batteries. At the same time, many superionic glasses provide significantly higher conductivity, even exceeding the required level of 10-3 S/cm at ambient temperature. This raises a fundamental question: why can the same conductivity not be achieved in dry polymers? Our analysis reveals that the fundamental limitation of ionic conductivity in dry polymer electrolytes is based on their relatively slow segmental dynamics, low dielectric constant, and limited concentration of mobile ions. As a result, the rate of ion jumps in polymer electrolytes remains low and insufficient to provide the required conductivity. Currently two approaches are used in battery applications of polymer electrolytes: (1) the use of gel polymer electrolytes, whereby ionic conductivity is accelerated through the liquid phase, and (2) the use of high temperatures so that the conductivity of dry polymer electrolytes becomes sufficiently high. Both approaches have significant drawbacks. The question remains as to whether we can reach the required conductivity in dry polymer electrolytes at ambient temperature. Based on the presented analysis, we propose two strategies for the design of dry polymer electrolytes with enhanced ionic conductivity. The first strategy focuses on the reduction of electrostatic interactions that might be achieved by a significant increase in the high-frequency (MHz–GHz range) dielectric permittivity or a strong delocalization of anion charge. The second strategy is based on creating nanoscale ionic channels with high local concentrations of ions that should reduce the energy barrier for small cation transport and reverse the role of ion-ion correlations. As is discussed in Section 4, a possible reason that ion-ion correlations enhance ionic conductivity in superionic ceramics maybe formation of one-dimensional ion channels. The formation of similar channels in polymer electrolytes might lead to similar conductivity enhancements. We believe that it should be possible to synthesize these kinds of PolyILs and thus address the challenging task of improving ionic conductivity. An alternative and maybe even more promising way of designing solid polymer-based electrolytes is the use of composite materials. Reducing the interfacial energy barriers for cation transport crossing from superionic ceramic to the polymer matrix remains the main challenge in this case. Overcoming these challenges seems feasible. This will pave the way to solid state batteries with significantly improved performance and safety.
