ABSTRACT

Substantial progress has been made in reducing proton-exchange-membrane fuel cell (PEMFC) cathode platinum loadings from 0.4-0.8 mgPt/cm2 to about 0.1 mgPt/cm2. However, at this level of cathode Pt loading, large performance loss is observed at high-current density (>1 A/cm2), preventing a reduction in the overall stack cost. This next developmental step is being limited by the presence of a resistance term exhibited at these lower Pt loadings and apparently due to a phenomenon at or near the catalyst surface. This issue can be addressed through the design of catalysts with high and stable Pt dispersion as well as through development and implementation of ionomers designed to interact with Pt in a way that does not constrain oxygen reduction reaction rates. Extrapolating from progress made in past decades, we are optimistic that the concerted efforts of materials and electrode designers can resolve this issue, thus enabling a large step towards mass-market-affordable fuel cell vehicles.

Thanks to substantial research and development in the last two decades, automotive manufacturers are now bringing proton-exchange membrane (PEM) fuel-cell electric vehicles (FCEV) to the market. Engineers have developed fuel-cell systems meeting automotive performance and durability requirements as well as resolved vehicle-integration challenges such as packaging, safety, and cold start. However, the fuel-cell system costs are still high when compared to the internal-combustion engine (ICE) incumbent. Some of the current high fuel-cell-system cost arises from manufacturing issues amenable to major reduction through economies of scale. However, precious metal costs, primarily Pt, in the electrocatalysts would not benefit from the economy of scale and might even increase at higher FCEV market penetration. Thus, the execution of a technical roadmap to reduce PEMFC Pt-use requirements is vital to the ultimate realization of the fuel-cell-vehicle market. The US Department of Energy (DOE) set a platinum-group-metal (PGM) target of 0.125 kWrated/gPGM or about 11.3 gPGM per mid-size-sedan-vehicle (90 kWgross) by year 2020.1 As a reference, the current clean light-duty ICE-vehicle catalytic converter generally requires <5 gPGM.2 In gasoline vehicles, the majority of Pt is often replaced by other less expensive PGMs. However, this degree of Pt reduction is not yet possible in lean-burn diesel vehicles. A study by Roland Berger concluded that, including Pt supply-demand factors, FCEVs would remain less than a few percent of global vehicle production unless the use of Pt was reduced to less than 10 gPt/vehicle.3 Thus, considering both the costto-consumer and the supply-demand relation in the precious metal market, a stretch target of 0.0625 gPGM/kWgross (~6 gPGM/vehicle) appears appropriate. The ultimate goal of PEM fuel-cell-catalyst research is to entirely eliminate PGM. Solutions that pack as many active sites as possible into carbon-nitrogen-based non-PGM catalysts resulted in breakthrough in ORR activity at beginning of life (BOL).4–6 Considering only the ORR activity at low current density (<0.1 A/cm2), some non-PGM catalysts demonstrated comparable performance to Ptbased catalysts. However, at their present stage, non-PGM catalysts suffer from poor stability and poor performance at high power, the latter due to severe transport limitations due to larger thickness of the electrodes (~100 vs. ~10 microns for PGM-based electrodes) that is required to provide acceptable ORR activity and reduce H2O2 production yield. Their useable power density (~0.1 Wrated/cm2) is about onetenth of the PGM catalyst system; this makes vehicle packaging impractical as it is the power density that sets the size of the fuel cell stack.7 In addition, if further PGM reduction is successful (<6 gPGM/vehicle), the cost of PGM itself will become a small fraction of the catalyst cost, making it unnecessary to entirely remove PGM. In a 2005 often-cited review paper, Gasteiger et al. described testing methods, benchmarks, and a development roadmap leading to 0.2 gPGM/kWrated for automotive PEMFC cathode catalysts,8 reductions to 22.5 gPGM/vehicle as shown in the first row of Table 1. The metric emphasized in that paper was oxygen-reduction-reaction (ORR) activity at low current density (<0.1 A/cm2), a characteristic closely tied to the system efficiency at low power (<10% of maximum). Under these conditions, kinetics dominate the electrode behavior and O2 and proton transport losses are usually minor. Specifically, a target ORR mass activity of 0.44 A/mgPt at 0.9 VRHE was set to enable a Pt loading of 0.1 mgPt/cm2 based on low-power efficiency targets. (The mass activity is the product of specific activity (A/cm2) and

The mass activity is the characteristic catalyst activity parameter that most directly translates to catalyst cost, since Pt is purchased by weight.) As we will describe below, tremendous progress has been made along that roadmap, and the next segment of the journey, to <0.2 gPGM/kWrated (<22.5 gPGM/vehicle), can now be envisioned. The primary challenge of this next phase involves improving the high-power performance of the cathode, as this in turn determines the fuel cell stack size and cost for a given power requirement. Recent Progress. In this section, we overview the important progress in ORR catalyst development for PEM application achieved in the past 10 years. Scientists used several innovative approaches to improve either the dispersion of Pt, the area-specific activity, or both. Brookhaven National Lab pioneered the development of a Pt-monolayer catalyst family, essentially placing all of the Pt atoms on the particle surface so that minimal Pt is wasted in the bulk of the nanoparticle.10,11 Argonne National Lab demonstrated spectacular ORR activity on a ‘Pt-skin’ overlaying an optimally modulated subsurface 6

Page 7

structure PtNi(111) single crystal.12 This phenomenon is being exploited in several Pt-alloy catalyst systems. For example, advancement in nanoparticle synthesis led to the production of shape-controlled (preferential (111) facets) and optimally sized PtNi nanoparticles with excellent ORR activity in ex-situ rotating-disk electrode (RDE) measurements.13–17 Even in more traditional spherical binary Pt-alloy (PtNi, PtCo) systems, it was shown that notable improvement in ORR activity and durability could be achieved by manipulating the structures of the Pt skin and subsurface alloy composition.18–21 This type of Pt-alloy catalyst has become available from a few catalyst manufacturers and is reported to be used in the Toyota Mirai FCEV.22 Not all promising RDE ORR activity results have translated to real-world membrane-electrodeassembly (MEA) performance. For example, despite the spectacular RDE results with size and shapecontrolled catalysts of up to a factor of 20 in ORR mass activity over that of Pt/C13–17,23,24, these catalysts have not yet produced encouraging data in MEA tests.15,25 Although both RDE and MEA are measured at nominally the same 0.9 V and 1 atm O2, the absolute values from the two methods often do not agree. RDE values are usually higher than those measured in MEAs,26 but in some cases the best catalysts (some Pt-alloy catalysts) can exhibit lower values in RDE.27 Differences in measurement parameters such as temperature and hold time often vary between RDE and MEA measurements, making it difficult to compare.28 The risk of RDE-MEA discrepancy demonstrates the need to include MEA development in early stages of catalyst material development. Stability is another key factor affecting the practical implementation of promising catalyst materials.

Many highly-active catalysts are also quite unstable, changing their morphology and

composition as the electrodes are prepared and/or the measurements are being done. For example, we have tested shape-controlled (e.g. octahedral) PtNi catalysts from several labs, either provided13–16 or prepared according to published procedures,17,23,24 and all of them lost Ni quickly after being made into MEAs, leading to poor activity and stability. These materials exhibited significantly lower stability, losing Ni more quickly than a conventional alloy catalyst as shown in Figure 2. We also found that the octahedrons lost their distinct shape quite quickly, likely accelerated by the Ni loss.29 Considering that 7

The Pt-alloy catalyst provides another excellent example of why it is important to incorporate MEA testing with catalyst development. These catalysts are prepared by removing the majority of transition metal (such as Ni or Co) from the surface of a Pt-alloy nanoparticle using a chemical or electrochemical process, forming a Pt-rich shell covering a transition metal-rich Pt-alloy core.30–32 (These materials are often referred to as dealloyed catalysts because of this critical step that enables relatively stable MEA performance, but we will refer to them simply as Pt-alloy catalysts in this paper.) An appropriate Pt-skin thickness and modulated subsurface structure induce geometric and electronic effects that enhance ORR activity. RDE studies showed that a Pt shell thickness of 1-3 monolayers provides the

This turned out to be about 4-7 monolayers for PtNi and PtCo catalysts in MEAs. We also found that uniform particle size and low particle porosity were also important. If one were to use a cathode with 0.05 mgPt/cm2 Pt loading, the voltage loss due to RO2Pt would double and the others would not be impacted. Furthermore, ionomer and gas-diffusion layer components have been the focus of significant development, and it is difficult to further reduce losses from proton resistance in the cathode catalyst layer (ηH+CCL, blue) or oxygen transport in gas-diffusion layer (ηO2GDL, red). Thus, it is clear that we need to take on the challenge of characterizing and mitigating this apparent resistance at or near the Pt surface. To quantitatively evaluate the local O2 resistance, RO2Pt, an O2 limiting current test can be used.36,42 In this measurement, O2 concentration and total pressure are systematically varied in order to separate the O2 transport resistance into Fickian (RF) and non-Fickian (RNF) components. RF is primarily due to intermolecular gas diffusion through larger pores (>100 nm diameter), while RNF is due to either Knudsen diffusion in small pores (<100 nm diameter) or diffusion through liquid water or ionomer films. It is RNF that strongly correlates with HCD performance. Implications for Catalyst and Electrode Developers. Considering the local O2 resistance, we can now recognize that the ORR mass activity (measured at LCD) does not account for a key catalyst characteristic that strongly impacts the HCD portion of the polarization curve.36 Specifically, the dispersion of the catalyst (ECSA) becomes critical, as this determines the r.f. for a given Pt areal loading in the electrode design. Figure 7 shows HCD (2 A/cm2) voltage predictions using an available fuel cell model for a catalyst loading of 0.1 mgPt/cm2.48

Lines of constant potential are shown vs. two key catalyst

characteristics under catalyst designer control -- the ORR mass activity and the dispersion (ECSA) of the catalyst. For catalysts with a given mass activity, a catalyst with higher ECSA has better voltage performance, because of the lower required local O2 flux to the Pt surface. With a local O2 resistance, RO2Pt, of 12 s/cm used in the simulation, the voltage drops precipitously when the ECSA is less than 40 m2/gPt. Such catalysts will suffer large loss at high power (HCD) and, even with adequate mass activity, fall short of meeting vehicle high-power requirements. Because the stack must meet high-power requirements throughout its service life, it is the mass activity and ECSA at end-of-life that ultimately determine the amount of Pt required in the cathode.

Pt-alloy catalysts, as described earlier, have already shown great promise in meeting requirements for further cost reduction. MEA results from PtNi and PtCo catalysts are provided in Figure 8. The initial

Page 17

performance is quite high but Pt surface area decreases noticeably after durability testing. Materials development that mitigates Pt dissolution and studies to improve understanding of its degradation mechanism can help extend the life of these catalysts. Decreasing the amount of Pt inside the core by either reducing the particle size or replacing the Pt in the interior with another non-PGM element, while maintaining its stability, can further improve its Pt surface area and mass activity. As shown in the figure for the Pt alloy material family, such incremental improvements in mass activity and ECSA with stepchange improvements in stability provides an approach to stack current density rating at 2 A/cm2 with only 0.05 mgPt/cm2 cathode loading (blue dashed line target). This material improvement would enable a significant improvement in required stack area, and thus cost, for a given power requirement. As discussed earlier in Figure 6, ionomer-free NSTF shows very small local resistance and acceptable HCD performance,26,44,45 and hence one might expect it not to be bound by the same restriction shown in Figure 8. However, we found that in a relatively short period of time, decomposed ionomer can adsorb on its catalyst surface and cause a rise in local resistance to the same level of other carbonsupported catalysts.47 Unless this can be prevented, an effort to significantly enhance ECSA is needed to make it more tolerant to voltage loss. The current NSTF catalyst uses a line-of-sight sputtering technique for Pt deposition which causes a non-uniform Pt coating with average Pt thickness of 3-8 nm and a low ECSA of 10-20 m2/gPt. This could be improved somewhat, but due to the high surface energy of Pt (making it difficult for Pt to wet other surfaces),52 it does not appear possible to reduce the Pt thickness below 2 nm. This gives an upper boundary for Pt ECSA of 25 m2/gPt, and the corresponding position on Figure 8 reveals that the HCD challenge discussed here disadvantages the NSTF system relative to more high dispersed catalyst systems. In RDE studies, octrahedral PtNi has exhibited similar ECSA as compared to Pt alloy catalysts but with significantly higher mass activity. However, the results have not yet been observed in an MEA; this is shown by the corresponding open diamonds in Figure 8, which fall below Pt-alloy BOL performance. As discussed, stability of this material family is currently unacceptable and its key developmental need. This challenge appears heightened due to thermodynamic instability of these sharply 17

Page 18

faceted particles in the acidic fuel cell system. From our perspective, it is too early to project the potential for this early-stage technology, so there is no corresponding shaded area on Figure 8. By placing essentially all Pt atoms on the surface, the Pt monolayer approach provides a great potential of achieving the lowest Pt loading as shown on Figure 8. Indeed, we found that Pt monolayer catalysts can match the HCD performance of a Pt-alloy/C catalyst at one half of the Pt loading thanks to its high Pt ECSA.53 Thus far, however, MEA testing on catalysts with Pt monolayer on several cores including Pd and PdAu have not shown sufficient Pt surface area retention.11 Pd was shown to leach out from the core quite readily resulting in destabilization of the Pt monolayer. Furthermore, heavy use of a relatively expensive Pd in the core lessens its economic benefit. Recent efforts to replace PGM in the core with refractory metals, particularly electrodeposited WNi, produced a catalyst with encouraging ECSA and mass activity retention, although the fuel cell performance was poor.54 Given the strong position of this catalyst family as shown on Figure 8, it clearly warrants further studies on both core and shell development. Of particular interest is the how the subsurface composition affects the stability and activity of these catalysts. In this section, we have considered how tailoring catalyst properties can enable their use at high power. However, if one could reduce the apparent RO2Pt, it would move the dashed lines in Figure 8 to the left, enabling the high-power capability of more catalyst materials. The RO2Pt value is an engineering parameter useful to correlate the data, but the actual physics/chemistry on which it is based are unclear. In the next section, we turn our attention to the fundamentals behind this parameter. Our ultimate goal is to fundamentally understand and mitigate this resistance with materials or processes in order to increase the catalyst material approaches able to provide high power at lower stack cost.

Fundamentals of Electrolyte-Pt Interface. Despite substantial efforts to develop new ionomers for fuel cell membranes, ionomer development for use in the electrodes has been scarce. This is largely because the commercial incentive is rather small due to its small volume (<50 gm/vehicle and about 10-20% of the membrane volume) and because the performance limitation of the ionomer has not been recognized 18

Page 19

until quite recently when the low-Pt-loaded electrode became a subject of research focus.

It is

understandable that a different ionomer designed for electrode use could be valuable. For example, gas permeability requirements for ionomers in the membrane (desired low for high fuel cell efficiency) and electrode (desired high for minimum resistance) and are very different. The local O2 resistance also signals the potential value of electrode-specific ionomers. As described earlier (Fig. 6), ionomer-coated NSTF electrode provides strong evidence that the 24 nm thin ionomer film on Pt is the source of the local O2 transport resistance that causes the performance limitation at HCD in low-Pt-loaded cathodes. Unpublished work by David Caulk in our lab showed that the resistance decreased with increasing temperature and humidity in proportions similar to that observed with a thick ionomer membrane. However, if one calculates an O2 transport resistance of the thin film from known O2 permeability of a thick membrane, the resistance is only 1-2 s/cm, a factor of 3-10 smaller than the resistance measured in a catalyst layer (Figure 6, 6-12 s/cm). Thus, it appears that the resistance must be associated with a unique property of ionomer thin films or due to the pore/ionomer or Pt/ionomer interface. The hypothesis that thin film ionomer has a different structure than its bulk36,39 has led to attempts to characterize the physical properties of thin ionomer films.55–61 Various groups have found that ionomer thin films exhibit lower water uptake and uptake rate compared to its bulk membrane and that the substrate/ionomer interaction could control the reorganization of the ionomer nanostructure.55–60 One particularly interesting finding was that as the film thickness was reduced to approximately 20 nm it showed loss of phase-separation.57 Thin ionomer also showed an order of magnitude increase in elastic modulus. This stiffening was attributed to the confinement effects in the thin film in which the polymer and water domains cannot randomly orient themselves as in the bulk. Since phase-segregation into hydrophobic and water domains in bulk perfluorosulfonic acid (PFSA) membrane is believed to be an important feature that gives rise to the superior proton conductivity as well as water and O2 transport as compared to other non-PFSA membranes,62,63 loss of this feature is likely to lead to increases in O2 transport resistance. 19

Page 20

A recent ex-situ measurement by Litster and coworkers indicates, however, that decreased O2 permeability of ionomer thin films cannot alone be responsible for the local O2 resistance. Their work showed that there was no such increase in thin ionomer O2 resistance when ionomer films were coated on a polycarbonate substrate and not in direct contact with a Pt surface.64 This is an important finding because it indicates that ionomer interaction with Pt surface is a key to the increased resistance. This logic path indicates that chemical interaction of the ionomer/Pt interface needs to be understood and optimized. Sulfonate groups on the ionomer have been found to adsorb on Pt and reduce ORR activity.65,66 The adsorption of the acid group also immobilizes the ionomer chain reducing its degree of freedom and further confining the ionomer.67–69 Indeed, Ono et al. has shown in MEAs that higher acid group concentration (lower equivalent-weight ionomer) in the cathodes increased their apparent local O2 resistance.70 Kodama et al. found that some super acids such as sulfonamide have lower adsorption energy on Pt surface although their benefit has not been demonstrated.71 Coincidently, Snyder et al. also found that when a room-temperature ionic liquid with sulfonamide anion was coated on a PtNialloy catalyst, ORR activity increased by a factor of 2-3.23,72 Jomori et al. studied the effect of different ionomers on the low current density decay and found that ionomers with short side chain and rigid backbone showed less performance decay.73 In summary, these are encouraging results suggesting that tailoring ionomer structure may be an effective approach to reducing local O2 resistance. Recent molecular dynamics and DFT simulation by Jinnouchi et al. showed that ionomers fold to enlarge the polymer/metal attractive interaction induced by the large number of CF2 groups.74 This leads to a highly dense layer adjacent to the Pt surface and in turn reduces O2 concentration close to the Pt surface to nearly zero (gray line in Figure 9). In a preliminary calculation, they also found that local O2 resistance increased by about a factor of two when the sulfonate group concentration was increased (equivalent weight of 1150 to 750 gm/molacid), in good agreement with experimental results.70

Figure 10a illustrates a reasonable hypothesis on why ionomer thin film exhibits high O2 resistance in the presence of Pt. The thin ionomer films (1-10 nm thick) loses its ability to phase segregate and form polymer and water domains due to a confinement effect as well as favorable adsorption on the Pt surface, leading to a stiffer backbone and increases in O2 and water transport resistances. Adsorption of sulfonic acid groups on the Pt surface immobilizes the ionomer and further lowers its degree of freedom. As a result, O2 concentration near the Pt surface is reduced and its permeation path is disrupted, leading to increased O2 resistance. One expects the following to improve local O2 transport: (a) reducing the concentration of sulfonic acid groups, (b) restricting the mobility of sulfonic acid group by using shorter side chain ionomer, (c) replacing sulfonic acid with a more weakly adsorbing group, or (d) modifying the ionomer main chain to avoid dense aggregation. Some MEA results70,73 support these hypotheses.

Attempts to improve O2 permeability through a more open structure ionomer are also being pursued with encouraging results.75–77 A basic strategy is to insert a small cyclical ring into the backbone in order to create a sterically enhanced O2 permeability (Fig. 10b). It was shown that this strategy could increase O2 permeability by a factor of 2-20 in a bulk membrane. Its effect on local O2 transport has not yet been well documented. How the new ionomer distributes in the electrode, its effective proton conductivity in an electrode, and its stability will be the focus of future study. Overall, we see that the development of ionomers designed for electrode, specifically cathode, application is a promising research frontier. When combined with improvements in catalyst materials guided by the principles illustrated in Figure 8, there is reason for optimism that a target of <5 gPGM/vehicle can be achieved.

Benchmarking Guideline and Target. In order to facilitate research in this important area, we provide in the SI a guideline for testing and generating representative performance data for use by catalyst and materials developers. Instead of the commonly used approach of testing under automotive-systemrelevant conditions with O2 stoichiometry of 2 or less, we propose testing under ‘differential’ conditions (i.e., with high gas flows giving >10 O2 and H2 stoichiometry) to eliminate variations in reactant concentrations and water content in the flow channel. Furthermore, we propose use of a standard 5 cm2

Page 23

flowfield and fully humidified conditions to minimize the membrane and electrode ionomer voltage loss as well as the risk of test stand errors due to humidification inaccuracy. We found that the effect of membrane thickness is negligible as long as the membrane is 25 μm or less. The test platform and welldefined conditions will allow more accurate evaluations of electrode performance as well as reliable determination of RO2Pt. For ease and consistency of comparisons, we encourage the community to use this approach when developing new catalysts, electrodes, or gas-diffusion layers. The possible improvements in the polarization curve are clearly very significant, translating to stack cost reduction of 25% or more (see SI) and further illustrating the importance of this research frontier.

Summary of High-Impact Research Directions. Several promising high-activity cathode catalyst families have been developed and demonstrated in RDEs. Although these materials are often found not to be sufficiently stable for use in a fuel cell, development of stabilization concepts and associated material development warrant further research effort. However, one must include rigorous MEA testing and characterization early in the development process as was demonstrated in the last decade with the Pt alloy catalysts. Recent studies with low-Pt-loaded electrodes have shed light on the importance and source of the local O2 transport resistance. The fact that this resistance scales inversely with Pt surface area leads to the requirement that a sufficiently high ECSA is needed to meet high power performance requirements. Thus it is not sufficient for catalyst developers to focus only on activity and durability, but concerted effort is needed to maximize the ECSA to ensure that O2 transport to the Pt at high current density is not a major source of voltage loss. 24

Page 25

Perhaps the least previously explored yet potentially very high-impact opportunity is electrode ionomer development. To reduce the local O2 transport resistance, ionomer with open structure for O2 permeation is preferred. Chemical structure optimization is warranted through use of an acid group that adsorbs more weakly on Pt as well as development of ionomer structures with restricted side chain and main chain mobility. Methods to quantify these properties and their Pt interaction impact also need to be further developed. If one or more of these challenges were resolved, Pt-cost for a fuel cell would be expected to rival the PGM-cost-equivalent in catalytic convertors for internal combustion engine vehicles. Additional fuel cell stack cost reduction would also be possible through the reduced stack area and material cost (e.g., plate, membrane, diffusion media) needed to meet the high power requirement. Such an accomplishment would greatly enable the mass production of a commercially viable fuel cell vehicle and facilitate the development of a sustainable CO2-emission-free transportation system.
