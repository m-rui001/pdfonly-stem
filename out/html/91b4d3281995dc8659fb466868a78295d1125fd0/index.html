Enhanced Efficiency and Stability of Inverted Perovskite Solar Cells Using Highly Crystalline SnO2 Nanocrystals as the Robust Electron-Transporting Layer Zonglong Zhu, Yang Bai, Xiao Liu, Chu-Chen Chueh,* Shihe Yang, and Alex K.-Y. Jen* Since their debut in 2009, organic–inorganic hybrid perovskite solar cells (PVSCs) have attracted tremendous research attention in the photovoltaic field and high power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) of >20% have been accomplished.[1] The perovskites (CH3NH3PbX3 (denoted as MAPbX3), X = I−,Br−, or Cl−) have been shown to possess the combined facile solution processability and exceptional semiconducting properties inherent to organic and inorganic semiconductors, respectively.[2] This results in high PCE PVSC near that of prevailing inorganic technologies such as silicon, CdTe, and copper indium gallium selenide (CIGS) but can be obtained through facile solution process at much lower temperatures, which has great advantages for next generation cost-competitive photovoltaic technology to address the scalability issue.[3] Given that the record PCE of PVSC has reached 21%,[4] the major challenge for bringing this technology for commercialization lies in its long-term environmental stability. Currently, the state-of-the-art PVSC is realized in a conventional n-i-p device configuration consisting of an n-type titanium oxide semiconductor capped with the perovskite absorber and a holetransporting layer (HTL).[5] However, the inverted p-i-n planar PVSC has also gained significant traction due to its better compatibility to high throughput manufacturing processes. In an inverted PVSC, the perovskite active layer is similarly sandwiched between an HTL and an electron-transporting layer (ETL) but with a reverse device polarity compared to the conventional structure.[6] Thus far, the most commonly used charge-transporting interlayers (CTLs) in such device configuration are based on poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene):poly(styrenesulfonic acid) (PEDOT:PSS) and phenyl-C61-butyric acid methyl ester (PCBM) to extract dissociated holes and electrons from perovskite absorber, respectively.[7] Based on this CTL combination, the

Here, we have successfully exploited the solution-processed SnO2 nanocrystals (NCs) to serve as an efficient ETL in the inverted thin-film PVSC. It has been recently suggested that SnO2,[14] compared to ZnO,[15] TiO2.[16] and other metal oxides,[17] appears to be a better ETL for PVSCs due to its high mobility, wide bandgap (≈3.8 eV), and low trap density. We first utilized the hydrothermal method to prepare highly crystalline SnO2 NCs and employed them as an ETL in PVSCs. Due to their high crystallinity, SnO2 NCs can preserve efficient electron transport in the derived devices at a relatively thick thickness of ≈120 nm. Consequently, a very compact barrier against oxygen and water can be formed on top of device to significantly improve ambient stability of the resulting devices. Finally, a high-performance (PCE: 18.8%) inverted PVSC is demonstrated with a remarkable ambient stability, which can

Originally, the SnO2 NCs were prepared though the typical sol–gel hydrolysis method; however, the paucity of characteristic peaks shown in the X-ray diffraction (XRD) (Figure 1A) indicates the inferior crystallinity of the prepared NCs (denoted as sol–gel SnO2). It has been documented that the crystallinity of SnO2 NCs synthesized by such way is strongly correlated with the hydrolytic rate of Sn4+ with tetramethylammonium hydroxide (TMAH) in a basic environment.[18] Hence, the hydrothermal method was adopted to prepare the SnO2 NCs (denoted as hydrothermal SnO2) since it has been reported to facilitate the growth of highly crystalline SnO2 NCs under high temperature (200 °C) and pressure in an autoclave.[18] Encouragingly, as clearly shown in Figure 1A, the hydrothermal SnO2 NPs exhibit intense characteristic peaks that are consistent with the tetragonal phase of SnO2 mesoporous single crystals (MSCs), wherein all of the conspicuous peaks are well-matched with the standard pattern of JCPDS No. 01-077-0447. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) were then conducted to characterize the morphology and crystallinity of the prepared SnO2 NCs. The SEM image of SnO2 NCs layer on a perovskite film capped with a thin C60 layer is shown in Figure 1B. As can be seen, a homogeneous SnO2 NCs layer with a grain size of 5–10 nm

(inset of Figure 1B) can be formed uniformly. The TEM image of SnO2 NCs presented in Figure 1C confirms the particle sizes of 5–10 nm and its corresponding electron diffraction pattern (inset of Figure 1C) reveals the polycrystalline structure of the hydrothermal SnO2 NCs. Figure 1D exhibits the high-resolution TEM of the hydrothermal SnO2 NCs, which further affirms their high crystallinity. As shown, the outer d spacing is measured to be 0.333 nm corresponding to the interplanar spacing of (110) plane of SnO2 MSCs. As compared to the hydrothermal SnO2 NCs, the sol–gel SnO2 clearly shows a less crystalline feature (Figure S1, Supporting Information), consistent to the XRD result shown in Figure 1A. To examine the efficacy of these prepared SnO2 NCs serving as an ETL in PVSC, inverted p-i-n planar devices with the configuration of fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO)/NiO/MAPbI3/ studied ETLs/Ag are fabricated. It is important to note that such thick ETL is an appealing feature to provide better tolerance for rollto-roll printing, given that the thickness of the commonly used ETLs is <100 nm.[6] In addition to the better processability, the robust SnO2 ETL is also very useful for making monolithic tandem device since it will not only be a good interconnecting layer but also be able to prevent the underlying layer from being eroded during the deposition of sequential layers. In summary, we have successfully demonstrated a facile solution-processed SnO2 ETL for high-performance inverted p-i-n planar PVSC. These SnO2 NCs prepared by hydrothermal method is highly crystalline thus can afford efficient electron transport in the derived devices (substrate/NiO/perovskite/C60/ SnO2/metal) at a relatively thick thickness of ≈120 nm. Benefiting from its high crystallinity, the SnO2 ETL can provide respectable electron transport to yield a high PCE of 18.8% in the derived devices. To the best of our knowledge, it is among the highest performance reported in the inverted PVSCs employing inorganic ETLs. More importantly, a compact and stable protecting layer for device is spontaneously formed to remarkably enhance the ambient stability of the derived PVSC. As shown, over 90% of its initial PCE can be retained after 30 d storage in ambient with >70% relative humidity. Experimental Section General Methods: All chemicals and reagents were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and used as received from commercial sources without purification and purchased. Methyl ammonium iodide was synthesized according to previously reported methods.[1a] All solutions were filtered with a 0.45 μm PTFE filter before use. Synthesis of SnO2 NCs: The SnO2 NCs were synthesized according to previous report.[18] For the hydrolysis sol–gel synthesis, SnCl4·5H2O (0.350 g, 1.00 mmol) and aqueous TMAH (1.00 m, 8.00 mL) were successively added into ethanol (3.00 mL) under intense ultrasonic treatment, followed by heating the solution at 100 °C for 12 h to obtain the sol–gel SnO2 NCs. For the hydrothermal synthesis, the solution was transferred to a Telflon-lined stainless autoclave (15 mL) and kept at 200 °C for 12 h. The products were collected by centrifugation at 10 000 rpm, and washed with deionized water several times. After drying in vacuum at room temperature, the hydrothermal SnO2 NCs can be obtained. The XRD spectra were measured from the SnO2 powders (both sol–gel and hydrothermal) on glass substrate, which were prepared by drying the as-synthesis sample under vacuum in room temperature. The SnO2 NCs solution was prepared by dispersing the SnO2 NCs in isopropyl alcohol (IPA) with different concentrations and stirred overnight. The SnO2 layer shown in Figure 1b was prepared by spin-coating a precursor solution with a concentration of 30 mg mL−1 at 2000 r.p.m for 60 s, which yielded a thickness of ≈120 nm. The thickness of SnO2 was tuned by changing the concentration of precursor solution to 15 mg mL−1 (60 nm), 45 mg mL−1 (180 nm), and 60 mg mL−1 (240 nm) at a fixed spin-coating speed. The samples of TEM shown in

Figure 1c,d were prepared by dropping the precursor solution onto TEM grids and dried under vacuum. NiO Thin Film Preparation: Nickel (II) acetylacetonate was dissolved in ethanol (0.1 mol L−1) with adding 10 μL HCl (38 wt%) into the solution.[9a] The solution was then stirred in a sealed glass vial in air overnight. FTO-coated glass substrates were cleaned and then ultraviolet-ozone treated for 15 min prior to the deposition of NiO layer. Afterward, an NiO layer was spin-coated onto the FTO-coated glass at 3000 r.p.m for 30 s and then annealed at 400 °C for 1 h in air. Solar Cell Fabrication and Characterization: To prepare the perovskite precursor solution, CH3NH3I with lead(II) iodide (PbI2) as a molar ratio of 1:1.08 were dissolved in γ-butyrolactone and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) with a volume ratio (7:3) and stirred at 60 °C overnight. The perovskite precursor solution was then spin-coated onto FTO/NiO substrate at 1000 rpm for 15 s and 4000 rpm for 45 s. During the last 25 s of the second spin-coating step, the substrate was treated with toluene drop-casting (0.7 mL). The as spun films were annealed at 100 °C for ≈ 10 min. Then, the C60 electron-transport layer (3 mg mL−1 in chlorobenzene) was deposited by spin coating at 2000 r.p.m for 30 s and 4000 r.p.m for 5 s. Afterward, SnO2 NCs layer was spin-coated at 2000 r.p.m for 60s. Finally, a 100 nm thick top Ag electrode was evaporated under high vacuum (<2 × 10−7 Torr). For all studied devices, the device area was defined as 11.2 mm2 by metal shadow mask. All the J–V curves in this study were recorded by using Keithley 2400 source measurement unit and the scan rate was kept at 0.05 V s–1. The photocurrent was measured under illumination from a 450 W thermal Oriel solar simulator (AM 1.5 G). The illumination intensity of the light source was accurately calibrated employing a standard Si photodiode detector equipped with a KG-5 filter, which can be traced back to the standard cell of National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL). The EQE spectra were measured by combining a monochromated 450 W xenon lamp (Oriel) with a sourcemeter (Keithley 2400) and calculated using a calibrated Siphotodiode (OSI-Optoelectronics).
