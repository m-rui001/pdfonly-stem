1. Introduction Development of alternative energy sources is crucial to tackle the challenges encountered by the growing global energy demand. The U.S. Energy Information Administration projects that the world energy consumption will grow by 56% by 2040.[5] While renewable energy and nuclear power are the fastest-growing energy sources, increasing at approximately 2.5% per year worldwide, fossil fuels continue to supply nearly 80% of the world‟s energy. An alternative energy source, which has attracted significant interest over the last couple of decades, is hydrogen fuel. [9] Hydrogen fuel is considered one of the best ways to store energy produced from renewable and intermittent power sources. It has a specific energy density of 143 MJ kg−1, which is up to three times higher than that of common hydrocarbon fuels such as natural gas (53.6 MJ kg−1) and gasoline (46.4 MJ kg−1). While hydrogen is currently mainly obtained from steam reforming, the possibility of producing hydrogen fuel from renewable resources, such as water through the direct conversion of sunlight to fuel via water splitting (electrolysis), has instigated substantial research efforts.[18] Moreover, hydrogen fuel obtained from clean renewable processes would also reduce emissions of carbon dioxide and other

greenhouse gases, addressing growing environmental concerns. Another important reason for using hydrogen as a fuel is that it can be converted into electrical energy via direct electrochemical conversion in fuel cells at high efficiency, not limited by the Carnot efficiency of the heat engine, and releases water as the sole byproduct. Hydrogen fuel cells will not create any air pollutants responsible for health-debilitating smog in some of the world‟s most populated cities, nor would they contribute to human-driven climate change. Therefore, hydrogen-powered fuel cells stand to fill a much-needed gap in the world‟s desire for establishing a clean-energy infrastructure. Through two electrochemical reactions, the hydrogen oxidation reaction (HOR) and the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR), the high energy content of a hydrogen fuel can be converted into electricity in polymer-electrolyte fuel cells (PEFCs). The HOR occurs at the anode of the PEFC and, due to relatively fast kinetics, requires only small amounts of catalyst. One important drawback to current fuel-cell technology is the high content of platinum group metal (PGM) electrocatalysts required at the cathode to perform the sluggish ORR. As a result, PEFC electrocatalysts represent nearly 40-50% of the overall fuel-cell stack cost for automotive and other applications. According to a recent report by the U.S. Fuel Cell Technologies Office (FCTO), a strategic analysis projects that catalyst cost will be the largest single component of the PEFC stack cost, i.e., 41% for 500 000 automotive systems per year.[19] To address this cost bottleneck, remarkable progress has been made over the last decade in the development of low-cost PGM-free electrocatalysts synthesized from inexpensive, earth-abundant, and easily sourced materials such as iron, nitrogen, and carbon (Fe–N–C). These PGM-free Fe–N–C electrocatalysts now exhibit ORR activities approaching that of precious metal catalysts but at a fraction of the materials cost, which promises to significantly reduce overall costs of fuel-cell technology.

The complex synthesis of active PGM-free electrocatalysts involves simultaneous formation of active sites and the host-carbon structure. Both activity and structure need to be analyzed in parallel since the ORR performance is directly related to the accessibility of active sites and the transport of reactants and products to and from the active sites. Further, another important topic of discussion for this review is related to the current status of longterm stability of the initially high activity of catalysts. 2. Early Developments of PGM-Free Electrocatalysts from Metal Chelates The catalyst that initiated interest in the development of PGM-free electrocatalysts for fuel-cell applications dates back to the 1960s when Jasinski reported the first metal chelate, cobalt phthalocyanine (CoPc), as electrocatalytically active for the oxygen reduction reaction in alkaline electrolytes.[22] This discovery sparked research of further phthalocyanines with increased activity, especially in acidic electrolytes. A very thorough review of early developments of PGM-free catalysts based on transition metal chelates was published by Jahnke et al. in 1976,[23] who reported on a wide range of metal-chelate structures with different central atoms (Fe, Co, Ni, Cu), as well as different transition metal (Me) complex structures (Me–N4, Me–N2O2, Me–N2S2, Me–O4, Me–S4). The most important conclusions of Jahnke et al.‟s review are: (1) higher activity observed with polymeric phthalocyanines than monomers, (2) ORR activity improvement by combining phthalocyanine with a carbon support, and (3) importance of a thermal „activation‟ step for enhancing ORR activity and stability in acid electrolytes (also reported by Alt et al. [24]).

A similar study performed by Martins Alves et al. compared the electrocatalytic and chemical coordination properties of heat-treated CoPc and PAN+Co electrocatalysts.[26] Their results revealed the presence of Co–Nx moieties in the polyacrylonitrile (PAN)+Co catalysts, as well as the formation of large (10–20 nm) Co nanoparticles at higher temperatures. Moreover, theoretical studies, based on molecular orbital theory of phthalocyanine compounds with different central metal atoms, predicted that the ORR activity of these phthalocyanine-based electrocatalysts increased according to the following order: Cu  Ni < Co < Fe, independently of the pH and support.[27] This trend in the transition-metal ORR activity continues to hold true in the development of more advanced PGM-free electrocatalysts today. A decade after the reports by Jahnke et al., a new era of PGM-free electrocatalyst development began with the breakthrough reported by Yeager et al.,[4] who demonstrated promising ORR activity (relative to that of the previously reported heat-treated macrocycles) in both alkaline and acid electrolytes of catalysts obtained by heat-treating polyacrylonitrile (PAN), high-surface area carbon (Vulcan XC-72) and either Co(II) or Fe(II) salt (Figure 1). The authors‟ hypothesis for achieving comparable performance to Co tetraphenylporphyrin (CoTMPP) was that the nitrile nitrogen of PAN acted as a binding site for transition metal ions forming similar Me–Nx complexes. These Me–Nx, or in some cases Mex–Ny, structures continue to be the foundation for many models proposed for the ORR active-site structure in PGM-free electrocatalysts. Nonetheless, the most important finding from Yeager‟s group work was the elimination of the need for metal-complex precursors to generate active ORR catalysts, which opened the window to a large number of combinations of nitrogen-containing

3. PGM-Free Electrocatalysts: Activity and Structure 3.1. Progress in Fe–N–C Electrocatalysts obtained via Pyrolysis of Complex Precursors Almost three decades since being published in 1989, Yeager‟s group findings continue to be the foundation for a plethora of PGM-free electrocatalyst development studies which mainly involve varying nitrogen-containing monomers or polymers and carbon supports to studying the addition of polymeric precursor with new heteroatom dopants for improved activity and durability. [10,14,15,28,29,30] For a comprehensive review highlighting recent developments in the synthesis of pyrolyzed PGMfree Fe and Co electrocatalysts from different precursors see Gewirth et al.‟s publication in which the authors lists various synthesis processes along with their resulting half-wave potentials in both alkaline and acid electrolytes.[31] Moreover, a more fundamental recent review focusing on synthesis methods resulting in desired edge pyridinic nitrogen moieties was published by Singh et al.[32] Nevertheless, the majority of reported synthesis methods continue to describe the pyrolysis of a number of catalyst precursor combinations without offering any control of the final active-site density, accessibility, or catalyst structure. These properties are critical to achieve the ultimate goal of rationally designing PGM-free electrocatalysts with much improved performance.

3.3. Rationally Designed PGM-Free Electrocatalysts The key need for atomically dispersing Fe atoms to achieve the maximum density of active sites, along with the importance of hierarchical pore structure have been the primary drivers of the recent PGM-free electrocatalyst development. Metal organic frameworks composed of metal ions and organic linkers have unique features that enable to increase the density of FeN4 moieties, as well as allow the control over the microporosity in PGM-free electrocatalysts, eliminating the complex and poorly controlled precursor mixing, which dominated the field for over three decades. 3.4. The Need for Modifications to the Catalyst Layer Structure for Improved MEA Performance Over the past few years, several research groups have reported high performance in both H 2–O2 and H2–air fuel cells.[1,6,15,35,36,39,40,50-53] The case of improved hierarchical pore structure resulting in improved H2–air fuel-cell performance was presented and shown in Figure 4d–f. The current U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) catalyst activity target for 2018 is 25 mA cm−2 at 0.9 ViR-free in a H2–O2 fuel cell. Zelenay and et al. recently reported 21 mA cm−2, as well as a H2–air performance of 105 mAcm−2 at 0.8 V,[52] achieved mainly by optimizing the catalyst layer (CL) structure. At this phase of development, the ORR activity of PGM-free electrocatalysts (at high loadings) is approaching that of the low-PGM catalysts, and optimization of the MEA performance becomes necessary to improve fuel-cell performance. Due to the low volumetric activity, the PGM-free catalyst layers are typically an order of magnitude thicker than those of the low-PGM ones (see above), which limits the applicability of the MEA optimization techniques developed over the years for PGM CLs to PGMfree CLs, notoriously impeded by both gas and ionic transport resistances. Minimal work has been performed to date on the catalyst layer architecture for thick PGM-free electrodes, therefore, CL optimization is necessary and should be performed according to the morphology of the PGM-free catalyst.[54] For instance, the ionomer-to-carbon (I/C) ratio for the PGM CL is approximately 0.9 (40 wt% for 40 wt% Pt/C on HSC) for optimal performance. 4. The PGM-Free Active-Site Conundrum The nature of the ORR active site in PGM-free electrocatalysts is a topic of intense debate and is still far from fully understood. The current inference that a pyridinic nitrogen is coordinated to a metal center in a graphitic plane has been derived through indirect/ex situ methods such as analytical methods (metal vs metal-free catalyst), X-ray spectroscopy techniques, and quantum chemical modeling techniques like density functional theory (DFT). The transition metal is suggested as the active site or, in some publications, as necessity to create defects that form the active site. Varnell et al. advocated that FeN4 is not a requirement for ORR active catalyst and Fe nanoparticles embed in CNx graphitic plane are the active sites.[61] Artyushkova et al. suggested the presence of multiple active sites where FeNx promotes a four electron reaction pathway while pyrrolic and pyridinic N sites act as sites for a two-site two-electron pathway.[57] Moreover, CNx moieties in the nitrogen-doped carbon have shown ORR activity but the performance is not believed to be comparable to the metal nitrogen coordinated sites with close to PGM activities.[30,62] No model catalyst exists that contains only homogeneous structures for clear identification of the active site. Recently, atomically dispersed highly ORR-active catalyst comprising only FeNx type of structures have been synthesized, which have the potential to serve as a model catalyst.[52,63] It should also be noted that the different FeNx moieties have shown different characteristics and cannot be considered all the same. In fact, recent modeling efforts suggest that the activity of FeN4 sites may vary based on the local carbon structure or transition metal ligation under different environments.[1,64] A recent publication by Chung et al. has directly identified the long-sought FeN4 moiety using highresolution electron microscopy studies of the (CM+PANI)–Fe–C catalyst (Figure 9).[1] Analysis of

4.1. X-ray Spectroscopy Characterization: Fe-Specific Techniques Out of the current characterization tools available for PGM-free active-site identification, the most common and dominant techniques, which are also Fe-specific techniques, are XAS (Fe K-edge), Mössbauer spectroscopy and nuclear resonance vibration spectroscopy (NVRS). While Mössbauer is a bulk technique, XAS can be both bulk and surface-specific, and NRVS can probe the specific vibration modes of Fe moieties. PGM-Free Active-Site Modeling—Evolving Models of the Fe–Nx Site Experimental progress in improving the ORR activity of PGM-free materials has been significant but future gains would be aided by targeted synthesis of dense arrays of highactivity sites, which hinges on knowledge of the ORR active-site structure(s). The atomicscale nature of PGM-free active-site structures in pyrolyzed Fe–N–C systems has been

Due to the highly heterogeneous nature of these materials, it is difficult to directly identify all atomic-scale structures and even more difficult to correlate observed structures to high ORR activity. The application of theoretical and modeling approaches allows for a solution of the inverse problem of what is the active site: what is the relative activity of a given atomic structure? By exploring a wide variety of systems, correlations emerge between structures and their activities. Some of the remaining outstanding questions regarding ORR activity of PGM-free sites currently being addressed by theory include (from Holby and Zelenay, [83] and references therein): 1. Role of transition metal species and coordinating N-environment(s) in affecting activity, durability, and experimental signatures. 2. Role of host C material, in particular, relation of active-site position to edges and pores in affecting activity, durability, and experimental signatures. 3. Role of persistent ligands and active-site poisons under in situ environments. All three of these considerations will modify the local chemical environment and thus modify the local electronic structure which gives rise to binding properties that lead to relative ORR activity. By addressing these issues at the atomic scale, it is believed that modeling insight regarding the activity of structures can inform synthesis in order to produce higher densities of the most active (and durable) structures with reactant accessibility. This knowledge-based, targeted design is likely necessary to further improve activities to the point that such materials are appropriate replacements for PGM materials.

Durability Challenges of Fe-Based PGM-Free Electrocatalysts In addition to the challenge of the identification of the PGM-free active site, another important challenge in the development of PGM-free electrocatalysts is the limited understanding of the mechanisms responsible for activity loss during fuel-cell operation. As previously mentioned, unlike the state-of-the-art PGM electrocatalysts, PGM-free catalysts have demonstrated a high tolerance to

virtually all contaminants and impurities commonly present in the fuel-cell hardware, as well as in the hydrogen and air feed streams. Typical contaminants include alcohols and other organics, carbon monoxide, sulfur oxides, and both inorganic and organic anions. While these contaminants significantly affect the performance of the PGM-based PEFC, typically by undergoing chemisorption, or strong adsorption, on the catalyst surface, the PGM-free active sites have shown no affinity to these contaminants and no decrease in ORR activity even in the presence of large quantities of various contaminants. A short opinion on the current durability challenges of PGM-free electrocatalysts has been recently published by our group.[12] In brief, proposed degradation of mechanism have been summarized into two types: atomic-scale degradation and macro-/mesoscale degradation (Figure 13).

Whereas degradation mechanisms for PGM catalysts, namely nanoparticle catalyst dissolution and agglomeration, as well as corrosion of the supporting catalyst matrix, are well known,[88] similar understanding of the mechanisms of degradation of PGM-free catalysts are limited. The limitation is mainly caused by the lack of knowledge of the active-site structure, a major challenge due to the highly complex heterogeneous nature of these materials. Undoubtedly, models developed for PGM catalyst degradation have permitted improvements to catalyst development by providing knowledge about nanoparticle coalescence, poisoning, and agglomeration.[89] Furthermore, theoretical models have also been developed for the better understanding of carbon support corrosion caused by the high potentials experienced during fuel-cell start/stop operation, which are largely responsible for secondary degradation phenomena such as platinum nanoparticles detachment and agglomeration.[90] The unsolved conundrum of the PGM-free active site presents a challenge in terms of developing atomicscale models for the study of catalyst activity degradation. While some similarities for the activity loss can be derived from the understanding gained from PGM catalysts, PGM-free

Main mechanisms of activity loss or degradation for PGM-free electrocatalysts will most likely differ from those of their PGM counterparts. Several degradation mechanisms have already been proposed: (i) active-site degradation either by demetalation or C/N corrosion,[91] (ii) active-site protonation causing anion adsorption,[92] (iii) hydroperoxyl radical attack due to peroxide generation,[93] and (iv) micropore flooding.[94] Thus, based on a similar rationale to that of PGM electrocatalysis, there are two overarching categories for the degradation of PGM-free activity: the first is related to the atomic-scale degradation of active sites, and the second the macro- and meso-scale degradation due to carbon corrosion, water flooding issues, or changes in local ionomer environment leading to mass transport limitations.

6.2. Macro- and Mesoscale Degradation of the Catalyst and Catalyst Layer Structure

The main mechanism for macro- and mesoscale activity loss is likely carbon corrosion. Carbon corrosion can lead to structural conductivity losses, dissolution of the active sites in the region of the carbon corrosion, removal of the active site from the triple-phase boundary necessary for the ORR, and flooding as corrosion of carbon generates oxygen-functionalized groups that increase the catalysts hydrophilicity. Carbon corrosion can also lead to compacting of the catalyst material and a loss of porosity, which could lead to fewer active sites being exposed to the environment and causing mass transport issues. Local ionomer degradation can also affect mass transport, leading to a decrease in accessibility of some sites and ultimately a loss of catalytic activity. These larger-scale mass transport effects are likely to be more pronounced in PGM-free catalysts than in PGM ones due to the increased thickness of catalyst layers typically employed. Significant efforts to understand the fuel-cell performance and long-term stability based on macroscale effects have been reported by Banham et al.[6,55,94,103] In a recent review, Banham et al. mentioned similar mechanism of degradation to the ones presented in this review as primary reasons for instability of PGMfree catalysts, i.e., leaching of the metal site; oxidative attack by H2O2; and protonation and possible anion adsorption.[103] A study by Banham and collaborators addressed the question related to possible issues of micropore flooding within the PGM-free catalyst particles,

Since losses are more significant in the kinetic region, they are likely related to active-site degradation. A recent study by Dodelet et al. reported on current density losses during H2/air fuel-cell operation of an initially highly active ORR electrocatalyst, NC_Ar + NH3.[104] The studied catalyst was derived from ZIF-8, Fe(II)(Phen)3 and phenanthroline mixture with high content of micropores hosting FeN4type active sites. Similarly, Yin and Zelenay reported on the degradation kinetics of a high-surface-area PANI-based PGM-free electrocatalyst, CM–PANI–Fe–C(Zn), which was synthesized with two different precursors acting as pore formers, cyanamide and Zn. They suggested a single, autocatalytic degradation mechanism of the active sites, through the formation of deactivation species (e.g., hydrogen peroxide and/or peroxide-derived radicals), is responsible for the loss of fuel-cell performance.

Concluding Remarks Herein, a summary has been presented on the recent progress on the development of Fe–N–C PGMfree electrocatalysts with improved activity, particularly in the technology-relevant H2–air PEFC. The most significant progress has been obtained from the realization of the importance of a hierarchical pore structure in which micropores can host a high density of PGM-free active sites, while macropores facilitate transport of reactants and products, as well as modifications to the catalyst layer structure, i.e., ionomer-to-carbon ratio and ionomer equivalent weight. The understanding of the competitive nature between high-activity atomically dispersed Fe–N active sites versus inactive or lower activity Fe agglomerates, and control thereof, has also led to significant improvements in activity. The new direction for the synthesis of PGM-free electrocatalysts via a more controlled, rationally designed process using metal–organic frameworks was briefly discussed. The conundrum of the PGM-free active-site structure was discussed giving insight into methodology to better understand, identify, and quantify PGM-free active-site structures and thus giving insight into future improvements. Furthermore, challenges associated with PGM-free degradation were assessed as they relate to both atomic-scale and macro-scale degradation. Expansion of theoretical approaches, particularly those that bridge detailed knowledge of active sites and their experimentally accessible signatures (including activity and durability, as well as spectroscopic signatures), will further improve understanding of how atomic and mesoscale structure couple to activity and durability. Suggestions for not only improving active-site density but also minimizing dominant degradation pathways are already starting to emerge based on findings from modeling and coupled experimental efforts. For example, hydrogen peroxide generation could be mitigated using current strategies employed for PGM-based electrocatalysts, such as radical scavengers; and increased level of graphitization can

Remarkable progress has been made in the development of Fe–N–C catalysts for the oxygen reduction reaction, enabling much improved fuel-cell performance. Since active site and support are closely interlinked in Fe–N–C catalysts, efforts to obtain the desired Fe–N–C catalyst structure, as well as to better understand the key limiting factors for activity and durability, are reviewed.
