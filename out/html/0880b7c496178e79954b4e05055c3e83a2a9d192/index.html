a b s t r a c t Hundreds of tons of pharmaceutical compounds are annually dispensed and consumed worldwide. Pharmaceuticals are an important class of emerging environmental micropollutants: their presence in water bodies is an increasing environmental concern. The aim of this review paper is to provide a comprehensive review of the occurrence of pharmaceuticals in freshwater aquatic environments in the African and European context. A literature survey has been performed, resulting in 3024 data points related to environmental occurrence. The concentration levels of 71 pharmaceuticals were assessed. The top ten most frequently detected and quantiﬁed compounds in both continents were sulfamethoxazole, carbamazepine, diclofenac, trimethoprim, ibuprofen, naproxen, paracetamol (acetaminophen), ketoprofen, venlafaxine and clarithromycin. The maximum concentrations of 17β-estradiol, estriol, ciproﬂoxacin, sulfamethoxazole, paracetamol, naproxen reported in African aquatic environments were ~3140, ~20,000, ~125, ~100, ~215 and ~171 times higher, respectively, than the concentrations reported in European based studies. The variation in pharmaceutical consumption, partial removal of pharmaceuticals in wastewater treatment processes, and the direct discharge of livestock animal farm wastewater were identiﬁed among the major reasons for the observed differences. Several pharmaceuticals were found in aquatic environments of both continents in concentration levels higher than their ecotoxicity endpoints. In Europe, compounds such as diclofenac, ibuprofen, triclosan, sulfadimidine, carbamazepine and ﬂuoxetine were reported in a concentration higher than the available ecotoxicity endpoints. 1. Introduction New and emerging pollutants present a new global water quality challenge with potentially serious threats to human health and ecosystems (Varela et al., 2016; Pino-Otín et al., 2017; Desbiolles et al., 2018; Ivanová et al., 2018). Pharmaceuticals are emerging environmental pollutants, and their fate in water bodies is an increasing environmental concern (Desbiolles et al., 2018). Multiple negative effects that are expected include the development of antibiotic resistant bacteria and genes (Rizzo et al., 2013; Birosová et al., 2015; Varela et al., 2016) due to sub-inhibitive concentrations of antibiotics in wastewater (Karaolia et al., 2014) and horizontal transfer of resistant gene to antibiotic sensitive microbial species (Baquero et al., 2008), as well as endocrine disruptive and other deleterious ecotoxicological effects (Schultz et al., 2011; Caldwell et al., 2012; Saravanan et al., 2014; Peltzer et al., 2017). Different studies mention different number of pharmaceutical compounds that are being prescribed worldwide, for human and veterinary use (Boxall et al., 2012; Dong et al., 2013; Hughes et al., 2013; Caldwell et al., 2014). By comparison, on a global scale, 3500 were reported by Caldwell et al. (2014) and 4000 by Boxall et al. (2012). For Europe, 5000 have been reported by Hughes et al. (2013) and 10,000 have been reported for the US market by Dong et al. (2013). Unlike in developed countries, the number of pharmaceuticals and their consumption are not readily available for Africa (MMV, 2017). With these high numbers of pharmaceuticals, it would be impossible to experimentally assess the hazards and risks in a timely manner (Boxall et al., 2012). The combination of occurrence data with ecotoxicological effects greatly helps researchers to identify a manageable and smaller subset of pharmaceuticals of high relative concern for deployment of resources for environmental and human health risk assessment, monitoring and regulatory purposes (Boxall et al., 2012). Various widely used pharmaceuticals in human medicine are excreted unchanged or in high concentrations as active metabolites and are continuously released into wastewater collection systems (Kostich et al., 2014). One of the most important factors that governs the occurrence pattern of individual pharmaceuticals in the environment is their consumption trend (Boxall et al., 2012) which is, the disease pattern in the area affects its consumption trend. For instance, low-income countries have a higher rate of infectious diseases (WHO, 2011) and generally higher rate of over-the-counter selfmedication (Ayukekbong et al., 2017). Moreover, only values for which the country of origin was named were selected. 3. Occurrence of pharmaceuticals in African and European freshwater environments According to Fent et al. (2006) the detection of pharmaceuticals in the treated wastewater was ﬁrst reported in Kansas City, US in 1976. Subsequently, in 1981, the presence of 25 pharmaceuticals in the river Lee (UK) was investigated with concentrations up to 1000 ng L−1 (Richardson and Bowron, 1985). Then after, several studies have detected pharmaceuticals in different environmental compartments across the globe, such as; land via manure, sediments, aquatic plants and coastal environments (Kim et al., 2011; Li et al., 2012, 2013; Na et al., 2013). Consumed human pharmaceuticals are continuously discharged into the environment via two important intermediate treatment steps as presented in Fig. 1: wastewater and solid waste (Kasprzyk-Hordern et al., 2009; Rizzo et al., 2013; Sui et al., 2017). Other sources of

pharmaceuticals in the environment include direct discharge of untreated wastewater to the environment through the leakage of septic tanks and the application of animal manure or WWTP (wastewater treatment plant) sludge as fertilizer in agricultural ﬁelds (Paíga et al., 2016). Compounds used in veterinary applications are in many cases directly released into the environment as non-point (or diffuse) source pollution (García-Galán et al., 2010; Jurado et al., 2012). Moreover, there are other factors that contribute to pharmaceutical contamination of water resources particularly in developing regions of the globe. For example, in many communities of African countries there are areas whereby there are poor or no sanitation processes, such areas do not have sewage treatment facilities, leading to a direct disposal of human excreta on the ground or in surface water (Segura et al., 2015). Therefore, during the rainy seasons, fecal matter is washed off from the ground into the surface waters. In general, natural environmental waters are contaminated by pharmaceuticals originating from WWTP efﬂuents, pharmaceutical industry and hospital wastewater discharges, landﬁlls, livestock breeding, septic systems, etc. (Kümmerer, 2001; Pal et al., 2010; Lapworth et al., 2012). In this review, the top ten maximum concentrations of pharmaceuticals that were quantiﬁed in the literature were all reported in African and European based studies and the extent of data-spread in both continents is presented in Table 1 and Table S2, respectively. The top ten most frequently detected compounds were sulfamethoxazole, carbamazepine, diclofenac, trimethoprim, ibuprofen, naproxen, paracetamol (acetaminophen), ketoprofen, venlafaxine and clarithromycin. In the following sub-sections, the occurrence of the most widely detected therapeutic groups will be discussed.

ARVDs drugs are more frequently in African aquatic environments than in Europe (Table 2). Didanosine, efavirenz, emtricitabine, lamivudine, lopinavir, nevirapine, rimantadine, ritonavir, stavudine, tenofovir, zalcitabine and zidovudine were almost only reported in African aquatic environments (K'oreje et al., 2016; Ngumba et al., 2016; Wood et al., 2017, 2015).

Analgesics and anti-inﬂammatories (AAF) Analgesics and anti-inﬂammatories (AAF) are the most frequently detected class of pharmaceuticals in the environment, and the publications considered in this review indicate their presence in water bodies from ng L−1 to several μg L−1 (Fig. 2). Diclofenac, naproxen, ibuprofen, paracetamol (acetaminophen) and ketoprofen are the most common AAFs in aquatic environments on both continents. Codeine is more frequently reported in European studies than in Africa (Gros et al., 2012a; Acuña et al., 2015; Lardy-Fontan et al., 2016; Petrie et al., 2016; Brieudes et al., 2016, 2017). This is in line with the global consumption pattern of codeine reported in a country report of 2015 (The Statistics Portal, 2015). In this report France, UK and Spain are in the top eleven of the countries where codeine was most frequently reported in the aquatic environments. Another study also shows that the use of codeine increased between 2006 and 2015 by 42% in France (Hider-Mlynarz et al., 2018). The consumption of codeine in Europe can be explained by the analgesic preferences of population in the countries and by the role of national guidelines, prescription policies and the marketing strategies of pharmaceutical companies (Dhalla et al., 2011; Hider-Mlynarz et al., 2018). Antibiotics (anti-infectives) Several antibiotics were reported in environmental waters. This is in line with the global increase of antibiotic consumption. The greatest increase in antibiotic consumption is observed in low- and middleincome countries (LMICs) and around 80% of all antibiotics used in this community are taken without prescription (Laxminarayan et al., 2013). Drug resistance is a major public health problem in Africa, significant resistance has been reported for diseases such as cholera, dysentery, typhoid, meningitis, gonorrhea, TB and malaria (Ndihokubwayo et al., 2013). About 24 antibiotics are frequently reported contaminants in surface water in Africa. From these 24 antibiotics, 14 were reported in European based studies. Sulfamethoxazole was the most detected compound in aquatic samples in Africa. An interesting observation is the quantiﬁcation of chloramphenicol in the aquatic environments of both continents (Chitescu et al., 2015; K'oreje et al., 2016; Oluwatosin et al., 2016), although it was banned for use in food producing animals in the European Union starting from 1996 due to its suspected carcinogenicity and associations with the development of a plastic anaemia in humans (European Union, 1996). Like the AAF therapeutic class, a large variation in concentration was observed for the different antibiotic compounds in Africa as well as in Europe (Fig. 3). The measured concentration ranges from 0.08 ng L−1 trimethoprim, in Finland (Ahkola et al., 2017) to 53,828 ng L−1 sulfamethoxazole in Mozambique (Segura et al., 2015). The highest reported concentration of sulfamethoxazole was reported to be due to untreated sewage as an important route of contamination of surface waters. WWTPs result in a signiﬁcant reduction of the sulfamethoxazole load. Its removal is reported in the range from 50 to 74% (Lindim et al., 2016). However, sulfamethoxazole is also used for livestock animals, which may contribute to the high concentration of this substance in surface waters in Mozambique. A high percentage of antibiotics can enter the environment via animal urine (up to 90%) and faeces (up to 75%) (Laxminarayan et al., 2013). In Europe, tetracyclines are the most consumed antibiotics for veterinary use. In 30 European countries in 2015, sales of the various veterinary antimicrobial classes for food-producing species show that tetracyclines were the most consumed (32.8%) (EMA, 2017). Moreover, the lower cost of sulfamethoxazole compared to other antibiotic subgroups such as macrolides (e.g., erythromycin) enhances its consumption. Concentration levels reported and their aquatic toxicities The ecotoxicology of pharmaceuticals provides valuable information on the risk to the environment and to people. Several pharmaceuticals encountered in aquatic environments have been linked to potentially causing a large variety of detrimental effects in both vertebrates and invertebrates (Hoeger et al., 2005; Veldhoen et al., 2006; Yang et al., 2008; Han et al., 2010; Contardo-Jara et al., 2011; Schultz et al., 2011; Caldwell et al., 2012; Saravanan et al., 2014; Peltzer et al., 2017). In this review, the potential of the measured environmental concentrations to poison aquatic organisms was assessed based on the concentration data compiled and obtained ecotoxicity endpoints from a comprehensive literature review in aquatic toxicity studies (Table 3). The ecotoxicity endpoint is the result of an experiment to determine the level of toxicity of a substance by looking at the adverse effects including change in growth, reproduction and survival (OECD, 2013). Those compounds identiﬁed with measured environmental concentration (MEC) levels higher than the toxicity endpoints are discussed in this section and the risk quotient for those pharmaceuticals are presented in Table 3. The risk quotients were calculated by dividing MEC by the ecotoxicity endpoints (US EPA, 2014).

From the AAF therapeutic class, diclofenac, ibuprofen, paracetamol and naproxen were quantiﬁed above the toxicity endpoints. The no observed effect concentration (NOEC) of diclofenac reported in the literature for brown trout is 500 ng L−1 for a 21 days exposure (Hoeger et al., 2005). The concentration measured in the surface water samples of Germany (Kunkel and Radke, 2012), Kenya (K'oreje et al., 2016), South Africa (Agunbiade and Moodley, 2016; Archer et al., 2017b; Gumbi et al., 2017), Czech Republic (Lacina et al., 2013) and the UK (Kay et al., 2016) were all higher than this ecotoxicity level. Another study also shows that diclofenac decreases thyroid hormone levels in ﬁsh, e.g., Indian major carp (Cirrhinus mrigala) with lowest observed effect concentration (LOEC) of 1000 ng L−1 (Saravanan et al., 2014). The measured environmental concentration (MEC) of ibuprofen was also found above the toxicity endpoints. Toxicity tests done on ibuprofen revealed that it decreased egg fertilisation in ﬁsh, for Florida ﬂag ﬁsh (Jordanella ﬂoridae) with LOEC 100 ng L−1 (Nesbitt, 2011). This LOEC of ibuprofen is lower than the concentration measured in the surface water samples of Algeria (Kermia et al., 2016), Finland (Lindholm-Lehto et al., 2016a, 2016b), Greece (Nannou et al., 2015), Kenya (K'oreje et al., 2016), Portugal (Paíga et al., 2016), Serbia (Petrović et al., 2014), South Africa (Agunbiade and Moodley, 2016; Archer et al., 2017b; Gumbi et al., 2017), Spain (Gros et al., 2012b; Boleda et al., 2013; Carmona et al., 2014; Acuña et al., 2015; Aymerich et al., 2016), Nigeria (Oluwatosin et al., 2016), Italy (Patrolecco et al., 2013), Luxembourg (Krein et al., 2013), Sweden (Daneshvar et al., 2012) and the UK (Kay et al., 2016). Other toxicity tests also show that ibuprofen increased vitellogenin (VTG) (a protein normally synthesized by female ﬁsh during oocyte maturation) production in male ﬁsh and reduced reproduction behavior in ﬁsh, Japanese medaka (Oryziaslatipes) with LOEC 1000 ng L−1 (Han et al., 2010). Another compound in the AAF class found above the toxicity endpoint is paracetamol. A study on acute toxicity of paracetamol in clams (Ruditapes philippinarum) shows that the antioxidant defence system is activated, and the metabolism, energy balance and energy reserves are deleteriously impacted at the highest exposure concentrations (2500 and 25,000 ng L−1) (Nunes et al., 2017). The MEC of paracetamol was by far higher (~43 times) than these toxicity endpoints in Kenyan environments. Moreover, from the AAF class the MEC of naproxen is also reported above the toxicity endpoint. The sub-chronic exposure to the naproxen sodium was found to have strong effects on the early life stage of common carp (Cyprinus carpio). Hatching, developmental rate, morphology and histopathology were inﬂuenced at LOEC of 10,000 ng L−1 (Sehonova et al., 2017). The MEC of naproxen was reported up to ~6 times higher than this in South Africa. The reported MEC of some antibiotics is also beyond the deleterious effect for freshwater organisms. Conclusions This review provides an overview of contamination by 71 pharmaceuticals in African and European aquatic environments. The contamination by pharmaceuticals in both continents are essentially due to a number of major factors, including uncontrolled wastewater treatment, discharge of drug manufacturing processes, domestic discharges from informal settlements without proper sanitation facilities, seasonal variation in pharmaceutical consumption, dilution factors, prescription rates of individual pharmaceuticals in the country, festival events, cost of pharmaceuticals, animal farm wastewater, resistance of individual pharmaceuticals to biodegradation, the choice of individual pharmaceuticals, pharmacokinetics of individual pharmaceuticals, reintroduction of the contaminants back into the water system after WWTP, the availability of drug return program in a country and transformation of pharmaceuticals into degradation products during metabolism. In Europe, the presence of pharmaceuticals in aquatic environments is explained by their limited removal in the WWTPs. Sulfamethoxazole, carbamazepine, diclofenac, trimethoprim, ibuprofen, naproxen, paracetamol, ketoprofen, venlafaxine and clarithromycin were most frequently detected compounds. Even though chloramphenicol was banned for use, it was detected in both continents. High detection frequencies for anti-retroviral drugs were less reported in Europe and appear to be much more region speciﬁc, which is strongly linked with the consumption pattern of ARVDs. Lamivudine, paracetamol and naproxen have the highest pharmaceutical concentrations reported in aquatic environments; all of these are reported in Africa. In a comparison, it is observed that there is a large variation in concentration for several compounds. The maximum concentration of pharmaceuticals measured in African environments is around 20,000 times higher than in European environments. Pharmaceuticals such as ibuprofen, paracetamol, naproxen, diclofenac, sulfamethoxazole, trimethoprim, ciproﬂoxacin, triclosan, ﬂuoxetine, estrone, 17b-estradiol and estriol appeared to be in higher concentrations than their ecotoxicity levels. The MECs in Africa went up to 7850 times than the toxicity endpoints. The MECs along with toxicity endpoints trigger the necessity and the urgency of advanced wastewater treatment in Africa, particularly for healthcare wastewater.
