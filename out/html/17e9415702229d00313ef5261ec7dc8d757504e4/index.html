Abstract The state-of-the-art Ni-rich layered oxides (LiNixCoyMn1-x-yO2, x>0.5) as the cathode material for lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) can push the energy and power density to a higher level than now available.1,2 However, volume variation associated with anisotropic lattice strain and stress that being built during lithium (de)intercalation induces severe structural instability and electrochemical decay of the cathode materials, which is further amplified when the battery operating at a high voltage (>4.5 V) essential for unlocking its high-energy3-6. Even after much effort by the research community, an intrinsic strain-retardant method for directly alleviating the continuous accumulation of lattice strain remains elusive. Here, by introducing a coherent perovskite phase into the layered structure functioning as a ‘rivet’, we significantly mitigate the pernicious structural evolutions by a pinning effect. The lattice strain evolution in every single cycle is dramatically reduced by nearly 70% when compared with commercial materials, which significantly enhances morphological integrity leading to a notable improvement in battery cyclability. This strain-retardant approach broadens the perspective for lattice engineering to

Page 2

release the strain raised from lithium (de)intercalation and paves the way to develop high-energydensity cathodes with long durability.

Anisotropic lattice strain and stress yielded upon insertion chemistry usually induces crystal structure fatigue including the formation of cracks.7,8 Development of zero-strain insertion materials such as lithium titanium oxides, thus, determines the superior electrochemical capability.9,10 Unfortunately, the strain regulation for Ni-rich layered oxides challenges the synthesis and crystal chemistry to maintain the ideal scenario in terms of defects free and phase purity,11,12 thereby remaining the mechanochemical degradation issue.8,13 Intrinsically, lattice strain is ineluctable in insertion layered oxides as lithium ions insertion and extraction.14 This strain primarily originates from the variation of ionic radius of redox species and Coulombic repulsions of oxygen.15,16 The former contributes to the contraction of transition metals-oxygen octahedrons (TMO6),17,18 reducing lattice parameter a(b) upon the entire charging process. The latter change in electrostatic interaction (O-O and O-Li-O) can critically alter the cspacing of the material, which is extremely severe at the high-voltage region where the c-spacing rapidly collapses.19,20 These anisotropic lattice displacements together yield the build-up of lattice strain and lead to irreversible phase transformation,8 which are widely believed as the origin of the deterioration of layered oxides. Tremendous efforts have been devoted to constraining the structural degradations originated from lattice variation via various approaches including elemental doping21-23, microstructure engineering24-26, and single-crystal development27-29, etc. Unfortunately, these typical approaches cannot solve the lattice displacement owing to the inevitable intrinsic evolution of anisotropic

Page 3

lattice parameters in layered oxides, the root cause of structural instability.3,13 With this regard, finding an effective method to regulate the raising lattice strain is essential for achieving high structural stability. Inspired by the function of “rivet” in the field of building architecture, we propose that a robust crystal structure can be created by introducing a strain-retardant phase into the layered structure to prevent the volume variations. To achieve this goal, the key is to choose the strain-retardant phase that can regulate the lattice strain changes as lithium insertion and extraction from the solid matrix. Searching for one phase that contains a stable physical structure and a good lattice match with layered structure oxides leads to a layered perovskite variant phase of La4[LiTM]O8 (LLMO, TM = Ni, Co, Mn) to work as the strain retardant. Specifically, the stable perovskite structure integrating layered structure with rock-salt structure determines an excellent match for layered structure cathodes encouraging the strain-retardant strategy. Secondly, the similarity in lattice parameters between LLMO and layered oxides ensures the possibility of coherent growth. Lastly, LLMO can be achieved by utilizing an optimized synthesis approach of layered oxides, 30 eliminating additional structural complexity induced by further treatment. Based on this knowledge, we successfully introduce the strain-retardant phase into the state-of-the-art Ni-rich layered material LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 (NCM). This material demonstrates significantly suppressed build-up lattice strain over the entire cycles, which is unveiled by multi-scale advanced characterizations from electronic, local atomic structure to the particles and electrode-level. Such dramatically mitigated lattice strain in the layered oxides results in a leading cyclability at a high cutoff voltage.

Strain-retardant strategy

Page 4

Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1 illustrates our strain-retardant strategy by employing a coherent perovskite phase into the layered structure to alleviate lattice displacement for achieving pseudo zero-strain cathodes. For typical layered structure oxides, three common lattice distortions usually occur upon lithium insertion and extraction. There is lattice expansion along with the cdirection, inhomogeneity in slab-twisting, and bending caused by the variations in local lithium concentrations.31 In addition, local structural distortion could also aggravate cationic migration, leading to irreversible phase transformation. The intrinsic structural instability is the critical factor for mechanical fatigue leading to electrochemical degradations in the layered oxides. To suppress this structural instability, we employ a perovskite LLMO phase into the layered oxide structure as the “rivet” forming a mechanically stabilized structure. This robust structure could maintain the mechanochemical stability attributing to large phase transition energy barriers in this “rivet” phase (pinning effect). Through this strategy, the structural distortion or lattice displacements in the layered structure can be dramatically suppressed towards pseudo zero-strain cathodes. The successful synthesis of the designed strain-retardant NCM (D-NCM) is revealed by synchrotron X-ray diffraction (XRD) and high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM). The XRD patterns of pristine NCM (P-NCM, Fig. 1b) show identical layered oxides structure with R3̅m symmetry. Rietveld refinements of D-NCM with two-phase model (Fig. 1c), which contains rhombohedral R3̅m and tetragonal I4/mmm phases, confirm the introduced LLMO phase with the weight fraction of ~0.82 wt% (Supplementary Table 1-3). The composition of D-NCM is also substantiated by inductively coupled plasma (ICP) and EDS measurements (Supplementary Table 4-5). Slight smaller lattice parameter c in LLMO can compress the c-spacing in D-NCM and a larger a(b)-axis in LLMO also slightly broadens lattice a(b) in D-NCM after the two phases of coherent growth (Supplementary Table 3). These lattice

parameters changes verified the coherent growth of LLMO and NCM, which is distinguished from common large metal ions doping in TM site that usually leads to an increase in c-spacing.32 Uniformly distributed elements and dark-field TEM image demonstrate the well-dispersed LLMO phase in D-NCM (Supplementary Fig. 2-4). Coherent growth of strain-retardant LLMO phase with layered oxides along [100] direction is confirmed in D-NCM as observed coherency boundary in HAADF-STEM image (Fig. 1d-e and Supplementary Fig. 5). Fast Fourier transform (FFT) extracted diffraction patterns (insert in Fig. 1e) display two separated structures of R3̅m phase along [100] direction and I4/mmm structure along [010] direction. A slight reduction in the dspacing along with (003) planes is also demonstrated by comparing the structural fingerprint in DNCM to P-NCM, which is consistent with XRD results. The emerged new peak at ~2.91 Å in extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) spectrum of D-NCM at Mn K-edge that corresponds to the Mn–La distance shows the clear signature of successful introduction of the perovskite LLMO phase (Supplementary Fig. 6). All these results substantiate the good coherent growth between layered oxides and perovskite phase, suggesting a successful structural and material design.

Strain retarding behavior To elucidate strain-retardant function of the LLMO phase in D-NCM, we conducted inoperando synchrotron XRD experiments during the initial fully charge-discharge cycle with a large voltage window of 3.0-4.8 V (Fig. 2). For comparison, structural evolutions in P-NCM were also tracked by combining in-operando synchrotron XRD with ex-situ high-resolution XRD (Supplementary Fig. 7-10), indicating H1-M-H2-H3 phase transitions, which is in good agreement with the reported results.15,33 These phase transitions are always in conjunction with the generation

of severe lattice strain inducing electrochemical decays. Exceptionally, no clear phase transition was investigated in D-NCM due to no obvious (003) peak splitting except for gradually continuous shifting over the (de)lithiation process under 4.3 V (Supplementary Fig.11). Only small second peak shows even at the fully charged state (100% state-of-charge, SoC, where x in Li1xNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 is close to 1). With eliminated concerns raised from surface residual lithium

compounds (low content residuals on the particle surface, Supplementary Fig.12),34,35 this suggests the extensively mitigated on the phase transitions. Although in-operando XRD results elucidate the substantially suppressed phase transitions with employed perovskite LLMO phase into the layered oxides, how the strain in D-NCM is retarded during lithium insertion and extraction remains ambiguous. We firstly analyzed the strain variations in lattice dimensions by Rietveld refinement against in-operando diffraction patterns (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 13). When charged to 4.3 V, the lattice parameter c and cell volume (V) in P-NCM change 0.62 Å (4.37%) and 6.60 Å3 (6.52%), respectively. In sharp contrast, only 30% variations in both lattice parameters (1.54% and 2.63%, respectively) display in the modified D-NCM material over the same voltage range. Even after a full delithiation to 4.8 V, these changes are still slightly less than that in P-NCM charged to the low voltage of 4.3 V. This suggests the effectually mitigation of lattice displacements, especially at high voltages where irreversible phase transition shows in P-NCM. One question still remains though, how exactly the strain-retardant LLMO phase alleviates the continuous accumulation of lattice strain with lithium removal from the matrix. Given that lithium diffusion along with (003) plane in the layered oxides, the lattice strain integrated into the c-axis direction would be larger than that in the a(b)-axis direction. To answer the aforementioned question, we quantitively analyzed this anisotropic strain evolution along with

Page 7

different directions (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 14-15). Although the overall microstrain still presents in D-NCM as the slope in Williamson-Hall plot increases (Supplementary Fig. 16),36 the dramatically slowing-down build-up of shear strain c/a by nearly 70% can be clearly observed at the start point of delithiation in D-NCM (Fig. 2d), delaying the phase transition process to high voltage range. In addition, large shear strain evolution in P-NCM over 3.8 V charging was eliminated in D-NCM as the c/a only slight changes in the orange region. The serious lattice strain at two planes perpendicular to each other is also analyzed to further reveal the origin of anisotropic strain.37 Compared to P-NCM, the strain in D-NCM shows only appropriately 20% and 30% variations in (003) and (110) planes, respectively. Further analysis of the lattice strain variation in LLMO upon charging (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. 17) clearly demonstrates the strain retarding behavior by altering the lattice strain in LLMO at each phase transition region in layered oxides. This strain-retardant phenomenon originates from the high phase transformation energy barriers of LLMO, which leads to a robust “rivet” to stabilize the framework of layered oxides from variation.38 These results elucidate the significantly suppressed accumulation of lattice strain with our strain-retardant strategy by introducing a coherent phase into the layered structure.

Electrochemical properties Electrochemical properties were characterized in both half and full cells with (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 18-23) to demonstrate the enhanced stability with strain retarded. Compared to P-NCM, although the discharge capacity of D-NCM is slightly decreased from 220 mAh/g to 210 mAh/g (Fig. 3a) owing to the mitigated H2-H3 phase transition at a high voltage of around 4.2 V, which is usually believed to be one of the main reasons for the electrochemical irreversibility in layered oxides, the increasing reversibility in D-NCM can be clearly recognized by the

Page 8

narrowing oxidation/reduction peaks in dQ/dV curves (Fig. 3b). Attributing to the strain retarding effect, the cyclability of D-NCM is greatly improved with high-capacity retention of 94.7% after 200 cycles, which is much higher than that of 60.2% for P-NCM (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 20). In addition, D-NCM sustained not only on the capacity but also on the voltage profiles over long-term cycling (Fig. 3d-e). Further electrochemical performance under various conditions including the cutoff voltage of 4.3 V and full cell results confirms the dramatic improvement of cycling stability in D-NCM (Supplementary Fig. 19-22). One general concern may also be raised from the blocking effect on lithium diffusion pathways that can lead to sluggish reaction kinetics by the newly introduced phase in the layered structure. To reveal lithium diffusion kinetics as a function of lithium content, we further performed potentiostatic intermittent titration technique (PITT) experiments at each SoC (Supplementary Fig. 24). The increased average D·(-π2/4L2) during the delithiation process in D-NCM demonstrates faster lithium diffusion kinetics, leading to a good rate performance (Supplementary Fig. 25), which eliminates such concern, if any. Further analysis of potential polarization at different cycles indicates the superior stability in electrochemistry of D-NCM owing to no obvious change in the voltage polarization compared to rapidly increased by 3.165 mV each cycle in P-NCM. All these results indicate the effectiveness of the structural stabilization effects of employing the strainretardant LLMO phase into the layered structure. To better understand the affirmative effects on the electrochemical properties by the proposed strain-retardant method, we further revealed the electronic, atomic, and mechanochemical structure changes underlying the materials at multiscale levels. The advanced synchrotron-based spectroscopic imaging technique by combining full-filed transmission X-ray microscopy (TXM) with X-ray absorption near edge structure (XANES) was used to track two-dimensional (2D)

Page 9

chemical phase mappings on the particles. Uniform color distribution of 2D TXM-XANES mappings (Fig. 4a-d) on both initially prepared P-NCM and D-NCM particles demonstrates a welldistributed Ni-related phase and homogeneous oxidation state of Ni element. More red color in the mappings of the aged P-NCM (Fig. 4b) indicates the augmenting degradation in Ni-related phase. In sharp contrast, D-NCM exhibited excellent stability owing to the similar homogeneous color distribution on the mappings after 200 cycles (Fig. 4d). Statistically analysis of the whiteline peak position confirms the high stability of D-NCM based on the slight changed electronic structure (Fig. 4e). Barely changed XANES spectra for all the metals collected at electrode level (Supplementary Fig. 26) exemplify the stabilized electronic structure in D-NCM after introduced LLMO phase. Besides the electronic and crystal structure, local structural evolutions could be another critical factor that thoroughly affects the electrochemistry in the layered oxides, by the newly employed perovskite phase. Atomic pair distribution function (PDF) analysis for both samples at the pristine and cycled states with the layered structure model obtained by XRD refinements demonstrates the maintained long-range crystal structure (Supplementary Fig. 27-28 and Supplementary Table 6-7). The first atomic peak of P-NCM shifted to low-r by 0.03 Å indicates the reduced TM−O bond length, which may be determined by the increasing bond strength that is associated with the growing oxygen vacancy after oxygen loss over long-term cycling.39 The sharp decrease in the second G(r) peak intensity revealed a weak TM−TM atomic interaction in the aged P-NCM. This weak interaction is highly associated with the migration of TM ions to the Li-layer, corresponding to the increasing cationic intermixing and/or the vacancy formation at TM site with their dissolution into the electrolyte.40 The G(r) profiles of D-NCM remain extremely persistent over long-term cycling, indicating a highly stable crystal structure.

Our primary goal is to eliminate severe mechanochemistry degradation caused by the accumulation of lattice strain via the proposed strain retarding approach. 3D nano-tomography results on the arbitrarily selected particle (Fig. 4f-h) display severe chemo-mechanical degradation in P-NCM compared to the pristine state (Supplementary Fig. 29). This mechanical degradation with the formation of cracks (Supplementary Fig. 30) can be explained by the accumulated stress induced by the repeated lattice fluctuation, which is believed as the main factors leading to electrochemical degradations.41 The development of pseudo zero-strain D-NCM can conclusively minimize the build-up of strain and eliminate grain boundary stress and cracks, maintaining morphological integrity and electrochemical stability (Supplementary Fig. 30-32). Further electrochemical impedance spectroscopy results (Supplementary Fig. 33) confirm the achieved pseudo zero-strain by the pinning effect of the strain-retardant perovskite phase in the layered structure, which remarkably enhances the mechanochemical stability leading to the superior electrochemical properties.

Conclusions In summary, we demonstrated a strain-retardant strategy by introducing a perovskite phase into Ni-rich layered structure. Attributing to their structural compatibility, the perovskite phase can well coherently grow into the layered structure. This coherent structure involves a robust matrix that can sustain the mechanochemical stability owing to large phase transition energy barriers of the electrochemically inactive strain-retardant phase. Methods Material preparations The Ni0.8Co0.1Mn0.1(OH)2 precursor was prepared by a typical co-precipitation method. NiSO4·H2O, MnSO4·H2O, and CoSO4·H2O material, at a molar ratio of 8:1:1, were dissolved in deionized water to obtain a 2.0 M solution. This solution was then slowly pumped into a 2 L tank and continuously stirred under nitrogen atmosphere. The solution pH value was maintained at 11.0 by the NaOH and NH3·H2O solutions. The reacting conditions of the temperature (60℃), pH value (11.0), and stirring speed (300 rpm) were carefully controlled and maintained constant. Then, Ni0.8Co0.1Mn0.1(OH)2 precursor was obtained after 12 h. Precursor was then mixed with LiOH·H2O (3 mol% excess) and calcined at 500oC for 5 h and 750oC for 10 h in oxygen at a ramp rate of 3 o

C/min, then cooled to room temperature naturally to achieve pristine LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 (P-

NCM). The modified material was obtained by a similar approach except for adding a process to mix La-source with the precursor. Precursor was firstly stoichiometrically mixed with La(NO3)3·6H2O (La: TMs = 1: 99, molar ratio) and LiOH·H2O (3 mol% excess) in water under vigorous stirring for 2 h. Then, the solvent was evaporated at 80℃. The obtained dry mixture was calcined at 500 oC for 5 h and 740 oC for 15 h in oxygen at a ramp rate of 3 oC/min, then cooled to room temperature naturally to achieve designed strain-retardant LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 (D-NCM).

XAS data was normalized, Fourier transformed, and analyzed using Athena and Artemis packages.42 X-ray diffraction (XRD) experiments were performed on the

The Williamson-Hall (W-H) method was used to estimate the lattice strain in the materials.36 The W-H analysis is based on the following equation 1, βhklcosθ = (kλ/D) + 4εsinθ

Physical characterizations The morphologies and elemental proportion of the samples were characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) using a FEI Quanta 3D FEG field-emission scanning electron microscope equipped with an energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) analyzer at an acceleration voltage of 20 kV. HR-TEM experiments were conducted with FEI Tecnai S-TWIN F20 equipment. HAADF-STEM images were taken with Titan ChemiSTEM equipment operating at 200 kV through an atomic-resolution analytical microscope.

Electrochemical measurements Electrochemical properties were tested in CR2032-type coin cells using a MACCOR cycler. Electrodes consisted of 80 wt% active materials, 10 wt% carbon black, and 10 wt% PVDF binder. Coin cells were assembled in an argon-filled glovebox, with moisture and oxygen levels of less than 0.5 ppm, using 1.2M LiPF6 in EC/EMC (3:7) electrolyte (GEN II with a water content below 20 ppm). The loading of active materials of each electrode is 4-5 mg/cm2. All batteries in this study were tested at room temperature. PITT experiments were conducted on both electrodes using by a multichannel potentiostat (Biologic VMP3) controlled by EC-Lab software. Each step composed
