Abstract Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have reached a certified 25.2% efficiency in 2019 due to their high absorption coefficient, high carrier mobility, long diffusion length, and tunable direct bandgap. However, due to the nature of solution processing and rapid crystal growth of perovskite thin films, a variety of defects can form as a result of the precursor compositions and processing conditions. The use of additives can affect perovskite crystallization and film formation, defect passivation in the bulk and/or at the surface, as well as influence the interface tuning of structure and energetics. In this review, we first discuss recent progress on additive engineering during perovskite film formation according to the following common categories: Lewis acid (e.g., metal cations, fullerene derivatives), Lewis base based on the donor type (e.g., O-donor, S-donor, and N-donor), ammonium salts, low-dimensional perovskites, and ionic liquid. Introduction Metal halide perovskites were first synthesized and reported in the 1970s, and they are in the form of ABX3, where A+ = methylammonium (CH3NH3+ or MA+), formamidinium (HC(NH2)2+ or FA+), cesium (Cs+), rubidium (Rb+); B2+ = lead (Pb2+) or tin (Sn2+); and X- = chlorine (Cl-), bromine (Br-), or iodine (I-).[1] In the 1990s, Mitzi et al. further developed more advanced solution processing as well as the understanding of structural and optoelectronic properties of these hybrid materials.[2] However, these hybrid materials did not gain widespread interest until the first few reports in 2009–2012 on solar cell application using MAPbI3.[3-5] In 2009, Miyasaka et al. first reported that the organic metal halide perovskites (MAPbI3 and MAPbBr3) could function similarly as dye molecules to absorb sunlight, and they were subsequently applied as the sensitizers in dye-sensitized solar cells (DSSCs); by using a liquid electrolyte (common to DSSCs), a non-stable power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 3.8% was demonstrated.[3] In 2012, Park et al. reported all-solid-state perovskite solar cells (PSCs) with a PCE of 9.7% employing a solid-state hole-transport layer¾2,2’,7,7’-tetrakis(N,N-di-pmethoxyphenylamine)-9,9’-spirobifluorene (spiro-OMeTAD).[4] Around the same time, Snaith et al. also reported spiro-OMeTAD-based solid-state PSCs with PCEs >10%.[5] These pioneering studies triggered an explosive research effort worldwide to improve the PSC fabrication with rapid advances demonstrated on optimizing device architectures and perovskite properties.[6-10, 11-14] At

2. Progress on Additive Engineering during Perovskite Formation 2.1. Lewis Acid A Lewis acid with the capability of accepting a pair of nonbonding electrons has the capability to passivate electron-rich defects, which can also be considered as Lewis bases.[31] The addition of a Lewis acid in perovskite can form a Lewis adduct with the undercoordinated halides and PbI3- through coordinate bonding or ionic bonding, eliminating the corresponding defects or traps and reducing undesired non-radiative recombination for longer carrier lifetimes. In this section, we divide Lewis acid into two catergories: metal cations and fullerene derivatives We disscuss the detailed effects of different strategies in each part.

2.1.1. Metal Cations Positively charged metal cations are effective passivating agents via ionic bonding and other electrostatic interactions with the negatively charged defects in perovskites. PbI2 is one of the typical Lewis acids.[32] Chen et al. reported an approach for self-induced passivation in MAPbI3

Progress on Additive-assisted Interface Optimization The photovoltaic process requires two successive steps: photoinduced charge generation via light absorption and separation/extraction of charge carriers to the external circuit to generate electric power.[162] An ideal selective contact should not deteriorate the light-absorbing layer and also should not induce and/or accelerate degradation within the device. In addition, there are also no energy losses when photogenerated carriers are injected from the light-absorbing material into the selective contact, minimum recombination at the contact interfaces, and the Fermi level of its corresponding carrier is maintained at the interface without any energy loss.[163] As reported previously, capacitive current probably originated from ion migration and charge accumulation can generate capacitor components near the ETL/perovskite and perovskite/HTL junctions.[164] This means that interfaces and bulk perovskite near interface are critical in controlling the hysteresis. Thus, interfacial engineering and bulk perovskite engineering are both necessary toward hysteresis-free PSCs.[165] The interface between ETL/perovskite absorber and perovskite absorber/HTL are critical to the performance of PSCs. In addition, perovskite/electrode interface[166] and carrier transport layer/electrode interface[167] are also crucial for efficient extraction of charge carriers and the improvement of moisture resistance. The presence of dangling bonds on the surface of charge transport layers can generate interfacial trap states, affecting charge extraction from the perovskite absorber layer with enhanced surface recombination and reduced PCE. Moreover, these surface defects may also act as crystallization sites, affecting perovskite nucleation.[109] This motivates the development of interlayer materials and methods for contact passivation. 4. Conclusion and Outlook A PCE exceeding 25% has been achieved in PSCs with unprecedented rapid development. But the commercialization of PSCs will require synergistically developing high-efficiency devices at large areas along with long-term operation stability as a competitive PV technology. The stability of the perovskite layer is usually optimized by compositional engineering, the application of low-D materials and additives engineering. On the other hand, incorporating proper charge-transport layers and electrodes or interfacial modifiers into PSCs are also considered as effective ways to improve device stability and efficiency of PSCs. Tailoring functional additives to passivate the defects in perovskite films and interfacial traps have proven to be effective approaches to reduce energy loss and improve PSC performance. Many groups have investigated the possible mechanisms of additive-engineered perovskite formation, but some in-situ characterization techniques are valuable for fully understanding how the additives control perovskite crystallization. To guide the design of efficient additives, we need to fully characterize the types and density of defects that form in different perovskite materials and gain a fundamental understanding of their impact on electronic transport properties. The crystal structure of passivation agents is also important for revealing the role of additives in perovskite formation. Comprehensive studies need to be conducted to reveal the effects and functions of additives with different molecular structures. In addition, standard characterization protocols should be established to properly evaluate the role of additives on the performance and stability of PSCs. In principle, a molecular library could be established by investigating the effect of functional groups, conjugated system, and the substituent of the additives on the passivation capacity. With research data accumulated and shared among the entire research field, we can use machine learning to discover the underlying principles for selecting materials and optimizing devices.
