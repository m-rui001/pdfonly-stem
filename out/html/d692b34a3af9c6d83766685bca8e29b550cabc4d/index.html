1. Introduction Recent developments in the construction industry, seeking out a way to advance conventional construction materials, have stimulated the use of different materials at the nanometre scale [1-7]. Graphene-based nanosheets (GNS), possessing extraordinary chemical and physical properties [8, 9], have exhibited potential to enhance crucial properties of construction materials such as mechanical strength and durability [10, 11]. In addition, GNS can provide useful functionalities such as selfcleaning, self-sensing, electromagnetic shielding and thermal interfacing [12-14]. Similar to other nanomaterials (e.g. nanosilica, nanoalumina, titania), GNS are likely to be increasingly employed in the construction industries [1, 15-18]. This great interest in GNS is associated with: (1) their potential in enhancing the mechanical properties and adding invaluable functionalities to materials at very low addition ratio; (2) the increasing knowledge about their nanoscale properties and new applications of these materials and (3) their decreasing manufacturing cost due to the ongoing development of largescale manufacturing processes. Graphene is the basic structural unit for graphitic materials of any dimension. It is composed of a single-layer sheet of carbon atoms closely packed into a two-dimensional (2D) (with thickness of 0.335 nm) honeycomb framework [19]. It can be wrapped up into 0D fullerenes, rolled into 1D nanotubes or stacked into 3D graphite [20] (Fig. 1). Although the rapid progress on graphene-based cementitious composites has benefited from the relatively mature research on other carbon-based nanomaterials, especially carbon nanotubes (CNTs) [20], the use of graphene in concrete has some intrinsic advantages over them. Compared to CNTs, for example, graphene is: 2-dimensional, presenting larger specific surface area (SSA); easier to produce in large quantities with fully reproducible properties; less hazardous for health and environment; and more easily dispersed in a media [21, 22]; which makes it more attractive for various applications in construction materials.

Fig. 1. Graphene is a 2D building material for carbon materials of all other dimensions. It can be wrapped up into 0D buckyballs, rolled into 1D nanotubes or stacked into 3D graphite [20]. Different forms of GNS, with properties and microstructure derived from pristine graphene, are also often employed in construction materials. Graphene oxide (GO), the most researched GNS in cement composites, also consists of monolayer sheets with a hexagonal carbon network. The main difference from graphene is that GO bears hydroxyl, epoxide, carboxyl and carbonyl functional groups on its surface and thus exhibits higher reactivity with cement and superior dispersion in the matrix, making it attractive for cementitious composites [23, 24]. Reduced graphene oxide (rGO) has less functional groups in its structure as compared to GO, presenting intermediate properties between GO and graphene. Graphene nanoplatelets (GNPs) are comprised of layers of graphene sheets (typically 10 < n < 100, with n being number of layers) with thickness less than 100 nm and diameter of several micrometres. Due to its increased thickness, GNPs are a cheaper alternative to graphene [25] and therefore the preferred form of GNS for structural composites [10]. However, its smaller specific surface area compared to single graphene sheets decreases its reinforcement efficiency. Graphene can also be doped with nitrogen and boron atoms [26], or functionalized with chemical and biologic groups [27], which enables the production of modified graphene with a wide range of functional properties. GNS Properties The great interest in the past decade on using GNS to fabricate novel construction composites is not casual. Graphene and its derivatives display a palette of remarkable chemical, mechanical, thermal and electrical properties that can be exploited to reinforce, increase the durability and provide smart functionalities to construction materials. Most of the extraordinary physicochemical properties of GNS arise from its 2D microstructure, which is comprised by adjacent carbon atoms linked by alternating single and double covalent bonds. This unique microstructure makes graphene the strongest, hardest, most impermeable, most stretchable material ever found [29, 30]. In addition, graphene is chemically inert, presents excellent thermal conductivity and unique electrical and optical properties [31]. Graphene’s mechanical properties have been consistently predicted through experimental and theoretical methods [47]. Atomic force microscope (AFM) nanoindentation experiments showed that individual graphene sheets (with a thickness of 0.335 nm) exhibits a Young’s modulus of 1.0 TPa, with an intrinsic strength of 130 GPa, which is 200 times larger than that of steel [32]. Graphene can also bear an elastic deformation of more than 20% without fracturing [32]. rGO (with a thickness of 1.0 nm) also exhibits an extraordinary Young’s modulus of 0.25 TPa and a high flexibility [48]. These experimental results are consistent with theoretical and computational studies. Molecular mechanics model [49], molecular dynamics (MD) simulations [50] and density functional perturbation theory (DFPT) [51] computed the Young’s modulus of graphene to be 1.06, 1.026 and 1.050 TPa, respectively. The exceptional mechanical properties of graphene are derived from the strong in-plane covalent bond and atomic thickness [52], and they have been exploited to nanoreinforce a wide range of composites, including concrete, by either bridging cracks at nanoscale and/or by modifying the microstructure of the matrix material [34]. The tightly-spaced carbon atoms and high electron-density of the atomic structure of graphene, combined to its astounding strength and flexibility, also makes graphene the thinnest and most impermeable material fabricated until date [39]. Bunch et al. [53] measured the permeability of graphene to several gases using a graphene-sealed microchamber device. By applying a pressure difference of 1–5 atm, it was demonstrated that a one atom-thick graphene sheet does not allow even the smallest molecules, such as helium, to pass through. Theoretical first-principles density functional calculations also determined that the energy necessary for a molecule, such as oxygen, to permeate through a graphene layer is prohibitively large, and does not occur at any temperature at which graphene is stable [54-57]. Due to this unique impermeability, GNS can function as physical barrier for aggressive agents, water and/or air components, and have thus been employed to create composites with enhanced durability, such as anti-corrosion epoxies [58, 59] and concrete with higher durability [60, 61]. In addition to excellent physical and mechanical behaviour, GNS display outstanding electrical and thermal properties that are not found in any other material [31]. It is estimated that the electrical conductivity of graphene can reach 108 S/m [39], which is higher than highly conductive metals such as copper [40]. Graphene also exhibits extremely high thermal conductivity, ranging from ∼3000– 5300 W m−1 K−1 [41-45], exceeding the conductivity of silver by one order of magnitude. The extraordinary conductivities of graphene are associated with the high mobility of electrons and phonons on its surface, which means that both electronic charges and lattice vibrations can travel extremely fast in the graphene structure [6]. The transport properties of GNS have been proven useful to provide smart functionalities to construction materials. For example, piezoelectric concrete

structures with self-sensing capabilities can be created by exploiting GNPs’ electrical conductivity [62-65]. GNS can be also used to change the thermal conductivity of construction composites and improve the thermal efficiency of buildings [66-71]. The potential of GNS to transform construction materials is not solely related to these unique properties, but also to the graphene’s geometry. Thanks to its 2D structure, a single sheet of graphene exhibits a specific surface area of ~2360 m2 g-1 [35], more than twice to that of CNT [36]. The large surface area of graphene is important because it maximizes the interaction of surrounding materials or environment with its superb electrical, thermal, chemical and surface properties, reducing its rate of addition into the composite. Such interactions can involve transfer of electrons, phonons, mechanical stresses, as well physical and chemical bonds, and are responsible for reinforcing and providing the useful functionalities to composite materials at very little addition ratios. For example, less than 0.1%wc GNS (‘% by weight of cementitious materials’ is abbreviated as ‘%wc’) is generally enough to provide a large area for nucleation reactions of cementitious materials, modifying its microstructure [72]. The large aspect ratio also allows GNS to function as an antimicrobial agent, by wrapping and depleting the resources of living cells [73]. In addition, GNS are also suitable for self-cleaning coatings thanks to its large rough surface [74]. The stability in different environments is another remarkable property of GNS. At moderate temperatures, graphene is a stable nanomaterial and exhibits great chemical inertness. According to ab initio calculations, when a carbon structure is composed by more than 24,000 atoms, graphene is the most stable carbon phase [35, 75, 76]. Regarding GO, it may undergo spontaneous chemical modifications at ambient conditions, displaying a decrease in the oxygen-to-carbon ratio (O/C) over time and in the number of epoxide groups [77-79]. However, it has been demonstrated that this decomposition of GO to produce either O2 and H2O is energetically and geometrically hindered [80], and even when it occurs, such modifications do not compromise the structural integrity of GO [79]. Nevertheless, the stability of GNS (and its astounding properties) under the harsh environments that construction materials are exposed to have not been directly researched yet. As national standards are getting progressively stricter and structures need to be designed to last for decades, it is crucial to understand the durability behaviour of GNS and its properties in such environments. 3. GNS Fabrication Although scientists were aware of the existence of graphene layers since the 1960s, a single layer of graphene was first isolated in a reproducible manner only in 2004 [19], being responsible for the explosive growth of interest in this nanomaterial [81, 82]. In the first method, few layers of graphene were prepared by mechanically peeling off highly oriented pyrolytic graphite. Although graphene produced from this approach displays only a few micrometres with irregular shapes and uncontrolled orientation [81], it is highly reliable for the production of single layer graphene nanosheets [19, 81, 82]. With the great interest on graphene and its outstanding properties, much has been developed from the first method for graphene production [83]. Generally, there are two main routes to synthesize graphene: bottom-up and top-down. Although both are used for producing graphene sheets, the yielded products from the two manufacturing concepts have different characteristics and thus are targeted for different purposes. Bottom-up methods comprise the growth of graphene with precise control of layers and defects. This approach is more costly and generally suitable for high-end technology industries, such as electronics, anti-corrosion coating and solar cells. On the other hand, top-down methods are more suitable for massive and cheaper production where defect-free graphene is generally not required, such as nanomaterials for concrete. Top-down production comprises the extraction of graphene layers from the bulk graphite. This process is based on the fact that the stacked layers of graphite are bonded by weak van der Walls forces, which can be broken by applying mechanical, chemical or electrochemical energy to yield few layers of graphene [81, 82, 84, 85]. However, due to the low productivity and high cost, extracting pristine graphene is unsuitable for large-scale use [86]. Using graphite oxide as a precursor for the large scale GO synthesis, therefore, is by far the most popular top-down method [77]. GO can be more easily separated compared to graphene and the presence of hydrophilic functional groups allows the production of the nanosheets in aqueous media without any surfactants [29, 77, 86].

To synthesize GO, graphite is firstly oxidized by the Hummers method in a mixture of concentrated sulfuric acid (H2SO4), sodium nitrate (NaNO3) and potassium permanganate (KMnO4) [77, 82]. H2SO4 intercalates the graphite layers, enlarge the volume between them and then oxidize the layers. Subsequently, ultra-sonication is used to separate the graphite oxide into individual GO sheets [82, 86] followed by a purification process to remove the remaining ions (i.e. K +, H+, Mn2+ and SO42−) from the GO structure. Finally, GO can be reduced into rGO via several available approaches including hydrazine vapours or solution (NH2–NH2) [84, 86, 87], alkaline conditions [82], photocatalysis [88], plasma methods [89, 90] or annealing [91]. Introduction of sulfonic groups can preserve the dispersibility of the rGO in water [92]. The Hummers method for fabrication of graphene is illustrated in Fig. 3.

GNS Applications in Enhancing Concrete Cementitious materials are the most commonly used materials for the construction of buildings and infrastructures mainly due to their high availability, cost efficiency and excellent compressive strength. However, poor resistance to cracking, low tensile strength and strain capacities are considered the major limitations of these materials. Numerous research efforts have been directed toward addressing these problems and great achievements have been made using various reinforcing materials such as steel bars, fibres, and more recently nanomaterials. While the incorporation of conventional steel bars and fibres improve the tensile strength of structural materials by inducing delay in the development of microcracks, nanomaterials provide even more effective reinforcement as they control cracks at nano level (at the initiation stage) before they develop into microsize cracks.

Page 14

Nanoparticles (e.g. SiO2, TiO2) with large surface areas have been extensively explored for their ability to act as seeding sites in cement paste and as pore fillers to produce dense microstructure. However, due to their low aspect ratio, these zero-dimensional nanomaterials are unable to arrest cracks and therefore lack the reinforcing effects. CNTs have also been widely studied as promoters for reinforcement in cementitious materials at nanoscale [99]. However, their poor dispersion property and weak bonding with cement matrix limit their application in cement composites. GNS, on the other hand, with planar structure and much higher aspect ratio, generate more potential sites for their interaction and bonding with the cement matrix. GO, in particular, with abundant surface functionalities, shows high dispersibility in water and potential to control and modify the microstructure of the cementitious matrix. Consequently, GNS have recently attracted tremendous attention as promising alternatives for the nanoscale reinforcement of cement-based composites and other structural materials.

4.1.

Strength Enhancement

Laboratory data reported in the literature, as summarised in Table 2, show significant improvements in strength properties of the cementitious materials with the inclusion of relatively low weight percentage levels of GNS. For example, Duan et al. [72, 100] reported an improvement of 41–59% and 15–33% in flexural and compressive strength, respectively, with only 0.05%wc loading of GO nanosheets in an ordinary Portland cement (OPC) matrix. The outstanding reinforcement of the matrix by the GO nanosheets was attributed to the strong interfacial adhesion between GO and cement matrix (Fig. 4). They described a toughening mechanism for these composites similar to that reported earlier for graphene ceramic composites [101] and GO reinforced epoxy polymers [102]. Unlike the plain matrix in which cracks usually traverse in a straight-through manner, in a graphene-cement composite, cracks are arrested and cannot pass through the graphene sheets (Fig. 5). GO sheets block and divert the in-plane propagation of micro cracks.

The overall results suggested that the performance of rGO was better than that of other nanomaterials, despite the significantly lower amounts that were used in the paste. For example, the addition of only 0.02%wc of rGO increased the 7-days flexural strength up to 70% as compared to control paste. In another work, Sharma et al. [105] compared the mechanical properties of functionalized-CNTs (FCNTs) and GO cement nanocomposites at same dosages (ranging between 0.125%wc and 1.00%wc). GO showed more uniform dispersion and higher possibility of interfacial adhesion with the hydration products as compared to FCNTs, which resulted in a better reinforcing action (in terms of compressive strength). With respect to control specimens, the highest enhancement of 77% in compressive strength was obtained for GO composites at 1%wc loading compared to that of 58% for FCNT composites at 0.5%wc loading. In terms of tensile strength, FCNTs composites showed slightly higher enhancement (40%) compared to GO (37.5%) composites with the same loadings (0.25%wc). The combination of nanomaterials, either 0D or 1D, with GNS have also exhibited interesting outcome. Hu and co-workers [18] found that the combination of GO and single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNTs) displayed an excellent co-effect, inducing a 72.7% enhancement in fracture strength of cement, which was larger than those obtained with GO (51.2%) or SWCNTs (26.3%). Zhou et al. [106] and Lu et al. [107] investigated mechanical properties of cement paste reinforced by hybrid GO/multi-walled carbon nano-tubes (MWCNTs) and also found the combination exhibited an excellent co-effect on the mechanical properties of the cement, which was higher than cement paste reinforced by MWCNTs or GO. For 0D nanomaterials, recent study [108] demonstrated that SiO2 nanoparticles/GO hybrids present better effects on the compressive strength of cement pastes than SiO2 nanoparticles or GO nanosheets alone for all investigated ages and nanomaterial loading ratio. At microscale, the integration of both carbon fibres (CF) and GO is promising as well [109]. On the other hand, variations on GNS characteristics and functionalities and their different influence on cement composites have been scarcely considered in the literature. Sharma et al. [110] studied the effect of size of the GO nanosheets on the mechanical properties of cement nanocomposites. It was found that the structural and mechanical properties of the composites were strongly dependent on the size of incorporated GO sheets. For example, composites prepared using larger GO sheets (14 nm thick and 900 nm average size) improved the compressive strength by a maximum of 63%, while the same loading ratio of smaller and thinner GO nanosheets (3 nm thick and 100 nm average size) formed superior crystalline structures with a maximum strength improvement of 86%. Another study by Abrishami and Zahabi [111] demonstrated that incorporation of amine functional groups on GO exhibited positive effects on its reinforcing efficiency. Addition of NH2-GO further reduced the porosity and improved the compressive and flexural strength of mortars by 23% and 38% respectively, compared to pure GO. Hu et al. [112] studied the compressive strength and microstructure of GO-doped cements treated with different curing process methods and compared with those of GO-free samples. The compressive strength of GO-free cement was 14.3 ± 0.2 MPa. Addition of 0.5%wc of GO increased the strength of the cement to 19.4 ± 0.9 MPa. Curing treatments further enhanced the strength, leading to a sequence: Microwave-cured GO-cement (32.4 ± 0.7 MPa)> Microwave and water-cured GO-cement (27.6 ± 0.8 MPa) > Water-cured GO-cement (23.1 ± 0.2) > GO-cement without curing (19.4 ± 0.9 MPa). Based on characterization data, it was shown that the combination of GO doping and microwave-curing greatly accelerated cement hydration, resulting in a regular and compact structure that lead to the observed high compressive strength. Recently, GNS has also been utilized to further increase the mechanical strength of dense cementbased matrices, demonstrating the great potential of employing graphene nanomaterials to produce ultra-high performance concrete (UHPC). For example, by increasing the content of GNPs (2-10 nm thick) up to 0.3%wc, Meng and Khayat found that the tensile strength and energy absorption capacity of the UHPC was increased by 45% and 153%, respectively [113]. The improvements were attributed to a combination of ‘bridging effect’ and ‘filler effect’ of the GNPs. In addition, mixtures with high GNPs content presented a slip-hardening behaviour at the post-cracking zone. Another study showed that UHPC mixtures with 0.01%wc of GO had an increase in compressive strength from 117 to 127 MPa and in flexural strength from 8.9 to 10.0 MPa [114]. The mechanical properties of cementitious materials other than Portland cement, such as magnesium potassium phosphate cement (MKPC), geopolymer cement (GPC), and alkali-activated slag (AAS)

have been enhanced with GNS as well. MKPC is a high early strength cementitious material whose mechanical properties are directly linked to the growth and crystallinity of magnesium potassium phosphate hexahydrate. Lu et al. [115] investigated the influence of GO on the MKPC paste and found that the incorporation of 0.05%wc of GO resulted in the crystallization of denser microstructures compared to MKPC paste without GO. Although, the FTIR spectra indicated that there was no chemical bonding between the hydration products and the incorporated GO, the flexural and compressive strength of the MKPC paste were enhanced by 6.8% and 8.3%, respectively. On the other hand, higher GO addition (0.1%wc) had negative effect on the cement microstructures and its mechanical properties. This was associated to the high absorption of water by GO, reducing the water available for the hydration of the MKPC. Geopolymer cement, similar to OPC, exhibits brittle behaviour and relatively low flexural strengths [116]. Saafi et al. [117] incorporated GO in a geopolymer matrix and observed that 0.35%wc GO increased the flexural strength, Young's modulus and flexural toughness by 134%, 376% and 56%, respectively. The authors suggested that the GO actually cross-linked and reduced to rGO within the geopolymer matrix, increasing the composite’s mechanical properties and greatly reducing its overall porosity. The in-situ reduction of GO in the alkaline environment of geopolymer precursor and its reinforcing effect on the geopolymer matrix was further confirmed using more detailed characterization by Yan et al. [118]. Ranjbar et al. [119] used thermally reduced graphene oxide nanoplatelets to systematically investigate their reinforcement effect on fly ash based geopolymer composites. Their results showed that adding 1%wc GNPs improves the compressive and flexural strength of the geopolymer by 1.44 and 2.16 fold, respectively. Jia and co-workers [120] reported that GO nanosheets can modify the rheological properties of geopolymer precursor, tightly anchor to the geopolymer surface, encapsulate individual geopolymer grains, and form a continuous 3D network throughout the whole GO/geopolymer nanocomposites. The network enabled, for the first time, the GO/geopolymer nanocomposite 3D printing that could not be realized by pure geopolymer. Recently, Yang and co-workers [121] studied the effects of the GO on AAS mortars. Addition of 0.01%wc of GO increased the composites flexural strength by 20% after 7 days. In addition, the microstructural study of AAS pastes showed the formation of a layered double hydroxides (LDHs) in the GO-AAS system. GO and its abundant oxygen containing groups have been also exploited to increase the hydrophilicity and roughness of carbon fibres (CF) [122-125], improving its dispersion and interfacial zone with cement matrix. For example, Chen et al. [126] and Lu et al. [127] demonstrated that CFs coated with GO present better dispersion in cement matrix. In addition, SEM observation indicates that the GO coating functions as a template for hydration of cement and give rise to a stronger bond between CF and cement matrix. [127] also reported a further improvement when the GO-coated CF were pre-dispersed in GO solution.

4.2.

Durability Enhancement

Durability is a major concern in construction industry. Durable construction materials not only reduce the infrastructure’s maintenance cost, but also ensure its standard design lifespan. Concrete, widely used in today’s infrastructure, is susceptible to various forms of deterioration, including ingress of aggressive elements (such as CO2, SO42- and Cl- ), alkali silica reaction (ASR), calcium leaching, freezing and thawing cycles, fire, thermal cracking and bacterial attack [149], which can significantly reduce its service life. In addition to more durable materials, continuous monitoring of civil infrastructures is also of great importance to rectify the infrastructure’s integrity and ensure its lifespan. Recent literature, shows that addition of GNS can have promising effects on the durabilityrelated properties of cementitious materials. However, the number of studies are still very limited, as observed in Table 3, and further in-depth investigations on the mechanisms of the influence of GNS on long-term properties of concrete are needed. Durability of concrete to the aggressive agents is mainly governed by its pore structure and permeability. Recent research shows that incorporating a small content of carbon-based nanoparticles such as CNT and GNS can increase the durability of concrete by optimizing the pore structure and altering the transport properties of the cement matrix [60, 61, 143, 150-153]. Experimental data have shown that the incorporation of carbonaceous materials into cement can reduce the water permeability, water sorptivity and gas permeability, and thus enhance its resistance to aggressive elements [60, 70, 154, 155]. The effect of GO on nano and microporosity of cement paste have been investigated by few authors. Using mercury intrusion porosimetry (MIP), Gong et al. [133] reported that the total porosity of cement pastes with 0.03%wc of GO was 13.5% lower than that of its plain cement counterpart. It was observed that GO decreases the content of pores between 10 and 100 nm whereas it increases the nanopores smaller than 10 nm. Similar trend was observed for mortars in another study [60], also using MIP. This nanostructure modification of the cement matrix is partially responsible in significantly reducing the penetration of aggressive agents through the matrix of GO-composites. For example, Mohammed et al. [60] reported that mortars with 0.01%wc of GO presented a five-fold reduction in chloride penetration compared to plain mortars, as shown in Fig. 7. Furthermore, addition of 0.03%wc of GO in mortars decreased their initial water sorptivity ratio from 0.00046 to 0.00020 mm s-½ [60]. The authors suggested that the layered structures of GO may also trap and reduce the chloride ingression depth. More recently, Mohammed et al. [156] also found that mortars with GO, after exposure of 18 months in a carbonation chamber, displayed a five-fold decrease in carbonation depth compared to the reference sample. Binding of Ca2+ ions on the surface of GO was also suggested as one of the reasons for the durability enhancement.

The freeze-thaw resistance of concrete can also be increased by incorporation of GNS. Mohammed et al. Cement mixes with GO also displayed less scaling damages on the specimen’s surfaces of GO. In addition to the higher compressive strength, the enhancement of freeze–thaw resistance of mixtures with GO was associated with the increase of nanopores over mesopores, as less temperature is required to freeze the water in smaller pores [162]. [164] reported that incorporation of 5 and 10%wc of graphene can increase the thermal diffusivity (cm2/s) of cement pastes by 25 and 75% at the first 44 h of curing. By quasi-steady-state method, Cui et al. [165] observed that 5%vc loading of nanographite platelets (thickness = 1 – 5 nm) increased the thermal conductivity of mortar composites from 1.1 to 1.95 W/(m.K), while decreasing their specific heat by 17%. More recently, Chu et al. [166] observed that 0.01%wc of graphene sulfonate nanosheets can increase the thermal diffusivity of sacrificial concrete exposed to temperatures from 25 to 1000 oC and improve the residual mechanical strength of the composites. These results indicate that graphene can function as a heat sink in cement composites, although its effectiveness in preventing thermal cracking still needs to be confirmed. Due to its electrical conductivity and piezoresistivity-based strain sensing ability, GNS can promote self-sensing capability of cement composites [62-64, 167]. Saafi and co-workers [117] investigated the electrical and piezoresistive behaviour of the geopolymers containing in-situ reduced GO nanosheets. By increasing the content of rGO from 0 to 0.35%wc, the electrical conductivity of the composite improved from 0.77 S m-1 to 2.38 S m-1. rGO also increased the gauge factor by up to 112% and 103% for specimens subjected to tension and compression, respectively. rGO’s electrical conductivity has also been exploited to create cement paste sensors, which can measure the chloride ion penetration in mortars independently of RH [155]. Pang et al. [63] noted that the increasing addition of GNPs considerably reduces the electrical resistance of OPC mortars (Fig. 8). According to the authors, as the addition exceeds 3.6% of GNPs (by volume of mortar), the conductive nanomaterial can create a continuous electrical path along the specimen and the composite’s electrical conductivity became insensitive to its moisture content, enabling reliable damage measurements in actual applications [14].

Proposed Enhancement Mechanisms

The reinforcement mechanisms of the GNS, especially GO, on cementitious materials have been proposed in several studies. A hypothesis, proposed by Duan et al. [72], for such efficient reinforcement of GO is that the nanosheets have significant seeding effect on the cement hydration kinetics, providing abundant additional sites for nucleation and growth of hydration products. This view is supported by experimental measurements. Based on an isothermal calorimetry, Li et al. [136] observed an acceleration of the hydration reaction with the addition of GO to the Portland cement paste. By means of XRD and FTIR, Li et al. [136] also concluded that GO, owing to the abundance of oxygen-containing functional groups, provides adsorption sites for both water molecules and cement components and accelerates the hydration rate of cement. More recently, Lu et al. [175] showed via X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and isothermal calorimetry that GO rapid adsorbs Ca2+ at early stages and can thus accelerate the cement dissolution and hydration. Duan et al. [72] also proposed that GO sheets containing carboxylic acid groups can induce strong interfacial adhesion between GO and cement matrix and, owing to the outstanding mechanical performance of GO, the mechanical behaviour of the cement reinforced by GO is remarkably enhanced. The chemical reactions between carboxylic acid groups and the calcium silicate hydrate (C–S–H) or Ca(OH)2 of the cement matrix had previously been reported for the functionalized CNTscement system [103]. It was shown that the strong covalent bond between the CNTs and cement can increase the load-transfer efficiency from the matrix to the CNTs, and it is believed that the similar load-transfer mechanism occurs with GO. Other researchers [139, 142] proposed that the enhanced toughness of cement composites reinforced with GO is due to a possible template effect of the nanosheets. Based on SEM observations, the authors suggest that GO can regulate the microstructure of hydration products and form compact cross-linking structures. A space interlocking mechanism was also proposed by Lu et al. [107] for a GO/CNTs/cement paste system. They consider that GO sheets can interlock the cement matrix together by linking separate hydration products such as C–S–H and portlandite. Therefore, the loading is more efficiently transferred between hydration phases. In addition, a possible formation of a space interlocking structure, the [CNTs–GO–CNTs] structure, could help the load transfer from the cement matrix to the GO/CNTs reinforcement (Fig. 9). Similarly, Wang et al. [132] pointed out that chemical reactions between –COOH on the edge of GO nanosheets and the Ca2+ from C-S-H also give rise to a 3D network structure, which was accordingly considered the direct reason for the enhancement of mechanical properties of GO integrated cement composites.

Although the preceding studies provide great insight of the reinforcement mechanism of GNS on the micro to macroscopic scale properties of GNS-cement composites, the understanding of how GNS interact and modify the cement matrix at nanoscale (1 to 100 nm), in particular its influence on the CS-H nanostructure, is still limited. Studies at nanometre level, although important to understand concrete’s macroscopic performance, are hampered by the complexity associated with studying cement at this scale. The most obvious example is C-S-H, which even though has been scrutinized and debated for more than one century [176], has not had its nanostructure fully resolved yet [177]. Li et al. [178] experimentally investigated the influence of GO and other widely used nanomaterials (TiO2, SiO2, and MWCNT) on the morphology of C-S-H. TEM observations revealed that GO mainly acts as templates for C-S-H to grow on, a similar effect observed for CNTs. Further microscopic characterisation indicated that GO (and nano-TiO2) can induce the formation of nanocrystalline

Page 26

regions of C-S-H, while these C-S-H nanocrystals were not observed for the addition of nano-SiO2 or CNT. The presence of Si and Ca in these nanocrystals were confirmed by means of energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). Similar nanocrystals with similar size, lattice fringe spacing and diffraction pattern have been previously identified in thinned sections of cement paste, but they were, however, assigned as Ca(OH)2 nanocrystallites coexisting with the C-S-H gel phase [179]. Due to the insufficient evidential experimental results, the most recent efforts to resolve the interfacial molecular structure and bonding characteristics between GNS and C-S-H are based on computer modelling. Using empirical force field molecular dynamic (MD) simulations, Sanchez and Zhang [180] studied the molecular-scale energetic, structural, and dynamic behaviours of the interface between C–S–H and graphitic structures functionalized with different groups. It was found that the binding capacity of the C–S–H/graphene hybrids increases with the polarity of the functional group, indicating that polarity of GNS can be considered as an indicator to affinity to C–S–H. It was also revealed that Ca2+ counter-ions play a mediating role in the interfacial interactions (Fig. 10). In order to investigate the interaction between GO and C-S-H in a more realistic model, Hou et al. [181] performed reactive force field molecular dynamics. Similarly, the authors concluded that the mechanical strength of the C-S-H and GNS composites is mainly governed by the functional hydroxyl groups and the mediating Ca2+ counter-ions. To understand the reinforcing mechanism provided by graphene at the atomic level, Chen et al. [182] used MD to simulate the pull-out behaviour of graphene in a C-S-H matrix. It was reported that graphene’s pull-out from the matrix, differently from that generally observed for microfibres [183, 184], is not governed by friction only (Fig. 11). Due to its 2D geometry, graphene presents an additional constant resistance force risen from the unbalanced adhesion forces near the crack surface. In comparison to other matrixes, such as aluminium and polyethylene, the bond between C-S-H and graphene was the weakest (interfacial shear strength of 17 MPa). The weaker bond was associated with the water molecules in the C-S-H matrix and the spaces between graphene and C-S-H acting as lubricant. For the pull-out of GO in a C-S-H gel, on the other hand, Fan et al. [145] found an interfacial shear strength of 650 MPa. The significantly higher strength compared to pristine graphene may be associated with the C-O-Ca covalent bonds formed between GO and the matrix, and to the considerations that GO have a structurally unaffected carbon plane.

Challenges 5.1. GNS Large Scale Fabrication Although graphene no longer needs any further evidence for its importance in terms of its astonishing properties, due to the substantial volume of material employed in civil construction, any material that is used in this field must be readily available in great quantity and preferably at low costs. For new materials, they also need to offset the costs associated with changing a production culture and training. If these parameters are not fulfilled, the use of the new material, even though it presents outstanding performance, become limited to highly specialised applications. Many challenges are associated with the synthesis of graphene and its derivatives, which is still a recent technology and its production scale is thus small and relatively expensive. Firstly, the GNS properties are heavily affected during the synthesis of nanosheets and they are not as astounding as those of pristine graphene. Although perfect graphene sheets can be obtained from a variety of bottom-up methods, these are prohibitively expensive, and bulk quantities of graphene sheets are thus commonly obtained by reducing GO. Such process induces significant number of defects on graphene’s surface and the obtained rGO sheets have therefore lower electrical conductivity and mechanical strength compared to that of original graphene sheets achieved via Scotch-tape mechanical exfoliation [48, 185]. In addition, the GO fabricated by current large scale techniques exhibits variability in surface functionality as well as in sheet size [77]. As shown in the previous sections, differences in the surface chemistry and the surface area can result in different interfacial interaction between cementitious matrix and GNS, cement hydration rates and GNS dispersibility levels, affecting the effectiveness in enhancing the mechanical and durability properties of GNScontaining cement composites. Moreover, differences in functional properties may jeopardize the GNS application in smart composites. In relation to the manufacturing process of GNS, Lowe and Zhong [77] summarized the challenges to be addressed before the modified Hummers method to produce GO can be scaled up to industry level. Four main problems can be highlighted: (1) the lack of high quality natural graphite in large quantities (graphite with ordered stacking and without defects); (2) the hazards and costs involved with KMnO4. For example, Mn2O7, the product of the reaction between KMnO4 and H2SO4, can explode by itself at temperatures greater than 95 °C. Although K2FeO4 have been proposed to replace KMnO4, K2FeO4 is not produced on an industrial scale yet; (3) the instability of GO at ambient conditions. Dried GO is metastable and its overall oxygen-to-carbon ratio falls with time; (4) the challenge of purifying large

Page 28

volumes of dispersed GO. In addition, the wastewater displays high salt concentrations and acidity after purification, requiring further treatments. Despite all challenges, the synthesis of GNS, in particular the modified Hummers method, has become safer, cheaper, more eco-friendly and scalable over the past decade, presenting great promise to make GNS production sufficiently large and reproducible for the construction industry. Nevertheless, it is undeniable that improving the reinforcing efficiency of GNS, and thus decreasing the fabrication volume of GNS to fulfil the need of the construction industry, would be highly desirable from the economic and environment perspective. 5.2. GNS Dispersion in Cement Matrix Based on existing research studies, addition of a small amount of GNS can effectively enhance the mechanical strength and transport properties of cement-based materials. However, large inconsistency in the performance of GNS cement composites is observed, not only due to differences in the employed production processes, but more importantly due to the difficulties in realizing uniform GNS dispersion into cementitious matrix, one of the main challenges in the processing of GNS cement composites [144, 186, 187]. As the high surface area of GNS maximizes the interaction between the nanosheets and matrix, ensuring their good dispersion in the cement matrix is thus crucial for enhancements in mechanical and durability performance of cement composites. GNS agglomerates can have negative impacts on the properties of cement composites by creating voids, zones of weakness, and potential areas for concentrated stresses in the matrix [61, 146, 188, 189]. However, like CNTs and other nanomaterials, good dispersion of these nanosheets is particularly difficult to attain due to their high surface energy and strong Van der Waals interactions. In addition, the highly hydrophobic behaviour of some graphene derivatives, in particular GNPs, further complicate their dispersion in aqueous media. As a result, graphene sheets display a high tendency to form irreversible agglomerates or even stack again and form graphite [86]. Although GO, compared to other forms of GNS, can be better dispersed in aqueous media due to electrostatic repulsion and hydrophilicity [86, 190], its dispersion in cement based materials has been found to be challenging as well [191]. Due to the high concentration of alkaline ions (i.e. Ca2+, Na+, K+) in the solution of fresh cement pastes, the repulsion between GO nanosheets is weakened and they crosslink [147, 192, 193]. Consequently, severe aggregation is often observed in experiments in both simulated pore solution and cement paste [186], hampering the full utilization of GO to the mechanical improvement of cement-based materials. Some researchers have recently attempted, with relative success, to improve the dispersion of GNS in cement matrix by either mechanical or steric separation of the nanosheets. Due to the similarity in chemical structures [194], most of the compounds that had previously been used for the dispersion of CNTs have also been tested for their suitability in improving the dispersion of GNS in cement-based composites. Silica fume, for example, was used to facilitate the dispersion of CNFs or CNTs by acting as wedges to mechanically separate them [195-197]. Similarly, Duan et al. [186] and Bai et al. [198] used silica fume to help the dispersion of GO nanosheets in cement composites. Silica sand, behaving similarly silica fume, was also used by Duan et al. [191] to mechanically separate GO sheets to achieve better dispersion in cement mortar. Methylcellulose (MC), known as traditional surfactant, has been widely used for the dispersion of CNTs and CNFs due to its wettability, dispersibility and adhesion properties [199, 200]. MC was recently employed to disperse GNPs in aqueous solution via ultrasonic processing and subsequently incorporated into cement paste to investigate the mechanical properties of GNPs–cement composites [138]. Polycarboxylate-ether (PCE) based copolymer, widely used as a superplasticizer to cementbased materials, have been also a popular dispersing agent for GNS, either by simply mixing with the nanosheets [131, 140, 146, 201-204] or by copolymerization [205]. Naphthalene sulfonate-based superplasticizer has been reported as well to act as a dispersing agent for the GNPs and other carbonbased materials [14, 158, 203]. However, recent research has demonstrated that polycarboxylatebased superplasticizers are more effective than those ligno- or naphthalene-based in dispersing GO [206]. Another study also indicates that PCE can disperse GO in the cement matrix more efficiently compared to air entrainer and Gum Arabic [207].

As another potential solution to the dispersion of GNS in cement matrix, chemical vapour deposition (CVD) may be exploited to directly form the nanosheets on the surface of cement grains or admixtures. For example, CVD was utilized to attach carbon nanotubes and nanofibers onto cement particles and effectively solve the dispersion problem of CNTs in cement matrix [208]. Nevertheless, this interesting method has remained unexplored in the case of GNS. 5.3. GNS Influence on Cement Mix Workability The negative impact on the workability (rheological behaviour), a critical property of fresh cement mixtures, is another key challenge of incorporating GNS to cement composites [205]. For example, Pan et al. [72] observed that the addition of 0.05%wc of GO sheets lead to a reduction of the workability by about 42% of cement pastes with w/c ratio of 0.5, whereas Li et al. [128] and Shang et al. [209] observed, for the same GO content, a reduction of 25% for pastes with w/c = 0.4. Substantial reduction in the fluidity was also observed for GO-AAS mortars. Pan et al. [72] attributed the reduction in workability to the large surface area and hydrophilicity of GO nanosheets, which thus absorbs the available water in fresh mix to wet their surface [34, 72, 140]. However, Shang et al. [209] later demonstrated that the negative effect of GO on the workability of cement paste is mostly due to the flocculation between GO and cement grains by electrostatic interactions, which entraps large amount of free water. Similarly, Wang et al. [210] proposed that GO are adsorbed onto the cement grains’ surface and, due to the van der Waals attraction of the nanosheets, the cement particles attach to each other, decreasing the fluidity of the mixture (Fig. 12a). In addition, as shown in Fig. 12(b), reaction between –COOH groups in GO and metal ions in the hydration product occurs during absorption, producing –COO−. This can result in chemical cross-linking of GO nanosheets and the formation of GO agglomerates, which subsequently reduces free water and the workability of cement paste due to their high water entrapment capacity [134, 135].

Fig. 12. (a) Adsorption schematic of GO nanosheets on the cement surface; (b) substitution reaction of GO in the adsorption system [210]. To offset the negative effects of the GO on the workability of cement pastes, different approaches have been recently proposed. Wang et al. [211] proposed the use of fly ash in the GO-cement system. Based on quantitative analysis of the pastes rheological parameters, the authors suggested a mechanism governing the positive influence of fly ash on the fluidity of the GO-cement paste. As illustrated in Fig. 13, fly ash can interrupt the flocculation configurations of GO-cement paste, release the entrapped water and as result increase the mixture fluidity. The spherical shape, size gradation, and less water requirements of fly ash were considered as possible factors contributing to this phenomenon. Shang et al. [209] demonstrated that GO encapsulated silica fume (GOSF) renders the cement paste a higher fluidity compared with cement pastes with only silica fume. This behaviour was attributed to the synergetic effect of the surface activity of GO and the shape effect of silica fume. More recently, Wang et al. [212] observed that polyether amine functionalized-GO can increase the slump flow of cement pastes. The improvement in fluidity was attributed to the steric hindrance of the hydrophilic polyether amine chains.

Conclusions and Final Remarks Research in the past five years demonstrates that GNS has a great potential to improve the properties of new and existing construction materials. These improvements are often associated with the combination of the GNS unique properties with its two dimensionality. More specifically, the large aspect ratio of GNS along with its atomic thickness provide a large surface area for the nanosheets or their functional groups to interact with the cement matrix, which, as it has been observed in this review, can dramatically change the mechanical and durability properties of cement composites. Recent studies reviewed in this paper indicate that GNS applied in construction materials is currently an active area of research, featuring: the development of more efficient and environmental sustainable fabrication processes, attractive applications ranging from structural strength/durability improvement and self-sensing structures to thermally conductive composites, MD simulations and mechanism of interface interactions. GNS, especially in the form of GO and GNPs, present positive influence on the durability and mechanical performance of different types of cement composites, including OPC and not as ordinary magnesium potassium phosphate cement, geopolymer cement, and alkali-activated slag, carbon fibre reinforced cement and also recycled concrete. In addition, some compelling research has already been initiated for the use of GNS to endow construction materials self-sensing and advanced thermal properties. The mechanical reinforcement mechanisms are often associated to GNS’ nucleation effects on cement hydration and capability to interlock different hydration products. Although experimental studies on the nanoscale interaction between GNS and cement matrix are scarce, Molecular Dynamic simulations predict that functional groups of GO can induce strong interfacial adhesion with Ca2+ ions from the C-S-H. On the other hand, the improvements on durability, in particular on permeability to aggressive agents and freeze thaw resistance, are attributed to GNS’ ability in tuning the matrix porosity, reducing the number of larger pores while increasing that of nanopores. Nevertheless, GNS have not become widespread in the construction field yet. Before this can happen, challenges that still hinder their implementation in the construction materials need to be resolved. One of the main challenges is associated to difficulties regarding the safety, sustainability and reproducibility of synthesizing high quality and low cost GNS at industrial scale. In addition to fabrication issues, the performance of GNS cement composites varies significantly in the reported literature. Besides the different processes used for mixing the cement-based composite blends, the discrepancies arise mainly for two reasons: 1) difficulties of uniform GNS dispersion in the cement matrix and 2) the negative effect of GNS on the workability of fresh cement mixes. Recently, both problems have been addressed by addition of common concrete admixtures, such as fly ash, silica fume and surfactant agents, or by GNS functionalization. Nevertheless, this review has demonstrated that great development on fabrication methods of GNS and on properties of GNS-based composites occurred in a relative short time. In relation to the synthesis methods, less hazardous top-down approaches with higher yielding efficiency have been developed. At the same time, the use of GNS in composites has become more reliable as researchers

Page 31

obtain a better understanding on their reinforcing, dispersion, transport and electric properties. With the growing knowledge on this marvellous nanomaterial technology, it is expected that GNS applications for the development of novel construction materials will become more widely accepted in the construction field. However, research is still required in the formulation of practical graphenebased products before commercially viable materials can be developed.
