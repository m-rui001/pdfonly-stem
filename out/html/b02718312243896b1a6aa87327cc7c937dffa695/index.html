Three-dimensional (3D) printing is an additive manufacturing (AM) technique that has drawn much attention from industry to academia for the development of advanced materials, architectures, and systems for a broad range of applications: energy[1,2], biotechnology[3–5], microfluidics[6–8], electronics[9,10], and engineered composites[11–16]. Among the recently developed AM techniques[17], extrusion-based (e.g. robocasting or direct ink writing) are the most versatile due to simple printing mechanisms and low-cost fabrication processes.[18,19]

Additionally, extrusion type 3D printing has a broad material selection including ceramics, metal alloys, polymers and even edible materials. In extrusion-based 3D printing, ink properties are directly related to material preparation, printing strategy, and architectural design. To develop a good printable ink, it is important to endow materials with high viscosity and shear-thinning behavior to facilitate 3D printing. Recently, graphene oxide (GO) has shown noteworthy printing capabilities and unique viscoelastic properties as an aqueous dispersion wherein its rheological behavior varies considerably with the graphene oxide concentration.[20] With low graphene oxide concentrations, the dispersions exhibit liquid-like behavior which is unfit for 3D printing. For highly concentrated graphene oxide dispersions, the graphene oxide ink displays gel-like behavior with a high elastic modulus, which can print fine filaments or complex 3D architectures.[20] Based on this feature, 3D printing of graphene oxide has been successfully made into different structural forms such as nanowires[21], aerogel microlattices[14], and complex networks[22,23]. A majority of studies focus on the fundamentals of 3D printed graphene oxide materials and their structures. Highly concentrated graphene oxide inks have been shown to be directly printed into fine filaments and then a layer-by-layer deposition strategy can be utilized to develop diverse architectures with exceptional properties (e.g. high surface area, high electrical conductivity, good structural stability)[14,21,22]. Recently, studies about GO in 3D printing have mentioned only a few potential applications, particularly using the porous 3D graphene structures as supercapacitors[24–26]. In this manner, extended applications of 3D printed graphene oxide materials are awaiting exploitations, especially in miniature energy storage devices. Due to the unique viscoelastic properties and diverse applications of graphene oxide, it can be envisioned that a new class of GO-based composite inks containing various

functional components can be used to fabricate advanced 3D printed material structures, architectures, and systems[27]. To the best of our knowledge, GO has not been applied to 3D printing technique for making lithium-ion battery applications. Representative work about 3D printing of batteries was from Lewis’s group that they fabricated interdigitated electrodes by using 3D printer for a Li-ion microbattery[1]. They developed concentrated inks with proper rheological behaviors to enable the 3D printing process. Their aqueous inks mainly consisted of electrode active materials to provide battery capacity and cellulose-based viscosifiers to control viscosity. Due to the absence of conducting additives, the electrodes possessed much lower electrical conductivity (10-4-10-6 S/cm) although they were higher than the intrinsic electrical conductivity of active materials (10-9-10-13 S/cm), which significantly limited the electrochemical performance of battery. To improve the battery performance, GO can be a good option. GO can possess good electrical conductivity after thermal annealing and has proven to be a promising material in electrochemical energy storage applications, especially the lithium-ion batteries.[28] Except electrode ink design, electrolyte design is the other important consideration for the 3D printing of batteries. Conventional combustible and fluidic electrolyte needs to be avoided or to be used at a minimum amount. This is because the evaporation and possible leakage of electrolytes may bring safety concern and capacity failure to batteries. Solid-state electrolyte will be a good candidate to replace liquid electrolyte because of the balanced electrochemical properties, good mechanical properties, and relatively safer properties. Recently, Kim et al. in Lee’s group used stencil printing technique to achieve the 2D printing of solid-state lithium-ion batteries. In their design, they developed a printable solid-state electrolyte ink and by adding active materials and conducting additives for making printable electrode inks, they printed out batteries on arbitrary

objects of complex design/geometries.[29] Therefore, we envision that if proper rheological properties of electrolyte can be achieved, 3D printing of all-component batteries including electrodes and electrolyte can be realized, and so as to the extended fabrication of multidimensional/multiscale complex-structures of more energy storage devices. In this communication, we developed GO-based electrode composite inks and solid-state electrolyte inks to achieve all-component 3D printed lithium-ion batteries. The 3D printed lithium-ion batteries were successfully created using aqueous GO-based inks consisting of highly concentrated graphene oxide sheets as well as cathode and anode active materials. Note that using water as a green solvent makes this aqueous ink system feasible for processing and drying safety and low cost. Highly concentrated graphene oxide sheets provide the prerequisite viscosity to bind the electrode materials together and enable 3D printing. Lithium iron phosphate (LiFePO4, LFP) and lithium titanium oxide (Li4Ti5O12, LTO) were selected as the cathode and anode materials respectively for the demonstration purpose. It is anticipated that this process can be extended also to other active materials. To create high mass loading per unit area electrodes in an interdigitated battery configuration, fine filaments were extruded directly from a nozzle and then deposited layer-by-layer using a preprogrammed printing routine. Due to the shear stress induced by the nozzle, the GO flakes are aligned along the extruding direction, which enhances the electrode’s electrical conductivity. In addition, the GO flake’s intrinsically porous structure offers a large amount of surface area to load the LFP or LTO nanoparticles as well as house the electrolyte. Our demonstration of 3D printed lithium-ion batteries featured graphene oxide as a promising printable material in 3D printing manufacturing and printable energy storage applications[1,28].

Figure 1 is a schematic representation of the 3D printed lithium-ion batteries. The cathode and anode inks were prepared separately and stored in different syringes. As shown in Figure 1a, graphene oxide sheets and electrode nanoparticles are uniformly mixed together. Note that the anisotropic liquid crystalline behavior of the graphene oxide sheets in aqueous solution leads to well aligned filaments along the extruding direction due to the shear flow conditions[30]. The electrodes are printed layer-by-layer using these extruded filaments into an arranged interdigitated pattern (Figure 1b). Both electrodes were then freeze-dried to remove the water solvent and solidify the 3D structures. A thermal annealing process was also implemented to enable the formation of reduced graphene oxide. The channels between the two interdigitated annealed electrodes was filled with a polymer composite ink containing a mixture of poly(vinylidenefluoride)-co-hexafluoropropylene (PVDF-co-HFP) and Al2O3 nanoparticles. This 3D printed solid-state composite served as the electrically insulated separator as well as the gel polymer electrolyte as shown in Figure 1c.

Ink properties, especially the rheological behavior, significantly influence the 3D printing process and its performance. Figure 2 displays the rheological properties of the printing inks through specific plots such as the apparent viscosity as a function of shear stress for the pure GO suspension, LFP/GO and LTO/GO inks (Figure 2a). The pure GO suspension had a GO concentration of 85 mg/mL. LFP and LTO nanoparticles were added to the highly concentrated graphene oxide ink to prepare the cathode and anode ink, respectively. The mass ratio of LFP or LTO to GO was 7:3 and the GO concentration in water was maintained at 80 mg/mL in order to achieve reliable printing. All solid material concentrations are ~285 mg/mL. Based on our experimental results as shown in Figure 2a, GO, LFP/GO, and LTO/GO exhibited overlapped viscosity curves, indicating that the addition of LFP or LTO nanoparticles has less influence to the GO ink system. These three inks exhibit similar shear-thinning behavior, which reveals that they are non-Newtonian fluids and can be printed.[20] Each ink has an apparent viscosity ranging from 102 to 103 Pa s at a shear rate of 1 s-1. This high apparent viscosity will enhance the printability of the electrode inks and enable complex 3D structures and design patterns. We also studied the rheological properties of traditional LFP slurry ink for the comparison with GObased electrode inks. The traditional electrode slurries were prepared by mixing LFP nanoparticles with carbon black and PVdF binder with a ratio of 8/1/1 (w/w/w) in N-Methyl-2pyrrolidone (NMP) solvent (Figure S1, Supporting Information). The solid content of the cathode slurry is ~30 wt %. The rheological behavior of traditional slurries exhibits much lower viscosity and less steep decrease in viscosity with shear rate. Figure 2b shows the stability of the inks in terms of apparent viscosity within a storage time of 4 weeks. The fresh ink is immediately preserved in a sealed glass jar and stored in a refrigerator at 7°C for the allotted time. The apparent viscosity of the ink is tested every week to record viscosity changes. Note that the test ink sample is kept at room temperature for 30 min before the viscosity measurement. Compared to the fresh ink, the apparent viscosity slightly increased during the 4 weeks. All of the inks still exhibit shear thinning behavior and their apparent viscosities remain within the same range (i.e. 102 to 103 Pa s at a shear rate of 1 s-1). This indicates that the ink system has stable viscosity over time and can be stored for extended periods. Figure 2c compares the storage G’ (elastic) and loss G’’ (viscous) moduli of LFP/GO and LTO/GO inks as a function of shear stress. For both inks, the storage modulus plateaus at 104 to 105 Pa, which is one order of magnitude higher than the loss modulus. The storage and loss modulus reflects the elastic and viscous properties of the fabricated inks, respectively. In the plateau region, a higher storage modulus indicates that the inks are relatively stiff and exhibit a solidlike response. After the crossover point between the storage and loss moduli, the loss modulus becomes higher than the storage modulus with an increase in shear stress of >102 Pa. This indicates that viscous deformation begins to dominate the viscoelastic ink properties. For both inks, the yield stress values are as high as 103 Pa. These high yield stress and plateau moduli values are essential for 3D printing stacked architectures. To determine the structural dependence under shear, we perform an oscillatory time sweep with an applied shear of 6 Pa at a frequency of 1Hz for three hours. The G’ and G’’ data as a function of time is plotted in Figure 2d. The G’ and G’’ values are nearly unchanged and relatively independent of time, which

Since the sample is viscous, a small (e.g. 20mm) steel plate geometry was used for testing. Figure 2e is the apparent viscosity of the polymer composite ink as a function of shear rate. The polymer composite ink is a mixture of a polymer (PVDF-co-HFP) and Al2O3 nanoparticles with a mass ratio of 1:10 in NMP solvent. The ink exhibits viscoelastic properties and strong shear thinning behavior. The ink has an apparent viscosity that exceeds 103 Pa.s at a low shear rate of 10-2 s-1. Due to the shear thinning property, the ink has an apparent viscosity close to 10 2 Pa.s at a higher shear rate of 1 s-1. Note that the polymer composite ink’s apparent viscosity is one order of magnitude lower than the electrode inks. A lower viscosity can make the ink a liquidlike material, which enables good wettability as the ink is printed. As a consequence, in Figure 2f, the yield stress point is shifted to a value lower than 10 Pa, which is almost two orders of magnitude lower than the electrode inks. The loss modulus of the polymer ink is higher than its storage modulus, which indicates that the predominant fluid behavior occurs after the crossover point. These properties cause the polymer ink to fill in the channels and make good contacts with the cathode and anode electrodes.

Figure 3 depicts the printing process to create the interdigitated pattern of electrodes and material characterizations. Figure 3a shows the two inks stored in separate syringes. These black and brown inks were vertically mounted to the 3D printer’s stage before printing and were prepared by homogeneously mixing the active battery materials with chemically exfoliated graphene oxide sheets (Figure S2a, Supporting Information). Graphene oxide sheets with diameters of several micrometers were produced using the modified Hummers method[31]. The thickness distribution of the graphene oxide sheets were measured with atomic force microscope (AFM). Based on the 37 randomly selected samples, the graphene oxide sheets have an average thickness of 2.5 nm, which indicates the sheets are mainly 2 layers thick (Figure S2b, Supporting Information). In the printing process, ink is ejected from the nozzle at a moderate speed which is controlled by an air-powered fluid dispenser. Figure 3b shows the printing process of a multilayered electrode using a pre-programmed design routine. The printed electrode architectures were achieved using the motion of the syringe and the stage’s 3-axis directions. Due to the ink’s viscoelastic properties, the filaments from the nozzle can be continuously and uniformly printed out and stacked layer-by-layer to build the designed structure. The interdigitated architecture of both cathode and anode electrodes were printed on a glass substrate (Figure 3c, Figure S3, Supporting information). The interdigitated cell occupies a size of 7 mm by 3 mm. Two tails,

which extended from the cathode and anode, were printed and served as the battery’s electrical conductors. The tails were printed with half the thickness of the electrode to conduct the battery’s electrical current. Note that the tail and interdigitated portions were printed together to create one structure. When one printing layer was completed, the printing nozzle would adjust extrusion height to print another layer on top of the existing structure in the reverse direction. After the LFP/GO printing concluded, the LTO/GO ink syringe was installed and a different pattern routine was used to create the anode. To understand the relation between the number of printing layers and stacking thickness, three electrodes with 6, 12, and 18 layers were printed (Figure S4, Supporting Information). Note that each filament layer has a uniform diameter regardless of whether it was printed on the bottom or on the top of the electrodes. Figure 3d compares the dimensions of the printed electrodes with the expected height/thickness as a function of layer number. For comparison purposes, we printed a single layer electrode and measured its thickness to use as the standard. One layer has a thickness of ~0.18 mm and electrodes with 6, 12 and 18 layers have a total thickness of 1.06, 2.20, and 3.26 mm, respectively. Compared to their expected thickness values of 1.12, 2.24, and 3.36 mm, the printed electrode demonstrates a linear relationship between layer number and total thickness, which can be contributed to the good viscoelastic properties of the GO-based inks. The asformed filaments, especially the initially printed filament at the bottom of the structure, demonstrated exceptional shape retention since the structure did not buckle during the continuous layer-by-layer printing process. After the electrodes were created, post-treatment procedures including freeze-drying and thermal annealing were used to remove the electrode’s (water) solvent and thermally reduce the GO materials. groups, the fresh electrodes can be shelved for more than 10 hours without any signs of structural failure. After the water is removed from the printed electrodes by freeze-drying, the dried samples maintain their structural dimensions and are stable in an ambient environment for a few weeks. Thermal treatment (600 oC in Ar/H2 for 2 hours) was applied to thermally reduce graphene oxide of the printed electrodes. Note that there was no delamination of the electrodes from the glass substrate during this treatment. After thermal annealing, the electrical conductivity of LFP/rGO and LTO/rGO electrodes measured by four point probe method are 31.6 and 6.1 S/cm, respectively, which are significantly improved than the that of non-thermal treated samples (10-6-10-7 S/cm) (Table S1, Supporting Information). Raman spectroscopy offered information regarding the degree of GO reduction after the thermal annealing (Figure 3e). The entire spectrum was normalized to its maximum value. Two peaks in the range of 1200-1450 cm-1 and 1460-1650 cm-1 are attributed to the D band and G band, respectively. Before the thermal treatment, LFP/GO and LTO/GO showed broad D and G bands, which is a typical GO Raman spectrum. After the thermal treatment, the peaks became sharper and the full width at half-maximum for both D and G bands decreased, which suggests a higher degree of graphitization for the reduced graphene oxide (rGO). Figure 3f consists of the X-ray diffraction (XRD) data for the annealed LFP/rGO and LTO/rGO. A perfect match with the standard orthorhombic LFP (JCPDS #40-1499) and cubic spinel LTO (JCPDS #49-0207) is apparent. This result is consistent with previous studies where the thermal treatment had no influence on the crystal structure of the LFP and LTO within the GO composites[32–34].

The filaments are closely stacked together to create good contact between adjacent filaments. This packing ensures good electrical conductivity and good mechanical integrity of the electrodes. The inner electrode structure is porous and consists of aligned rGO sheets (Figure 4d). This alignment is caused by the external shear stress along the filament during the 3D printed extrusion process[21]. The alignment could enhance the electrical conductivity of the electrodes since the sheets are interconnected and create continuous structural pathways.[37] The SEM image in Figure 4e shows that the LFP nanoparticles are heavily coated on the rGO sheets. Figure 5a shows the charge and discharge curves of the LFP/rGO half-cell tested with a 10 mA/g specific current. The initial charge and discharge capacity is 168 and 164 mAh/g respectively, which nearly reaches the theoretical capacity of LFP (170 mAh/g). The voltage hysteresis between the two plateaus of the charge and discharge curves is only 0.1 V, which demonstrates the LFP/rGO electrodes’ good electrical conductivity. In the 10th and 20th cycles, the charge and discharge capacities remain close to the 170 mAh/g theoretical value. The charge and discharge plateaus are stable and there is no sign of any increase in voltage hysteresis. LFP/rGO electrodes tested at various specific currents are shown in Figure 5b. At a specific current of 50 mA/g, the charge and discharge capacities are 152 and 151 mAh/g, respectively. The voltage hysteresis slightly increased to ~0.3 V when a specific current of 200 mA/g was applied. The cell showed a higher charge capacity than the discharge capacity at a specific current of 200 mA/. This may be due to the possibility that the RGO matrix lengthened Li+ diffusing path and provided a Li+ resort so that diffusion was hindered and the capacity was reduced, as reflected by the increased capacity loss at elevated specific currents, and similar observation has been reported previously[39–41]. With increase in specific current values, the charge and discharge capacities tend to decrease but remain above 100 mAh/g. The charge-discharge curves for the LTO/rGO electrode at higher specific current are shown in Figure 5d. The discharge capacities are 163, 127, and 73 mAh/g at a specific current of 50, 100, and 200 mA/g, respectively. Based on the rate performance of LFP and LTO electrodes, we notice that LTObased electrodes suffered more capacity decay than LFP-based electrodes at elevated specific currents. This probably comes from two reasons: the first is that LTO-based electrode has oneorder lower electrical conductivity than LFP-based electrode that the values are 6.1 and 31.6 S/cm for anode and cathode, respectively. The low electrical conductivity of electrodes causes relatively slow kinetics for LTO-based electrode. The second reason is that LTO nanoparticles are ~200 nm in diameter but LFP has less than 50 nm in diameter. The smaller nanoparticle created shortened Li+ diffusion distance, leading to fast insertion kinetics at elevated specific currents. Since the temperature for the thermal reduction method is limited by the thermal stability of the LFP and LTO materials, the addition of conductive materials such as carbon black and carbon nanotubes could be an effective method to enhance the electrical conductivity of the electrode. In our future work, we will incorporate conductive additives into the GO-based ink and/or use carbon coated active materials to investigate its printability and electrochemical performance. In conclusion, this work is the first to create GO-based electrode inks with high viscosity and optimum viscoelastic properties that can be used to fabricate lithium-ion battery prototypes using extrusion-based 3D printing. Rheological studies indicate that the GO-based electrode inks maintain high apparent viscosities while exhibiting excellent shear-thinning behavior, notable ink storage over relatively long time periods and good loss moduli stability. This enables the inks to produce continuous filaments that can be constructed into complex 3D architectures. Additionally, a polymer composite ink with controllable viscosity is developed and serves as the electrically insulated separator as well as the polymer electrolyte after being soaked by typical liquid electrolyte. The 3D printed LFP cathode and LTO anode exhibit stable cycling performance with specific capacities of ~160 mAh/g and ~170 mA/g, respectively. An entirely 3D printed full cell featured a high electrode mass loading of around 18 mg/cm2 when normalized to the overall area of the battery. The full cell delivers initial charge and discharge capacities of 117 and 91 mAh/g with good cycling stability. Based on this work, it is conceivable that a class of 3D printable GO-based composite inks can be designed and developed to fabricate advanced structures, architectures, and systems with a diverse range of functionalities and applications.
