ABSTRACT: In drinking water distribution systems (DWDS), biofilms are the predominant mode of microbial growth with the presence of extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) protecting the biomass from environmental and shear stresses. Biofilm formation poses a significant problem to the drinking water industry as a potential source of bacterial contamination, including pathogens and in many cases also affecting the taste and odor of drinking water and promotes corrosion of pipes. This article critically reviews important research findings on biofilm growth in DWDS, examining the factors affecting their formation and characteristics, as well as the various technologies to characterize, monitor and ultimately, to control their growth. Research indicates that temperature fluctuations potentially affect not only the initial bacteria-to-surface attachment but also the growth rates of biofilms. For the latter, the effect is unique for each type of biofilm-forming bacteria – ammonia oxidizing bacteria for example, grow more developed biofilms at typical summer temperature of 22C compared to 12C in fall , while the opposite occurs for the pathogenic V. cholera. Recent investigations have found formation of thinner yet denser biofilms under high and turbulent flow regimes of drinking water, in comparison to the more porous and loosely attached biofilms at low flow rates. Further, in addition to the rather well-known tendency of significant biofilm growth on corrosion-prone metal pipes, research efforts also found leaching of growth-promoting organic compounds from the increasingly popular use of polymer-based pipes. Knowledge of the unique microbial members of drinking water biofilms and importantly, the influence of water characteristics and operational conditions on their growth, can be applied to optimize various operational parameters to minimize biofilm accumulation. More detailed characterizations of the biofilm population size and structure are now feasible with fluorescence microscopy (epifluorescence and CLSM imaging with DNA, RNA, EPS, protein and lipid stains) and electron microscopy imaging (ESEM). Importantly, thorough identification of microbial fingerprints in drinking water biofilms is achievable with

Page 3

DNA sequencing techniques (the 16S rRNA gene-based identification), which have revealed prevalence of previously undetected bacterial members. Technologies are now moving toward in situ monitoring of biomass growth in distribution networks, including the development of optical fibres capable of differentiating biomass from chemical deposits. Taken together, management of biofilm growth in water distribution systems requires an integrated approach, starting from treatment of water prior to entering the networks, to potential implementation of ‘biofilm-limiting’ operational conditions and finally, to the careful selection of available technologies for biofilm monitoring and control. For the latter, conventional practices, including chlorine – chloramine disinfection, flushing of DWDS as well as nutrient removal, and emerging technologies are discussed with their associated challenges.

INTRODUCTION Safe drinking water is a basic need and its provision has been a top priority issue world- wide. The main challenge to the drinking water industry is to deliver a product that is microbiologically and chemically safe, as well as aesthetically pleasing. While disinfection practices remove the majority of microorganisms found in raw water, the treated water is not sterile and low levels of microorganisms persist in the water when entering the distribution networks. Studies on drinking water distribution systems (DWDS) have indicated that more than 90% of the total biomass resides in matrix-enclosed microbial colonies on pipe walls called biofilms, with only up to 5% of the biomass suspended in the bulk water.1 Biofilms are ubiquitous and persistent microbial communities growing on surfaces, capable of continuous shedding of cells that promotes the spread of microorganisms. Biofilms in DWDS range from a few tens of micrometers to a few mm.2, 3 The biofilms consist of complex and functionally organized microbial communities composed of cells embedded in a gelatinous matrix of biological origin comprised of extracellular polymeric substances (EPS).4 The EPS matrix is responsible for the integrity of the three dimensional structure of biofilms, gluing cells together and onto surfaces. The EPS also provides protection for the microbial community from adverse environmental conditions. Recent studies revealed that microorganisms can also dwell in loose deposits, such as particulate matter that accumulates at the bottom of the pipes or on suspended solids that are transported through the network.5-7 DWDS harbor biofilms even in the presence of disinfectants,8 potentially affecting the turbidity, taste, odor and color of the water,9 and in many cases, promoting the decay of residual disinfectants.10 Growth of biofilms therefore necessitates increased levels of disinfectant agents to improve the disinfection outcome, which can negatively impact the chemical and aesthetic quality of drinking water. Biofilm growth in distribution systems could also increase flow resistance,3 affecting the network’s hydraulic efficiency in the long

run. Moreover, biofilms in many cases secrete acid metabolites that corrode concrete and metallic pipes.11-13 Posing a major health threat, biofilms have been known to harbor pathogenic microorganisms,14-16 potentially releasing them into the water flow through the natural shedding cycle of biofilm.17 The consumption of contaminated water has been known to cause a wide range of diseases and health problems, particularly affecting infants, young children, the elderly and the immune-compromised population.18 Examples of pathogens found in DWDS include Vibrio cholerae (causes cholera), Salmonella typhimurium (typhoid fever), Escherichia coli, Giardia lamblia and Cryptosporidium parvum (gastroenteritis), Naegleria fowleri (amoebic meningoencephalitis), Mycobacterium avium (pulmonary infections) and hepatitis viruses.18-20 In the USA alone, waterborne infections are responsible for over 40,000 hospitalizations, costing the economy $970 million per year.21 A core yet still debated issue is the development and impact of biofilms in DWDS, that is, how the characteristics of water and operating conditions of DWDS determine the traits of the growth and subsequently, their roles on corrosion, degradation of disinfectants as well as in facilitating proliferation of pathogens.16 The knowledge is key for appropriate monitoring and control strategy of biofilm growth in the distribution system. In this Review, we describe the occurrence and characteristics of biofilms in DWDS and how the growth is potentially affected by the various operational conditions, including their fluctuations. The choice of pipe materials, flow rate variations, and quite often, changes in temperature and pH, affect biofilm formation, including the initial stage of microorganism attachment onto pipe surfaces. The Review further describes a range of technologies that have been available or that are currently being developed for potential use in the monitoring and characterization of drinking water biofilms. Finally, practices to control biofilm development are discussed, including the emerging catalysis and biometabolic based technologies. The Review is expected to provide

Biofilms in water distribution systems are mainly comprised of water from the gelatinous matrix, which can occupy up to 99% of the total volume, while microorganisms in fact represent only 2–5% of the volume.44-46 EPS accounts for 50–90% of the total organic carbon in biofilms47, 48 and is generally comprised of polysaccharides and proteins as the major components (75-89%),49 with varying amounts of nucleic acids, lipids, phospholipids and humic substances.50 Inorganic particles such as corrosion products, suspended solids and sand, may also be incorporated in biofilms, increasing its mechanical strength and biomass accumulation.51 According to Characklis and Marshall,52 bacteria are generally the dominant members of biofilm microbial communities in DWDS due to their high growth rates, relatively small size, adaptation capabilities and ability to produce EPS. Viruses, filamentous fungi, algae and protozoa may also be present in drinking water biofilms.15, 53, 54 These ‘secondary’ microorganisms, in particular, viruses and protozoa could rapidly attach and persist in existing drinking water biofilms,17 while the involvement of filamentous fungi in biofilms has not yet been satisfactorily established.55 Protozoan opportunistic pathogens including Acanthamoeba (causing keratitis mostly from contact lenses stored or washed in tap water) and Naegleria (encephalitis via nasal washes) are regularly found in DWDS, probably mainly in reservoirs. Cryptosporidium (protozoa responsible for gastrointestinal diseases) was reported to be present in some DWDS, potentially introduced via faecal contamination.16 In DWDS, true monospecies biofilms are rare. Organic and inorganic matter as nutrients for biofilm growth in water distribution systems Organic matter content in water distribution systems is fuel for biofilm formation, with most aquatic microorganisms metabolizing biodegradable organic matter (BOM) for energy sources (dissimilation) and for the production of cellular materials (assimilation). Studies have correlated the structural and physicochemical characteristics of organic matters to their biodegradability or in other words, their bioavailability. Unsaturated aliphatic compounds, such as simple carbohydrates, low molecular weight proteins and organic acids are in general more accessible for microbial degradation compared to the more hydrophobic aromatic compounds, including aromatic carboxylic acids, phenolic compounds and humic substances.88-91 Sun et al.90 reported a positive correlation between the percentage of aliphatic carbon, indicated by H-to-C ratio of organic matters with their bioavailability. Significantly influencing the species of microbial growth, the levels of BOM are unique to each distribution system depending on the water source and the capability to identify which carbon species are present is still not so well-developed in the water industry.16 Regardless, oxidative drinking water treatments, such as ozonation92, 93, UV radiation94-96 and a combination of UV/H2O2 treatment97, have been suggested to increase the bioavailability of organic matters in distribution systems due to alteration of their chemical structures. Reduction of BOM contents, typically by biological filters, is therefore necessary prior to the water entering the distribution system to control the subsequent biofilm growth and development. Biofilm growth is also significantly influenced by the presence of inorganic nutrients, such as phosphorus, even in distribution systems with high organic matter contents.98-100 Growth of bacteria in drinking water needs phosphorus,99, 101 as it is indispensable for cellular metabolism, e.g. for the formation of high energy compounds such as ATP, as a building block in DNA, RNA and phospholipid biosynthesis, as well as in post-translational control of protein activity although it is absent in nascent proteins.102 Despite its growth enhancing

Batté et al.67 reported a significant increase in γ-Proteobacteria within biofilms, which potentially includes common pathogens. Influence of water temperature fluctuation in distribution systems on biofilm development DWDS, while they are commonly buried underground, are often subjected to temperature fluctuations, in particular in multi-seasonal countries. For example, the temperature of distribution systems in North America typically vary from around 22C in summer, to 12C in fall and 6C in winter,128 while temperatures ranging from 6C to 35C129 are not uncommon in Australia. Such temperature fluctuations could significantly affect the initial cell-to-surface attachment and subsequent formation of drinking water biofilms through a number of innate mechanisms. Temperature affects expression of many genes that could result in changes in the microbial ability to generate EPS as well as modification of the cell surface hydrophobicity. Such temperature-dependent modulations have been observed in bacteria, such as Listeria monocytogenes,130-132 P. aeruginosa133 as well as other bacteria,134 that have been found to form biofilms. L. monocytogenes for example, is reported to produce EPS at 21C and therefore adhering on surfaces and forming biofilm, but not at 10C or 35C,130 as also observed by other studies.131 Relevant biofilm-forming bacteria, including Acinetobacter, Agrobacterium radiobacter, Alcaligenes, Arthrobacter sp., Corynebacterium sp., E. coli, P. aeruginosa, P. fluorescens and P. putida,135 have been known to become more hydrophobic during the exponential growth phase,135 and temperature variations are reported to modulate cell surface hydrophobicity in a growth phase-dependent manner. With decreasing temperature from 37C to 8C, Chavant et al.136 observed a more prominent decrease in cell hydrophobicity with stationary phase L. monocytogenes compared to exponentially growing cells.136 It has been suggested that bacteria modify their cellular membrane lipid composition as a function of temperature, leading to changes in hydrophobicity,137 and subsequently their affinity for attachment to a particular substratum. A clear example would be where the hydrophobic nature of L. monocytogenes cell surface at 37C corresponds to a higher degree of initial

attachment onto hydrophilic surfaces (stainless steel) compared to hydrophobic surfaces (polytetrafluoroethylene, PTFE).136 In contrast, at 8C, the cell surface becomes hydrophilic and cell attachment was observed not only on the hydrophilic surfaces, but comparably also on the hydrophobic surfaces.136 Despite potential differences in the initial bacteria-to-surface attachment, it has been frequently observed that over prolonged periods (days or months), there is generally no observable difference in the extent of biofilm accumulation on hydrophilic surfaces compared to those on hydrophobic surfaces.138 Microorganisms tend to form biofilms at a lesser extent at lower temperatures.128, 136 This is primarily due to the prolonged lag time, the length of time before cells start to proliferate, and the reduced growth rate at sub-optimal temperatures.139, 140 For example, L. monocytogenes forms three-dimensional biofilms at 37C and 20C on both hydrophilic (stainless steel) and hydrophobic (PTFE) surfaces, with only a monolayer of cells observed at 8C.136 In other drinking water relevant cases, temperature fluctuation does not appear to affect the presence of ammonia oxidizing bacteria (AOB) in chloraminated systems – the bacteria are capable to deplete monochloramine and generate nitrate.128 However, less developed AOB biofilms are formed at 12C compared to those formed at 22C.128 Interestingly, some microorganisms form more developed biofilms at lower temperatures. Taken together, the findings demonstrate the clear influence of temperature on the affinity of relevant biofilm-forming bacteria to unique types of surfaces as well as on the growth of biofilms. An understanding of the temperature-dependent susceptibility of water distribution systems to biofilm formation will allow for prompt implementation of appropriate biofilm monitoring and control strategies. It is noteworthy to mention however, that in general an increase in temperature leads to higher rates of disinfectant degradation, 143 which in turn, increases disinfectant demand. Applications of higher doses of disinfectant are therefore necessary, in particular during warmer temperatures, to maintain the microbiological quality of the water. 432 433 Effect of pipe materials on biofilm formation A range of pipe materials have been used for the distribution of drinking water. The majority of the pipeline networks have been of iron (stainless steel and galvanized steel), copper or cement based materials, while polymer based materials such as, polyvinyl chloride (PVC) and polyethylene (PE) are becoming increasingly popular as they are easier to handle and install. The choice of pipe materials could affect development of biofilms in distribution systems. Polymeric pipes could be a source of biodegradable volatile organic compounds (VOCs) in drinking water,144 due to leaching of polymer additives, polymer degradation as well as by-products of polymer oxidation. It has been shown that microorganisms could proliferate by metabolizing small molecular weight plasticizer, residual monomers as well as anti-oxidants, potentially promoting biofilm growth on pipe surfaces.145, 146 Many studies however, have observed less growth and microbial diversity on polymeric pipes compared to those formed on corrosion-prone materials, including iron based pipes (Figure 1a).147-150 In contrast to the ‘smooth’ surfaces of polymeric pipes, it is thought that the pitted surfaces of corroded iron pipes (old iron pipes can become severely encrusted with scale and rust

exceeding 10 centimetres in depth) protect biofilms from physical perturbation and/or chemical disinfection, as well as promoting microbial attachment and colonization due to greater surface area.16, 138, 148, 151 Further, dissolved and solid iron corrosion products in DWDS could support the growth of specific biofilm-forming bacteria. Iron-oxidizing bacteria, such as Gallionella spp. oxidize ferrous iron to ferric iron,152, 153 while iron-reducing bacteria, such as P. aeruginosa, P. fluorescens and some members of Bacillus spp.13, 154 reduce soluble155 or solid iron (III) species156, 157 to iron (II) species.158 Corroded pipe material could retain nutrients, including carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus,159, 160 for subsequent utilization by biofilm bacteria. Corrosion products could also react with disinfectants, depleting residuals particularly near pipe surfaces.161 Indeed, an increase in microbial concentration and diversity has been observed on biofilms formed on severely corroded pipes.162 It is therefore clear that the right choice of pipe material would mean better management of biofilm development in DWDS. Corrosion-prone materials, such as iron should be avoided due to the growth-promoting effects of the corrosion products, including depleting disinfectant residuals. Phylogenetic analyses of microbial communities in biofilms Although viable, many of the microbial members of sampled biofilms are often uncultivable as they do not grow on commonly used cell culture media.226 Culture-based techniques therefore most of the time underestimate the diversity and relative abundances of microorganisms in biofilms.71 Cultivation-independent molecular techniques applicable to drinking water biofilms have been developed, and as herein discussed, these can be classified into two approaches, those that are based on DNA fingerprinting methods, such as denaturing gradient gel electrophoresis (DGGE), single-strand conformation polymorphism (SSCP) or terminal restriction fragment length polymorphism (T-RFLP) and the 16S rRNA gene sequencing method. DGGE examines microbial diversity in mixed-culture biofilms, as well as population shift in response to altered environmental conditions or stress.65, 227-230 Relevant to the DWDS, the method has been used to investigate dominant bacterial members in microbial communities on different plumbing materials, to compare the effect of material choice on biofilm formation,216, 231, 232 and to determine any population shift during water treatment steps and subsequent distribution.217, 228 DGGE separates polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplified gene fragments of the same length but with different base pair sequences based on the decreased electrophoretic mobility of partially melted double-stranded DNA molecules in polyacrylamide gels containing a linear gradient of DNA denaturants.233 The number of bands observed in DGGE profiles provides an estimate of species richness in biofilms while the relative intensity of each band is thought to reflect the relative abundance of each species. DNA bands from DGGE gels can be further processed for sequencing to identify the corresponding microbial species. Gradually being abandoned, the method is limited by the risk of bias introduced during PCR amplification,234, 235 co-migration of DNA from different

In PCR-SSCP analysis, target sequences in genomic DNA are simultaneously amplified, then denatured to a single- stranded form and subjected to non-denaturing gel electrophoresis. SSCP separates PCR amplicons of the same fragment length with different nucleotide sequences on the basis of the conformation of single-stranded DNA. SUMMARY AND PERSPECTIVES

Growth of biofilms in drinking water distribution networks although unavoidable, is potentially controllable. An in-depth understanding of biofilm characteristics and how the conditions of the distribution systems affect their development, will allow for potential tuning of the operational parameters to limit the growth. The choice of pipe materials, flow rates, temperature and pH of distribution system affect all stages of biofilm development, from the initial attachment of microorganisms onto pipe surfaces, to the extent and characteristics of biofilm growth. Whenever applicable, the ‘biofilm-limiting’ operating conditions of distribution systems, together with the already implemented biofilm control practice, such as removal of organic and inorganic nutrients and treatment with disinfectants, feature a potential for improved management of biofilm growth throughout the network. Equally important is the implementation of suitable biofilm monitoring practice to probe the likely

Page 46

changes in biofilm characteristics as a result of fluctuations in operating conditions and disinfection treatments. The quite recent development of in-situ biomass sensors and the increasingly cost effective biomolecular analysis of microbial communities will enable more frequent and thorough assessments of biofilm characteristics and their distribution profile across the drinking water networks. This will allow for timely administration of control measures, particularly in response to unforeseen changes in operating conditions that could promote biofilm growth in distribution systems, including an abrupt temperature increase or a pH drop due to growth of nitrifiers. Management of the persistent biofilm growth requires an integrated approach of water pretreatment, biofilm monitoring and control, as no single practice thus far appears to be sufficiently effective. In closing, a systematic survey of DWDS microbial ecosystems and their correlation to water characteristics and the systems’ operational conditions, is key for effective monitoring and treatment strategy and importantly, for anticipation of potential shifts in the microbial profile in response to treatment change. The survey is indispensable, in particular with the now known prevalence of antibiotic resistant bacteria (ARB) and antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) in drinking water, with no regulation currently in place for such presence of resistance entities.
