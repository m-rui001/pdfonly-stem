Abstract Wearable industry is on the rise with a myriad of technical applications ranging from real-time health monitoring, internet of things, robotics, to name a few. However, there is a saying “wearable is not wearable” because the current market-available wearable sensors are largely bulky and rigid, leading to uncomfortable wearing experience, motion artefact and poor data accuracy. This arouses world-wide intensive research quest for novel materials, with an aim to fabricate next-generation ultra-light-weight and soft wearable devices. Such disruptive second skin-like biosensing technologies may enable the paradigm shift from current wearable 1.0 to future wearable 2.0 products. This report aims to give a comprehensive coverage of the stateof-the-art progress made in the key phases for future wearable technology, namely, wear®sense®communicate®analyze®interpret®decide.

1. Introduction

The potential for wearable technologies (WT) is vast, as they will potentially be able to provide real-time sensing information about the wearer, and the environment. Opportunity areas exist in healthcare, sports, robotics, defense, and maintenance, to name but a few.[1] The wearable technology industry is now on the rise. In particular, the market is boosted by growing interest and awareness about the benefits of supporting healthcare, transitioning from current hospital-centered diagnostics to patient-centered diagnostics.[2] The quantity of WT devices in global market has reached 125 million units in 2016, and the users are estimated to approach 900 million by 2021 with an average compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of 23%.[3] According to IDTechEx, the market share is expected to grow to more than $75 billion in 2025.[4] However, the current generation of WT products is not yet capturing the huge market opportunities available because they fail to drive long-term sustained engagement and behaviour change. Consumers need wearable devices that give users accurate information in real time and then synthesize that data to provide insights that lead to better decision making. Globally, current-generation WT products are represented by Fitbit, Apple Watches, Xiaomi Band and Google Glasses. These gadget technologies are crude in their ability to sense the environment and do not provide useful data for the wearer as they are rigid and not able to conform to human surfaces. In general, the current wearable products have the following issues: • Awkward design issues with wearing discomfort. The current-generation ‘wearables’ are mainly limited to gadgets, such as wrist bands/watches/glasses/earrings. These rigid gadgets

Current wearable sensors are dominated by accelerometers, which can only provide limited information on the movement. More useful information such as skin/muscle stretching/deformation/biological markers cannot be provided with any existing wearable products. •

Ideally, wearable software should be hardware-agnostic. Current software systems for wearables are diverse and fragmented. Greater collaboration across designers and information technology urgently need to develop standard platform software and improve algorithms as a result. Future wearable devices will be soft and go beyond the conventional rigid wafer and planar circuit board technologies. Such soft wearable devices will be lightweight and thin, soft and elastic, inexpensive and durable. These devices will be skin-attachable, flexible, stretchable, bendable and twistable whilst maintaining excellent sensing performances. Such disruptive WT 4

2. Wearable materials

Current circuit board technologies are sophisticated and well-developed and have evolved into today’s highly miniaturized products. Nevertheless, the cornerstone materials (such as silicon) are mechanically rigid and based on planar design. This causes their inherent limitation in current-generation wearable electronics which cannot conformally integrate with soft and curvilinear human body. The ideal wearable sensors need to maintain outstanding performances when being significantly deformed such as being bent, stretched or twisted. They need to be sufficiently thin so that wearers don’t feel or see the presence of sensors, just like 6

Page 7

tattoos. In addition, they need to be biocompatible and be able to function under wet body fluid conditions. Evidently, such disruptive soft wearable electronics won’t born until breakthrough materials innovation is achieved. The past decade has witnessed encouraging progress in the quest of novel materials and/or materials design. Silicon is not stretchable but becomes stretchable when structures such as wave/wrinkle, origami/kirigami and serpentine are involved. The serpentine bridge-island structure is one of the most widely used design, in which the rigid electronic components are interconnected with serpentine lines.[20] Under external strain, the serpentine lines are able to rotate both in plane and out of plane, to release strain energy in every single serpentine unit, ensuring the functionality for the whole device.[11b] The serpentine patterns are usually fabricated by sophisticated photolithography.[21] Deposited on ultrathin substrate, serpentine structures are viable platform to fabricate epidermal electronics. Such a design can lead to thin skin-conformal wearable sensors as thin as 5 µm.[22] An additional approach is to use novel nanomaterials deposited onto or inside elastomers to construct percolation networks. The nanomaterials may be zero-dimensional (0D), onedimensional (1D) and two-dimensional (2D) (Figure 3a-f). They are ususally metallic (Figure 3a-d) or carbon-based (Figure 3e-f) materials. While they all may lead to conductive pathways but following different scaling laws with different exponents.[23] In particular, 1D nanomaterials have been heavily used because of their low percolation thresholds, which is desirable for saving the amount of active materials required.[16, 24] Nevertheless, standing gold nanowires turn out to be a unique type of wearable materials with unconventional Janus materials properties including optical, mechanical, wetting and conductivity.[25] 7

Page 8

Liquid conductors may be also used as novel wearable materials such as liquid metals and ionic liquids. In the absence of breakup, the liquids may be stretched infinitely.[26] Liquid metals are ideal combination of conductivity and fluidity. The liquid metal based conductive fibers fabricated through injection strategy demonstrates a high stretchability up to 700%, while still keep the metallic electrical continuity.[27] The unique fluid features including fluidity and surface tension also endow liquid conductors additional exciting functionalities, such as printability, patterning ability and self-healing.[28] When exposed to air, the liquid metals automatically form a layer of oxides, which helps to maintain configuration at the damaged interface.[29] The oxide layers are able to merge again (Figure 3g) upon contact, resulting in reconstruction of conductive paths.[30] In addition, liquid conductors may be also ionic species such as ionic liquids. Unlike liquid metals, ionic liquids can be easily spread onto many elastomers forming uniform coating[31] or trapped between textile fibers forming liquid bridges (Figure 3h).[19] This simple strategy could lead to versatile strain sensors in various formats. An additional type of wearable material is conducting polymer, which has been widely used in wearable electronics.[32] Conventional conducting polymers are not soft and can hardly be stretched while keeping conductivity because of conjugated aromatic ring structures. In order to achieve high conductivity, chains of conducting polymers should be arranged in high crystallinity, which renders them mechanically rigid.[33] Taking poly(3,4-ethylene dioxythiophene):poly(styrene sulfonate) (PEDOT:PSS) for an example, a typical high conductivity of 1000 S cm−1 could be achieved but the electrical failure might occur at only 5% strain.[34] This challenge may be addressed by using ionic additive named assisted stretchability and electrical conductivity (STEC) enhancer.[34b] The STEC enhancer serves as “soft domains”, 8

Page 9

consequently reducing the overall Young’s modulus. The presence of STEC enhancer also aggregates conductive domains (PEDOT) to generate partially crystalline network inside the PSS matrix (Figure 3i). The synergistic effect on both conductivity and stretchability enables PEDOT:PSS to demonstrate the highest recorded conductivity of 3390 S/cm under 100% strain. Hydrogel represents another type of wearable materials. Similar to ionic liquids, hydrogel typically conducts via ionic motions in hydrated polymer matrix. The moduli of hydrogel may match very well with human skins/tissues, enabling establishment of conformal contact. Despite of softness, hydrogel can be super tough.[35] By integrating covalent crosslinks between polymer skeleton and ionic crosslink between Ca2+ and carboxyl groups, the hydrogel made from the synergy of two crosslinks demonstrated a super high stretchability over 1700% (Figure 3j).[35] Upon receiving external stretch, the ionic crosslinks are unzipped first to bear strain energy while the presence of covalent crosslinks restricts polymer chain movement, resulting in reversible stretchability. It has been demonstrated that hydrogels may be used for fabricating epidermal antenna, which is made of silver nanowires/alginate hydrogel.[36] The sensing mechanism is based on resonance frequency shift upon mechanical stretching. Inspired by the adhesion behaviors of mussels, conductive graphene oxide hydrogels may be designed to achieve high adhesion with skins simultaneously with self-healing properties. This enables their uses as EMG electrodes to regulate cell activity and implantable electrodes for recording in vivo signals.[37]

3. Wearable sensors

Page 10

With the appropriate choices of wearable materials and/or design, wearable sensors may be fabricated by using various signal transduction methodologies including resistive[14a, 38], capacitance,[39] piezoelectric[40] or triboelectric.[9c, 41] While these transduction approaches report signals using electrons, transduction may also be achieved with ions to form so-called ionic skins.[42] The electrical double layer (EDL) at a metallic condutor and an ionic conductor behave like a capacitor, which may be coupled with dielectric capacitor in series to detect pressure and strains due to deformed EDL structures.[42] The EDL-based capacitor sensors may offer a sensitivity of three orders of magnitude higher than traditional dielectric capacitance sensors.[43] with at least three orders of magnitude larger than that of the EDL-free counterparts. When ionic liquids are used as electrolytes for EDL, they can offer wide electrochemical sensing windows in field effect transitor [44] beneficial for wearable electronics.[45] While this has been discussed in a few recent reviewers,[46] this article will mainly focus on discussion of biometric data instead. Based on the signal types, all the wearable sensors may be categorized into three kinds: electrophysiological, physical and chemical sensors (Figure 4). Electrophysiological sensors detect the electrical potential difference between electrodes at specialized tissues like heart (Electrocardiograph; ECG), brain (electroencephalography; EEG) and muscle (electromyography; EMG). Physical sensors measure the electrical property changes subjected to mechanical, temperature or optical stimuli. Chemical sensors identify or quantify analytes (including ions, molecules, proteins, etc.) through active electrodes that can be tailored to respond to specific chemical species. Electrophysiological sensors detect the biopotential changes of targeted organs during cardiovascular, neurological and muscular activities.[47] The core issue for all these sensors is 10

Page 11

to design thin, conformal and biocompatible epidermal electrodes to decrease skin-electrode contact impedance. It is crucial to consider the interface between such soft electrodes and skin in order to extract reliable electrophysiological signals (Figure 5a-c). Intimate contact is the key to reduce skin-electrode contact impedance. Conductive hydrogel electrodes (Figure 5a) can establish reliable conformal contact with topologically rough skin surface but suffer from the following limitations: unavoidable drying prevents gel electrodes from long-term use; ionic species in the gel may cause skin irritation.[48] Alternatively, a thin dry electrode may be used (Figure 5b). Nevertheless, it is nontrivial to establish fully conformal contact because of rough skin surfaces. The presence of air gaps increases interfacial impedance, hence, leading to significant environmental noise. To overcome the limitation of the dry electrodes, microneedles may be used (Figure 5c). The microneedles only penetrate into the layer of stratum corneum which is composed of dead cells, therefore, they are essentially painless and considered to be non-invasive. The height of the microneedles matches the thickness of the stratum corneum layer, enabling their direct contact with the moist epidermis,[49] thereby reducing electrode noise without causing discomfort. Physical sensor detects the physical signals of human body. There are a number of physical signals directly related to human health, such as blood pressure, body temperature and muscle/skin stretching, etc. While it is easy to measure such vitals in hospital, it is challenging to monitor them continuously in a non-invasive manner anytime anywhere. Current rigid gadget-based wearables may be able to address this challenge to some degree, but poor wearability and data accuracy prevent such products from reaching clinical standards. It is highly encouraging to witness the strong research interest in developing skin-like wearable 11

Page 12

pressure, strain and temperature sensors in academia.[50] In particular, a wide range of materials including silver nanowires,[12] gold nanowires,[13a] carbon nanotubes,[50b] graphene,[51] conducting polymers,[52] ionic liquids,[53] and liquid metals[54] have been exploited to design skin-conformal wearable pressure sensors. Key parameters such as sensitivity, linear sensing range, hysteresis, and durability have been thoroughly evaluated.[13a] Additionally, ultrathin skin-like strain sensors have also been designed with various materials and design strategies.[24, 55]

Sensitivity and stretchability are usually the two key factors in designing wearable strain

sensors. With a crack-based design, an extremely high sensitivity of 2000 at 2% was reported;[56] with a hierarchical wrinkle design of carbon nanotube sheet wrapped elastomeric fiber (Figure 5d),[57] a high stretchability up to 1320% was achieved. Conventional temperature sensors like infrared digital cameras and paste-on temperature sensors cannot provide continuous, cost-effective temperature mapping abilities. Incorporation of microscale temperature sensors made by thin serpentine gold film on an ultrathin substrate, skin-like temperature sensor coated conformally on epidermis is achieved. Such device can provide continuous, accurate thermal characterizations to distinguish subtle vibration caused by mental activity.[50c] Human skin sensing system is elegant, which can specifically identify local physical stimuli across the entire body. Millions of pixels will be needed to map such biometric signals, which is obviously impractical. Therefore, it is in high demand to develop pixel-reduction technologies. In this context, antiparallel staircase-like standing gold nanowire films have been used.[58] With the linear assembly of two sensors, 6 location-specific detections could be

Page 13

achieved without dependence on external pressure (Figure 5e); with the concentric assembly, sharpness-specific detection was demonstrated in a wireless manner (Figure 5d). Another type of important biomarkers is chemical species including glucose, lactic acid, pH and cationic ions, which are related to diabetes, cardiovascular diseases and stress, respectively.[59] It is estimated that chemical sensors may account for at least 40% of wearable market share in 2020.[2] Nevertheless, it is challenging to monitor chemical vital signals in a continuous and non-invasive manner with current rigid gadget-based technologies. As the proof of concept, tattoo-like glucose biosensors from the sweat have been reported.[60] In the absence of sweat, iontophoresis may be used to stimulate sweat excretion (Figure 5g).[61] With the help of electrochemical twin channels, clinical trials showed improved correlation with blood glucose level. For the real-world application, it is highly desirable if multiple sensing modalities can be integrated into a single sensor platform. It has been demonstrated that lactate and ECG sensor could be integrated into a flexible patch (Figure 5h).[62] The challenge is the interference between electrochemical redox reactions and bio-impedance. This may be minimized by applying a hydrophobic coating to the sensing electrodes. In addition, simple background subtraction might also differentiate glucose-sensing signal from mechanical strains.[60a]

4. Disruptive wearable integration

In addition to fundamental studies on performances of soft sensors aforementioned, it is important to integrate such sensors with other electric components, such as data collection and wireless signal transmission, which enable continuous health monitoring in real-world setting. 13

Page 14

While traditional integration circuits are well-established, it is nontrivial to integrate soft sensors with rigid circuitry boards in a fully skin-conformal integrated system. The past decade has witnessed encouraging progress in the fully-integrated flexible and soft sensors.[11b, 20, 63] Typically, soft integrated systems can be achieved by utilizing 1) stretchable structures, 2) stretchable materials, or 3) the combination of both. A lot of novel structural designs have been pioneered, such as wavy structures and serpentine layouts, which enable conventional rigid materials bonded on elastomer substrates to be stretchable.[64] Also, the material engineering of nanomaterials provides alternative routes to achieve intrinsic stretchability, which is important for constructing electrical interconnects between active devices.[63c] With the combination of both material and structure approaches, various kinds of stretchable electronics, optoelectronics and integrated functional systems have been developed.[11b] Soft wearable force sensors, including pressure, strain, torsion, etc., are of paramount importance for various kinds of applications such as electronic skin, flexible display, and soft robotics.[13a, 24, 65] They can be integrated with traditional circuitry enabling user-friendly interface with mobile phone. It is even possible for the highly stretchable gold nanowire patches (stretchability of ~800%).[25a] By designing 9 pieces of e-skin patches specific to 9 pieces of skins related facial expression, it showed the potential for monitoring autism disorder.[25a] As schematically illustrated in Figure 6a, the signals acquired could be transmitted to mobile devices via Bluetooth wireless communication. Five basic emotions could be identified, providing real-time readable facial expressions on a mobile screen (Figure 6b).[25a] It is also possible to integrate wearable chemical sensors with wireless circuit towards monitoring various human body fluids, including sweat, blood, and saliva. In a typical wearable 14

Page 15

biomonitoring platform, screen-printed tattoo-like glucose and alcohol biosensors were integrated with a wireless flexible printed circuit board. The integrated system could be attached on human skin conformably (Figure 6c),[66] enabling real-time reading of biological signals and wirelessly transmit them to a mobile phone. While it is possible to integrate multiple types of soft wearable sensors into functioning circuits, it remains challenging to achieve a soft skin-like sensing system with conformal contact with curvilinear human body surfaces under dynamic conditions. With structural design (e.g. serpentine or buckling), low-modulus components could be integrated with high-modulus components in a wearable and stretchable system, which could laminate onto the skin surface conformably for wireless physiological monitoring.[67] The entire system was highly stretchable, which combined the advantages of soft microfluidics, structural adhesive surfaces and controlled mechanical buckling structures, yet capable of detecting various kinds of electrophysiological signals such as ECG, EMG, EEG and electrooculograms (EOG) (Figure 6e). The whole system could function under dynamic deformation of twisting and stretching (Figure 6f).[67] In the state-of-the-art integrated wearable sensing systems, multi-layer device configurations become possible. Recently, a three-dimensional (3D) integrated stretchable sensing system, containing four layers of stretchable electronics, has been reported by transfer printing of different layers of stretchable circuits layer by layer (Figure 6g).[68] Interlayer circuits were connected with vertical interconnects produced by layer ablation and controllable soldering. Also, the data collected by the 3D circuits could wirelessly extract different signals from the human body, promising for future healthcare, wearable remote control and soft 15

robotics.

5. Soft wearable energy devices

Lithium-ion battery technologies dominate current wearable energies, which however are not ideal for future soft wearable products in that they are mostly planar, heavy and rigid. For many soft wearable systems reported to date, the lithium-ion batteries account more than 50% of the total weight, in order to ensure the reasonably long-term data collection as well as BLE and WIFI which also consume majority of energy. In addition, the bulk and non-stretchability of battery certainly limit the wearability of current wearable products. Although several sensing systems have achieved wireless data transmission based on PCB (printed circuit board) with Bluetooth or antenna, most of them are either still powered by rigid battery or unable to acquire sensing information continuously. In this context, the soft wearable energy solution is in urgent need to keep up with the pace with the development of soft wearable sensors.

To date, the research in the field of soft wearable energy can be roughly categorized into energy storage devices (such as battery and supercapacitor), energy conversion devices (such as solar cell and biofuel cell) and energy harvesters (such as piezoelectric, triboelectric and thermoelectric nanogenerators). Besides improved performances, the key designing objectives are to achieve features include softness, ultrathinness, stretchability and skin-conformability.[16, 69]

To realize those, versatile strategies have been developed.[70] For example, the island-bridge

designs were introduced to build stretchable Li-ion battery[11a] and biofuel cells[71] (Figure 7a and b) while supercapacitors could be made into fiber-shaped or ultrathin tattoo-like configurations (Figure 7c).[72] Functional materials such as nanorods embedded in elastomers 16

Page 17

were studied for constructing stretchable solar cells and nanogenerators (Figure 7d and e).[73] There are other designs involving the use of liquid metals or wavy structured conductors in conjunction with stretchable polymers, as well as helical one-dimensional structures.[74] Multiple types of soft wearable energy devices may be integrated along with other electronic components to construct the whole wearable system on the polymer substrates or garments. However, this remains in the embryonic stage of development because multiple materials interfaces and connections between different components often fail under severe mechanical deformation. Researchers have coupled energy harvesters with storage units by developing a self-powered wearable textile woven by fiber-shaped dye-sensitized solar cells, triboelectric nanogenerators and supercapacitors so both the sunshine and body motion energies could be collected and stored.[75] Another recently reported skin-mountable energyintegrated system presented in Figure 7f, wireless charging coil and triboelectric nanogenerator, supercapacitors and lithium-ion battery were built on PDMS substrates to achieve wireless power transmission.[76] This power management system could then power all kinds of sensing components. For example, fiber-shaped lithium-ion battery woven into fabric could provide power for electrochemically sweat analyzing and data transmitting wirelessly.[77] Moreover, MSC (micro-supercapacitor) arrays could also be charged by a wireless radio frequency (RF) power receiver and power all kinds of sensors while the whole device was built on Ecoflex substrates, making it skin-attachable (Figure 7g).[78] As shown in Figure 7h, soft, thin, biocompatible and skin-mountable power management systems could be constructed by coupling solar cells with lithium-ion batteries, which have been exploited in fully wireless sensors for precision skin thermography.[79] 17

Despite encouraging progress in soft skin-like wearable energy field, [15] grand challenges ahead need to be addressed in order to be prepared for the application in future-generation 2.0 wearable products. It is extremely difficult to achieve ultrathin, soft and conformal energy devices with sufficiently high performances to power the whole system including sensors, data processing and transmitting for a reasonably long period time. In addition, it is challenging how to establish reliable connections between energy supply devices and all other components that can ensure normal function under various mechanical deformation. Other aspects of energy technology advances may involve further addressing size reduction and biocompatibility.

6. Wearable communications and data processing

After the integration of disruptive soft sensors and wearable energy device, it is important to have electronics chipset such as microcontroller, signal leveling module, filters and amplifiers integrated to collect, analyze and transfer information from wearable sensors to third party devices (Figure 8). Microcontroller, a crucial component in every wearable device which incorporates a processor, memory, peripheral and interfaces in a micro-sized chip[80] to perform signal processing, data storage, and communication with other integrated circuits (IC).[81] However, raw signals from wearable sensors often have several undesired problems such as signal bias, high noise level or signal level exceeded microcontroller ADC range. In these situations, signal conditioning components such as voltage regulator, wheatstone bridge, filters and amplifiers will be incorporated to transform the raw signal obtained before feeding to the microcontroller for downstream computation. As part of wearable devices, wireless communication protocol is required to seamlessly 18

Page 19

stream important sensor information to the users. There are a plethora of wireless communication protocols including BLE, near field communications (NFC), wireless fidelity (WIFI) and Narrowband Internet of Things (NBIoT) available to be employed for data communication with third party computing device ultimately to back-end server for real-time analysis, clinicians’ review and call for timely intervention when required.[82] The choice of wireless communication protocol is highly dependent on a few crucial factors summarized in Table 1. In general, BLE (Figure 8 b and c) and NFC (Figure 8d) are the most favorable protocols to be used in transmitting data from wearable device due to their low power consumption which allows researchers and developers to achieve smaller and thinner sized wearable devices.[82a, 83] One should understand that the ultimate goal of wireless communication tool is to transmit relevant informative data to the end-users or clinicians. Raw signal from wearable sensors should be preprocessed on board and transmitting only useful information to the next terminal to reduce the density of data packets and lower the energy consumption. For example, ECG waveform consist of 3 main components, which are atria depolarization (P wave), ventricle depolarization (QRS wave) and ventricle repolarization (T wave).[84] In a normal situation, these signals would be fairly consistence. The wearable ECG device should pre-process the signal waveform collected and sent out information about these waveforms to the end-user terminal.[85] The device should only send out full signal waveform during emergency or when heart irregularity was detected.

7. Summary and perspectives

Page 20

Unlike current wearable 1.0 products which start from devices, disruptive soft wearable 2.0 technologies may start from scratch, namely materials innovation. While materials are the focal point of discussion, this progress report covers comprehensively all key components required in next-generation soft wearable technologies. Significant progress has been made in fabricating various soft sensors using various wearable materials including metallic nanowires, carbon materials, conducting polymers, ionic liquids and liquid metals; successful attempts have also been made in integrating them into hybrid electronic systems enabling collection of biometric data wirelessly; various strategies have also been put forward to address pressing energy issues; flexible PCB has been successfully used for wireless signal readout and data processing. All of these complete a full loop as a system required in researching disruptive wearable technologies.

Nevertheless, it has to be noted that current research is limited to address the mechanical mismatch between soft biological materials and rigid electronic materials. Indeed, there remain other fundamental gaps towards wearable bio-integrated sensing system in that biology and electronics are virtually distinct system. They differ in materials composition, functional mechanism, fabrication, and working environment (Table 2). Smart biological soft sensing systems such as eyes, ears, noses and synapse may inspire us to design bionic soft sensing system towards detection of physiological, physical and chemical biometric signals. To realize this, multidisciplinary collaboration is crucial across academia, industries and government. With such a fully soft and biocompatible wearable sensing system, we will then realize our ultimate dream of bio-integrated electronics.
