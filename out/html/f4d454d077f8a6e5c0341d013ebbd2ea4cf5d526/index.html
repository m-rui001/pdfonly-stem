† Present address: Department of Nuclear Science and Engineering, Massachusetts Institute of Technology, Cambridge, MA 02139, USA * Abstract email: jinhyuk@mit.edu, gceder@berkeley.edu There is an urgent need for low cost, resource friendly, high energy density cathode materials for Li-ion batteries to satisfy the rapidly increasing need for electrical energy storage. In this work we show a novel strategy of combining high-valent cations, partial oxygen to fluorine substitution, and a disordered rocksalt structure, to incorporate the Mn2+/Mn4+ double redox couple in Li-excess cathode materials that achieve ultrahigh capacity (> 300 mAh/g) and energy density (~1000 Wh/kg). The use of the Mn2+/Mn4+ redox reduces oxygen redox activity thereby stabilizing the materials, and opens up new opportunities for the design of high performance Mn- rich cathodes for advanced Li-ion batteries.

Li-ion based energy storage is becoming a pervasive technology in today’s society. Introduced in the early 1990’s for use in portable electronics, it has now migrated to applications such as transportation and grid for which energy storage needs will soon dwarf the use in electronics.1,2 Indeed, today with less than 1% penetration of electric vehicles, almost half of all Li-ion batteries produced are already directed towards transportation. These new applications increase the demand for safe high energy storage at low cost, and without relying on constrained natural resources.1,2 In this context, it is remarkable that almost all Li-ion cathode materials rely on only two transition metals, Ni and Co, which are the electroactive elements in the layered- rocksalt cathode materials in the Li(Ni,Co,Mn)O2 chemical space (NMC’s).3 On one end of this compositional spectrum LiCoO2 dominates the electronics sector, whereas Ni-rich materials are of interest for the automotive sector.2 While Mn has been used in a spinel cathode,4 and Fe in the LiFePO4 olivine,5 these compounds suffer from low energy density. Given the limits of energy density that can be achieved with the layered NMC’s and the potential resource constraints on cobalt,2 it is of interest to develop cathode materials with high capacity based on other redox metals. In particular, transition metals (TM) that can exchange two electrons are of interest for their ability to create high capacity, similar to the Ni2+/Ni4+ couple in NMC cathodes. In this regard, the low cost and low toxicity make the Mn2+/Mn4+ couple particularly desirable6 to design high-performance Li-ion batteries that are also inexpensive and eco-friendly. Mn is currently used in cathode materials, but mostly in the inert Mn4+ state as in NMC cathodes or for its Mn3+/Mn4+ couple, as in LiMn2O4 spinel.1,3,4,7 More recently Mn3+ has been used in the disordered rocksalt-type cathodes such as Li1.3Mn0.4Nb0.3O2, in which the low capacity from Mn3+/Mn4+ needs to be overcome by a large amount of oxygen (O) redox,8 which can trigger O loss, resulting in substantial voltage and capacity fade.7,9,10 The high theoretical capacity from the Mn2+/Mn4+ redox couple can potentially resolve such issues by making charge compensation rely less heavily on O redox. However, introducing Mn2+ in the dense layered or disordered materials has been difficult, as the Li-excess (x > 1 in LixTM2-xO2) required to achieve high practical capacity10-12 demands a high average transition metal valence. In this work, we demonstrate for the first time that ultrahigh capacity (> 300 mAh/g) and energy density (~1000 Wh/kg) can be achieved in disordered rocksalt Li-rich intercalation cathodes from Mn2+/Mn4+ double redox combined with a small amount of O redox. A critical 2

Page 3

step is that we are able to lower the Mn valence in the cathode material via a combined strategy of high-valent cation (Nb5+, Ti4+) substitution, and O2– replacement by F–. The latter was recently shown to be facilitated by Li-excess and cation-disorder.13 We target the Mn2+-containing compositions Li2Mn2/3Nb1/3O2F and Li2Mn1/2Ti1/2O2F which have a theoretical Mn2+/Mn4+ redox capacity of 270 mAh/g and 230 mAh/g, respectively. Given the high Mn capacities, only a small amount of O redox is required for these materials to deliver a total capacity over 300 mAh/g, mitigating O-redox related problems. Thus realized, high capacity from Mn2+/Mn4+ double redox opens up new opportunities for the design of high performance Li-ion cathode materials. Results and discussions Structure characterization of Li2Mn2/3Nb1/3O2F To evaluate the Mn2+/Mn4+ redox strategy, we first test a new disordered Li-rich material: Li2Mn2/3Nb1/3O2F (= Li1.333Mn0.444Nb0.222O1.333F0.667), synthesized by a mechanochemical ball milling method.14 The theoretical Mn-redox capacity of this material (270 mAh/g) is more than twice that of a typical Mn-based Li-rich cathode, as illustrated in Fig. 1a. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Table 1) and elemental analysis (Supplementary Table 2) show that the compound forms in a disordered rocksalt phase with a composition close to the target composition. XRD refinement yields a lattice parameter of ~4.262 Å (Supplementary Table 1). Energy-dispersive-spectroscopy mapping (EDS) on a Li2Mn2/3Nb1/3O2F particle, using a transmission electron microscope (TEM), reveals a uniform distribution of Mn, Nb, O and F (Fig. 1c). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) shows that the primary particle size of the as-prepared Li2Mn2/3Nb1/3O2F compound is between 100 to 300 nm (Fig. 1d), which is reduced to less than 100 nm after shaker-milling the Li2Mn2/3Nb1/3O2F powder with carbon black (Fig. 1e) for electrode fabrication. TEM shows that the primary particles are in fact polycrystalline and made of ~15 nm grains (Supplementary Fig.1). Electrochemical performance of Li2Mn2/3Nb1/3O2F

capacity of 238 mAh/g (708 Wh/kg) between 1.5 and 4.6 V, which increases to 277 mAh/g (849 Wh/kg) and 304 mAh/g (945 Wh/kg) with higher charge cutoff voltages of 4.8 V and 5.0 V, respectively (Fig. 2a–c). In a test between 1.5 and 5.0 V at 10 mA/g (Fig. 2d), the discharge capacity further increases to 317 mAh/g, delivering a very high energy content of 995 Wh/kg (3780 Wh/l). This discharge capacity of ~320 mAh/g and specific energy approaching ~1000 Wh/kg are among the highest values achieved by Li-ion intercalation cathodes.10,15 The rate capability of Li2Mn2/3Nb1/3O2F is quite good. Fig. 2e compares the first cycle profiles of Li2Mn2/3Nb1/3O2F under different rates between 1.5 and 5.0 V. The material delivers as high as 226 mAh/g (695 Wh/kg) at 200 mA/g and up to 140 mA/g (410 Wh/kg) at a very high rate of 1000 mA/g (Supplementary Fig. 2). The lack of significant hysteresis in the voltage profiles as compared to other Mn-containing Li-excess compounds suggests that structural changes and oxygen loss are small.8,16,17 Only upon very high-voltage charging to above 4.7 V is an apparent voltage plateau observed which is barely seen in discharge (Fig. 2f). As Li2Mn2/3Nb1/3O2F delivers a higher capacity than its theoretical Mn-capacity (270 mAh/g), we expect that the charge plateau at ~4.8 V accompanies oxygen oxidation. The asymmetry in charge/discharge voltage is similar to previous observations in which the O-oxidation plateau is not recovered in the discharge.8,16,17 Nevertheless, this plateau in Li2Mn2/3Nb1/3O2F appears only after charging above ~250 mAh/g, leading to less voltage hysteresis as compared to the other Mn-redox-active disordered compounds in which the O-oxidation plateau occurs typically much earlier in the charge.8,16,17 The smaller amount of O- oxidation and negligible changes in the voltage profile of Li2Mn2/3Nb1/3O2F are further supported by differential electrochemical mass spectroscopy (DEMS) results, which show negligible O2 (g) evolution up to a charge of 5 V (Supplementary Fig. 3). In addition, voltage fade is small in this material compared with other Mn-based Li-rich materials (Supplementary Fig. 4). These results indicate that our strategy of going to Mn2+ compounds to increase the Mn-redox capacity at the expense of O-redox is successful in increasing capacity and reversibility.

Upon charging the a- lattice parameter decreases from 4.258 Å to 4.130 Å at the top of charge, and returns to 4.25 Å after full discharge. Hard X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) suggests that Mn2+ is oxidized during charge toward Mn4+, a process which is reversed upon discharge. Figure 3b shows the Mn K-edge X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) for Li2Mn2/3Nb1/3O2F at various states of charge and discharge. As the charge capacity increases from 0 to 135 and 270 mAh/g, the Mn K-edge shifts from an energy close to MnO (Mn2+ reference) to that of Mn2O3 (Mn3+ reference) and then partway to the energy seen in MnO2 (Mn4+ reference). Further charging to 360 mAh/g leads to only minor shifts. The Mn K-edge almost completely returns to its original position after discharge. Although the exact amount of each valence state cannot be quantified, as the near- edge structure is sensitive to both the oxidation state and bonding environment,19 this result suggests that upon full charge Mn2+ is oxidized to Mn4+ with some Mn2+ or Mn3+ ions remaining. Full recovery to Mn2+ occurs upon discharge. This interpretation is further supported by a derivative analysis on the Mn pre-edge at ~6540 eV (Supplementary Fig. 5). Nb5+ does not participate in redox processes (Supplementary Fig. 6). While hard X-rays probe metal oxidation, soft X-ray absorption using a total-fluorescence- yield (TFY) can be used to investigate oxygen redox behavior. Fig. 3c traces the pre-edge features of O K-edge XAS spectra of Li2Mn2/3Nb1/3O2F at various states of charge. The pre-edge is primarily associated with the O 1s→2p transition and its intensity is attributable to the density of unoccupied Nb 4d / Mn 3d–O 2p hybridized states. We associate the pre-edge feature around 530.9 eV to unoccupied Nb 4d–O 2p hybridized states, as Mn2+-oxides (e.g., MnO) typically exhibit a pre-edge feature above ~533 eV.20,21 Charging to 135 mAh/g (theoretical Mn2+/Mn3+ limit) increases the intensity in the 530–532 eV range which is typical for Mn3+-oxides such as Mn2O3.21 After charging to 270 and 360 mAh/g, we observe a major intensity gain around 529 eV (feature A) characteristic for Mn4+-oxides (e.g., MnO2, Li2MnO3)20-22 suggesting some Mn3+/Mn4+ oxidation on charge. Along with this feature A, we see an intensity gain at 530–531 eV (feature B) after charging to 270 and 360 mAh/g. Previously, O oxidation has been shown to 5

create a broad component around 530.2 eV in Mn-based disordered Li-rich cathodes.8,17 Therefore, this feature B most likely indicates O oxidation which concurrently occurs with Mn3+/Mn4+ oxidation. Discharging to 320 mAh/g restores the pre-edge shape and intensity, indicating Mn and O reduction. Hence, the electrochemical processes in this compound are quite reversible. Ab initio study of Li2Mn2/3Nb1/3O2F In conventional Li–Mn oxides (e.g., LiMnO2, Li2MnO3), Mn oxidation up to Mn4+ is not competitive with O oxidation.8,10,16,17 The question is then: why is there a partial overlap between these redox processes in Li2Mn2/3Nb1/3O2F? The major differences between Li2Mn2/3Nb1/3O2F and other Mn-based Li-excess oxides are the presence of fluorine and the relatively large lattice parameter (a = ~4.26 Å for Li2Mn2/3Nb1/3O2F vs. ~4.19 Å Li1.3Mn0.4Nb0.3O2)8,16,17 which leads to a larger Mn–ligand distance. To elucidate the impact of these features on electrochemical behavior, we study the effect of F-coordination and lattice volume on the redox mechanism using density functional theory (DFT) calculations. Fig 4a and 4b show the Mn and O average oxidation states as a function of (i) delithiation (x in Li2-xMn2/3Nb1/3O2F) and (ii) varying degrees of compressive strain relative to the fully-relaxed discharged state ([l0-l]/l0 × 100 %, l0 = lattice parameter of the fully-relaxed discharged state, l = compressed lattice parameter). The vertical bars account for the range of results obtained from various structural models of disordered rocksalt Li2Mn2/3Nb1/3O2F. Initial delithiation up to x ≈ 0.667 (theoretical Mn2+/Mn3+ limit) only modifies the Mn oxidation state, but further Li removal leads to concurrent O and Mn oxidation. Smaller lattice volume increases the degree of Mn3+/Mn4+ oxidation at a fixed lithium level. At 270 mAh/g charge (x = 1.333) the experimentally observed lattice parameter reduction is 2.6 % (Fig. 3a). At this strain the calculations indicate Mn~3.5+ and O~1.8– average oxidation, supporting the presence of an overlap between Mn3+/Mn4+ and O redox, and fully consistent with our experimental results. This result seems to indicate that the large lattice parameter of Li2Mn2/3Nb1/3O2F is partly responsible for the overlap. Figure 4c-d clarify the impact of local environment on the oxidation of Mn and O. Fig. 4c compares the average oxidation state of Mn atoms which have two or less F ligands out of six 6

Page 7

anion neighbors, versus that of Mn with three or more F ligands. At a given level of delithiation, Mn atoms with high F-coordination are less oxidized than those with low F-coordination, indicating that the substitution of O for F favors lower Mn oxidation states and thus leads to more redox overlap with oxygen. On the oxygen side, we observe more oxidation from O atoms with five and four Li-nearest neighbors than those with three Li neighbors (Fig. 4d). This trend is consistent with previous theory and experiments that indicate that the lack of TM–O hybridization in Li-rich environments increases the energy of some oxygen orbitals so that they can be more easily oxidized.22,23 Hence, the presence of Mn–F bonds, the Li-excess O environments, and the larger bond distance of Mn-O in this material all contribute to some competitive Mn/O oxidation at very high states of charge. Nevertheless, owing to the large Mn2+/Mn4+ reservoir, O redox is much less needed in Li2Mn2/3Nb1/3O2F than in other Mn-based Li-rich materials, rendering Mn double redox an effective way to achieve high capacity without the typical polarization and capacity fade that is observed with excessive use of the oxygen redox. Structure and performance of Li2Mn1/2Ti1/2O2F With diverse choices of high valent cations, Mn2+/Mn4+ double redox can be realized in many different systems. As a demonstration, we have developed another novel material, Li2Mn1/2Ti1/2O2F, in which Ti4+ is the high-valent cationic species. This material also forms a disordered rocksalt phase (a = 4.206 Å) as can be inferred from the XRD pattern in Fig. 5a (Supplementary Table 1). SEM shows that, after shaker milling the compound with carbon black the average primary particle size is about 50 nm (Fig. 5b). TEM-EDS shows a uniform distribution of Mn, Ti, O and F (Fig. 5c). Li2Mn1/2Ti1/2O2F delivers high capacities similar to Li2Mn2/3Nb1/3O2F. When cycled between 1.6–4.8 V (Fig. 5d) and 1.5–5.0 V (Fig. 5e) at 20 mA/g, this material yields reversible capacities of ~260 mAh/g (~780 Wh/kg, 2738 Wh/l) and ~320 mAh/g (930 Wh/kg, 3264 Wh/l), respectively. These values are again among the highest values achieved by advanced cathode materials.10,15 Additional electrochemical data are presented in Supplementary Fig. 7. As in Li2Mn2/3Nb1/3O2F, the voltage profiles of Li2Mn1/2Ti1/2O2F barely change after the first cycle, indicating a reversible reaction without a major structural change or O loss, as evidenced by the DEMS results (Supplementary Fig. 3). The O-oxidation plateau appears only after 7

Page 8

charging above 230 mAh/g (> ~4.7 V), which is substantially delayed compared with other Mn- based Li-rich materials. Nevertheless, this plateau is slightly longer than in Li2Mn2/3Nb1/3O2F, likely due to the smaller Mn2+ content in Li2Mn1/2Ti1/2O2F, requiring more O oxidation to achieve a given capacity. Ex situ XRD of Li2Mn1/2Ti1/2O2F indicates a reversible change in lattice parameter, shrinking from ~4.20 Å to ~4.105 Å after a 400 mAh/g-charge and then recovering to ~4.19 Å after a 330 mAh/g-discharge (Fig. 5f). Hard XAS, shown in Supplementary Fig. 8, confirms Mn2+/Mn4+ redox in the material. As in Li2Mn2/3Nb1/3O2F, additional capacity beyond Mn-redox capacity is likely delivered by O redox. Based on its high capacity and reversibility, there is significant potential of Li2Mn1/2Ti1/2O2F as a high-performance Li-ion cathode. Outlook for the Mn2+/Mn4+ redox Double redox couples are tremendously important for the development of advanced cathodes. Indeed, today’s modern NMC-based layered cathode structures all rely to some extent on the Ni2+/Ni4+ double redox. With Li2Mn2/3Nb1/3O2F and Li2Mn1/2Ti1/2O2F, we have demonstrated that combined fluorination and high-valent cation substitution can introduce Mn2+/Mn4+ redox in a Li-excess disordered rocksalt structure, which leads to ultrahigh capacity Mn-based Li-excess cathodes without an excessive use of O redox. This discovery is important as our strategy can be widely applied to design high-performance Mn-based Li-excess cathodes which do not suffer from structural degradation triggered by extensive O redox. The combination of Mn2+/Mn4+ redox with the disordered cation structure8,11,16,18 and the partial replacement of O by F13,14,24 leads to a potentially large chemical space of new cathode materials. We expect to see significant optimization through the use of different high-valent charge-compensating elements, as well as through minor alloying additions to stabilize the structure further and increase other performance aspects. The disordered rocksalt framework has previously shown high structural stability,8,11,16 and its compositional flexibility, enabled by not requiring the preservation of the layered cathode structure, can be used to tune not only the Li- excess level for Li transport,11,12 but also the content of F and high-valent cations (e.g., Sn4+, Sb5+, Te6+). These handles can all be used to modify the size of the Mn2+/Mn4+ reservoir and balance

Mn- and O-redox activities. Finally, microstructure control such as by surface coating25,26 and 8

Page 9

carefully tailored electrolytes27,28 will further assist in the development of the high performance Mn2+/Mn4+-based cathodes for advanced Li-ion batteries. Conclusion Using fluorination and high-valent cation substitution, we have demonstrated Mn-based Li- excess cathodes that achieve ultrahigh capacity (> 300 mAh/g) and energy density (~1000 Wh/kg) through Mn2+/Mn4+ redox combined with a limited use of O redox. Our strategy of Mn-double redox leads to a range of new opportunities in the design of high performance Mn-rich cathodes for advanced Li-ion batteries, which can bypass issues triggered by an excessive use of O redox.

Methods Synthesis To synthesize Li2Mn2/3Nb1/3O2F, Li2O (Alfa Aesar, ACS, 99% min), MnO (Alfa Aesar, 99 %), Nb2O5 (Alfa Aesar, 99.9%) and LiF (Alfa Aesar, 99.99%) were used as precursors. For Li2Mn1/2Ti1/2O2F, Li2O (Alfa Aesar, ACS, 99% min), MnO (Alfa Aesar, 99 %), TiO2 (Alfa Aesar, 99.9 %) and LiF (Alfa Aesar, 99.99%) were used as precursors. Other than Li2O, which we used 10 % excess amount (vs. stoichiometric) to compensate for possible loss of Li2O during synthesis, stoichiometric amount of precursors were dispersed into (Ar-filled) stainless steel jars and then planetary ball-milled (Retch PM 200) for 40 hours at the rate of 450 rpm, during which Li2Mn2/3Nb1/3O2F or Li2Mn1/2Ti1/2O2F form mechanochemically. The total amount of precursors in each jar (50 ml) was approximately 1 g, and five 10 mm-diameter and ten 5 mm-diameter stainless balls were used as the grinding media. Electrochemistry To prepare a cathode film from Li2Mn2/3Nb1/3O2F or Li2Mn1/2Ti1/2O2F, 480 mg of active compounds and 240 mg of carbon black (Timcal, SUPER C65) were first shaker-milled for one hour in a (Ar-filled) 45 ml zirconia vial with 10 g of 5 mm-diameter yttria stabilized zirconia balls (Inframat Advanced Materials) as grinding media, using SPEX 8000M Mixer/Mill. Polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE, DuPont, Teflon 8A) was then added to the mixture as a binder, such that the cathode film consists of the active compounds, carbon black, and PTFE in the weight ratio of 60:30:10. The components were then manually mixed using a mortar and pestle and rolled into a thin film inside an Ar-filled glove box. To assemble a cell for all cycling tests, 1 M of LiPF6 in ethylene carbonate (EC) and dimethyl carbonate (DMC) solution (1:1, BASF), Glass microfiber filters (Whatman), and Li metal foil (FMC) were used as the electrolyte, the separator, and the counter electrode, respectively. Coin cells (CR2032) were assembled in an argon-filled glove box and tested on a Maccor 2200 or an Arbin battery cycler at room temperature in the galvanostatic mode otherwise specified. The loading density of the cathode film was ~6 mg cm-2. The specific capacity was calculated on the amount of the active compounds (60 wt%) in the cathode film. 10

Differential electrochemical mass spectrometer (DEMS) measurement A DEMS measurement was used to detect and quantify O2 and CO2 gas evolved during charging and discharging (Supplementary Fig. 3). The custom-built DEMS and the cell geometry used is described in previous publications.31-33 The electrochemical cells used with the DEMS device were prepared in a dry argon glove box (< 1 ppm O2 and H2O, MBraun USA, Inc) using modified Swagelok design and the same materials as discussed previously. The assembled cells were charged under a static head of positive argon pressure (~1.2 bar) after being appropriately attached to the DEMS. Throughout the charge, argon gas pulses periodically swept accumulated gases to a mass spectrometer chamber. The mass spectrometer absolute sensitivity has been calibrated for CO2 and O2, and therefore the partial pressures of these gases can be determined. The amount of CO2 and O2 evolved is then quantified based on the volume of gas swept to the mass spectrometer per pulse. Density functional theory calculations Density functional theory (DFT) analysis of the redox mechanism was performed using the Vienna Ab-Initio Simulation Package (VASP)34 using the projector augmented-wave method. First, structural models of the Li2Mn2/3Nb1/3O2F disordered rock-salt were obtained using a cluster-expansion based Monte Carlo simulation, chosen to find low-energy disordered structures with representative short-range order, while suppressing phase separation. The cluster-expansion Hamiltonian used for the Monte Carlo simulations consists of a decomposition of the internal energy of a particular atomic configuration on a rock-salt lattice into contributions from two-, three-, and four-body terms up to maximum interaction distance of 7.0Å, 4.1Å and 4.1Å respectively, relative to an ideal rock-salt lattice with a primitive lattice constant of 3.0Å, on top 12

Page 13

of an electrostatic model based on the formal charges of all species.35 To obtain the interaction terms, we first calculated 450 representative configurations of Li+, Mn2+, Nb5+, O2-, and F- on a rocksalt lattice within the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof exchange-correlation functional,36 supplemented with the rotationally-invariant Hubbard U correction37 to the transition metal d states to correct self-interaction error (UMn = 3.9 eV, UNb = 1.5 eV based on previously reported fits to oxide formation enthalpies38). These calculations were performed with a reciprocal space discretization of 25 A-1, 520 eV plane wave cutoff, and a 10-5 eV and 0.02 eV/Å convergence on total energy and interatomic forces respectively. The strength of each cluster interaction, as well as the dielectric constant, was then fit using a L1-regularized least squares regression, optimized by cross-validation, which resulted in an out-of-sample error of 9 meV/atom. The redox mechanism of Li2Mn2/3Nb1/3O2F was calculated on 12 structural models of 36 atoms each, obtained from the Monte Carlo simulations described above. Oxidation calculations were done using the hybrid Heyd-Scuseria-Ernzerhof functional,39 using a 650 eV plane wave cutoff, 10 A-1 reciprocal space discretization, and a 10-5 eV and 0.02 eV/Å convergence on total energy and interatomic forces respectively. The fraction of exact exchange was set to 0.30 based on a calibration to the Kohn-Sham gaps of α-Mn3O4, γ-MnOOH and β-MnO2 calculated within the G0W0 approximation, following previously reported methodology for reproducing the redox competition between transition metals and oxygen.40 To investigate the order in which various redox couples are activated in the material, suppressing major structural rearrangements, we trace the oxidation state of each species (obtained from the magnetic moment of each atom) as electrons are removed from the material and charge compensated by a uniform background charge,41 allowing the local atomic arrangements to relax at each step but keeping the lattice fixed. As the order of oxidation reactions is determined by the character of the valence band edge at various states of charge, such electron titration provides an efficient way to look at the electronic contribution to the redox mechanism.
