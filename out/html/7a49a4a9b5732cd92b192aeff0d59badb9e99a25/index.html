Single-layer transition-metal dichalcogenides (TMDs) receive significant attention due to their intriguing physical properties for both fundamental research and potential applications in electronics, optoelectronics, spintronics, catalysis, and so on1-8 . So far experiments have been mainly focused on MoX2 and WX2 (X = S, Se)13 , while many other monolayer TMDs still remain to be discovered. Here we report

the epitaxial growth of high-quality single-crystal, monolayer platinum diselenide 1

Page 2

(PtSe 2 ), a new member of the layered TMD family, by direct selenization of a Pt(111) substrate (analog of direct oxidation). A combination of experimental characterizations and theoretic calculations reveal the atomic structure of the monolayer PtSe 2 /Pt(111). Angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy (ARPES) data establish that, unlike bulk PtSe 2 , epitaxial monolayer PtSe 2 is a semiconductor.

Methylene-blue

photo-degradation

experiments

reveal a

photocatalytic activity four times larger than that of nanocrystals 9 . Calculations predict that monolayer PtSe 2 also has potential applications in valleytronics.

Following the discovery of the many unique properties of monolayer graphene 10, 11, attention has been being paid to other two-dimensional crystalline materials with novel properties like sizable bandgap. Among them, layered TMDs with the general formula MX2 , where M represents a transition metal from groups 4-10 and X is a chalcogen (S, Se or Te), are particularly desirable not only for fundamental research but also for technological development3-5,

12 .

Recent investigations of MX2 have resulted in

discoveries of dramatically different electronic structures at the monolayer limit compared to the bulk materials due to quantum confinement effects. For example, while pushing from bulk to the monolayer limit, MoS2 and MoSe2 show an indirect-to-dire ct bandgap transition13,14 . With these exciting findings, experimental research efforts so far have been mainly focused on semiconducting prototypical MX2 with group 6 transition metals (M=Mo, W). In the large family of layered TMDs, many other promising TMD and related quantum-defined properties remain to be explored experimentally. However, the synthesis and fabrication of high-quality, monolayer TMD materials other than group 6 TMD remains a challenge.

In this Letter, we report the growth of high-quality, monolayer epitaxial PtSe2 films on a Pt substrate by direct “selenization”, an analog of direct oxidation, which is a straightforward, precursor-free method. Unlike conventional growth methods of MX2 by exfoliation or chemical vapor deposition, the present epitaxial process is very direct: only one element, Se, is deposited on a Pt substrate. PtSe2 film has been determined by a combination of low-energy electron diffractio n (LEED), scanning tunneling microscopy (STM), scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) measurements and density functional theory (DFT) calculatio ns. Angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy (ARPES) measurements directly reveal the semiconducting electronic structure, which is consistent with calculated band structure of the free-standing monolayer PtSe2 , quite different from the semimeta llic band structure

of bulk crystals. We performed

methyl-blue

Monolayer PtSe2 films were grown by a straightforward precursor-free method, i.e., through a single step of direct selenization of a Pt(111) substrate (see schematic in Fig. 1a). Selenium atoms were deposited on a Pt(111) substrate, and the substrate was then annealed to ~200 ℃ to obtain epitaxial PtSe2 film. The growth of PtSe2 thin films was monitored by in situ X-ray photoemission spectroscopy (XPS). Figure 1b shows the XPS spectra of the Se 3d core level during the growth of PtSe2 thin films. The shift of the peak to lower binding energy and the appearance of two peaks (labeled by the blue arrows at binding energies 55.19 eV and 54.39 eV, which are the characteristic signal of Se2-) indicate the change of some Se atoms from Se0 to Se2- , corresponding to selenization of the sample. Further annealing of the sample to 270 ℃ results in the disappearance of Se0 peaks (at 55.68 eV and 54.80 eV) and the dominance of Se2- peaks, indicating full selenization, consistent with the formation of epitaxial PtSe2 films on the Pt(111) substrate (Pt is known to appear in compounds as Pt4+ or Pt2+).

To investigate the atomic structure of the as-grown epitaxial films, the samples were characterized by LEED. Figure 2a shows a LEED pattern. Hexagonal diffraction spots from PtSe2 (red circles) are observed to have the same orientation as those from the Pt(111) substrate (blue circles), suggesting rotational-domain- free growth. A (3x3) diffraction pattern of the epitaxial film is clearly identified, which corresponds to a well3

Page 4

defined moiré superstructure arising from the lattice mismatch between the epitaxia l film and Pt(111). Furthermore, identical LEED patterns were observed on the entire sample surface (4 mm x 4 mm in size), indicating the growth of a large-area, homogeneous and high-quality film.

To gain further insight into the epitaxial film, we performed STM studies. Figure 2b is a large-scale STM image with a well-ordered moiré pattern of PtSe2 thin film on Pt(111). The periodicity (b1 ) of this moiré pattern is equal to 4 times the lattice constant of Pt(111). Figure 2c is an atomic-resolution STM image revealing hexagonally arranged protrusions with an average lattice constant of a1 = 3.7 Å, which is the interatomic spacing of Se atoms in the (0001) basal plane of bulk PtSe2 . Therefore, we interpret the hexagonal protrusions in Fig. 2c to be the Se atoms in the topmost Se plane of a PtSe2 film. A regular (3x3) moiré superstructure with respect to the PtSe2 lattice is then established, with a periodicity of b1 = 3a1 ≅ 11.1 Å (labeled by the white rhombus). The orientation of the moiré pattern is aligned with that of the PtSe2 lattice. This is in agreement with the LEED observation (Fig. 2a), where the diffraction spots of the (3x3) superlattice are in line with those of the PtSe2 lattice. Based on LEED and STM measurements, the moiré pattern can be explained as the (3x3) PtSe2 supercells located on the (4x4) Pt(111) atoms.

To determine the thickness of the PtSe2 films, we performed a cross-section high-angle annular-dark-field (HAADF) STEM study of the PtSe2 /Pt(111) interface. A Z-contrast image of the PtSe2 /Pt(111) interface is shown in Fig. 2d. One bright layer combined with two darker layers observed on the topmost surface suggests a Se-Pt-Se sandwich configuration (as indicated by a model diagram superimposed in Fig. 2d). The atomically resolved bulk Pt substrate lattice with an experimentally measured interla yer spacing of 2.28 Å served as a reference for calibrating other spacing measureme nts. After the calibration, the spacing between the Se sublayers in the Se-Pt-Se sandwich is found to be 2.53 Å, which is in agreement with the calculated value in single-la yer PtSe2 . These atomic-scale cross-section data obtained by STEM further verify that the 4

Page 5

fabricated structure is indeed a single-layer PtSe2 film on the Pt(111) substrate.

The combination of LEED, STM and STEM studies indicate a (3x3) single- layer PtSe2 on a (4x4) Pt(111) structure. We then carried out DFT calculations based on this structure. The simulated STM image is shown in Fig. 2e, in which the overall features of the experimental STM image (Fig. 2c) are well reproduced. Only the hexagonal Se atoms in the topmost sublayer of monolayer PtSe2 are imaged. The remarkable agreement between the STM simulation and experimental STM observation strongly supports our conclusions and thus demonstrates the successful growth of a highlycrystalline PtSe2 monolayer on the Pt(111) substrate.

Having grown highly-crystalline, single- layer PtSe2 , we investigated its electronic structure by ARPES. Figure 3a shows ARPES data measured along the high symmetr y direction K-Γ-M-K in the hexagonal Brillouin zone at a photon energy of 30 eV. Data taken at other photon energies show the same dispersion, confirming the 2D character of the monolayer PtSe2 . Second-derivative spectra of raw experimental band structures (Fig. 3a) are depicted in Fig. 3b to enhance the visibility of the bands. Here the top of the valence band is observed to be at -1.2 eV at the  point, and the conduction band is above the Fermi level, indicating that monolayer PtSe2 is a semiconductor. It is quite different from the bulk PtSe2, which, according to calculations15 , is a semimetal. A direct comparison between the second-derivative of the ARPES spectrum (Fig. 3b) and the calculated band structure (green dotted lines in Fig. 3b) shows excellent quantitative agreement. Combining the ARPES spectra with DFT calculations, we confirmed that we synthesized a single- layer PtSe2 on a Pt(111) substrate, and that the PtSe2 essentia lly has the same electronic properties as that of the free-standing single- layer PtSe2 . For the first time, the band structure of monolayer PtSe 2 films has been determined experimentally.

The semimetal-to-semiconductor transition was revealed by DFT-LDA calculations. As shown in Fig. 3c, the band structure and density of state (DOS) suggest single-la yer 5

Page 6

PtSe2 is a semiconductor with an energy gap of 1.20 eV (2.10 eV bandgap is predicted from GW calculation16 ). As a comparison, the band structure and DOS of bulk PtSe2 are plotted in Fig. 3d, which confirmed that bulk PtSe2 is semi-metallic15 . When increasing the thickness, bilayer PtSe2 remains a semiconductor, but the energy gap decreases to 0.21 eV. Starting from a trilayer, PtSe2 becomes semi-metallic. (Calculated band structures of bilayer and trilayer

PtSe2 are listed in the Supplementar y

Information.) Therefore, only single- layer PtSe2 is a semiconductor with a sizable bandgap. This is different from the extensively studied MoS2 and MoSe2 for which an energy gap exists from single- layer to bulk material.

The opening of a sizable bandgap within the range of visible light makes monolayer PtSe2 potentially suitable for optoelectronic devices and photocatalysis. We explored the photocatalytic properties of monolayer PtSe2 by the degradation of methylene blue (MB) aqueous solution, which serves as a typical indicator of photocatalytic reactivity1 7 , 18 . The MB molecules

adsorbed on the PtSe2 films are degraded by electrons that are

excited by visible light. The time-dependent degradation of MB with a single layer PtSe2 catalyst was monitored

by checking the decrease in the intensities of

characteristic absorbance peaks of the MB molecules (shown in Fig. 4). The photo degradation portion of MB molecules reached 38% after visible-light irradiation for 24 minutes. This rate is about 4 times faster than the rate obtained using PtSe2 nanocrystals9 , putting single- layer PtSe2 in the same class as nitrogen-doped TiO 2 nanoparticles for photocatalysis19, 20 .

With the existence of an energy gap in the single- layer PtSe2 , optical excitations can occur between the Γ point and the valley point along the Γ-M direction. This is reminiscent of the recently discovered valley-selective circular dichroism in MoS 2 by circularly polarized light21-23 . To explore this possibility we calculated the degree of circular polarization of free-standing single-layer PtSe2 . The calculated circular polarization due to the direct interband transition between the top of the valence band (VB) to the bottom of the conduction band (CB) is shown in Fig. 5a. It shows signific a nt 6

Page 7

circular dichroism polarization along the M-K direction and near the Γ point. In view of the indirect energy gap, this process can be assisted by lattice vibrations. It is noteworthy that the circular dichroism polarization not only exists in the transitio n between the top of the VB and the bottom of the CB, it also exists in transitions to higher energy levels. Circular dichroism polarization due to transitions from the vicinity of the VB to the vicinity of the CB can be clearly seen in Figs. 5b, 5c and 5d. Due to this significant circular polarization, in the presence of a non-vanishing in-plane electric field, the anomalous charge current driven by the Berry curvature would flow to the opposite edges, leading to a valley-polarized current and the resulting quantum valley Hall effect24, 25 .

In summary, we successfully fabricated high-quality, single-crystalline, monolayer semiconducting PtSe2 films through a single-step, direct selenization of a Pt(111) substrate. Characterization by LEED, STM, STEM, and DFT calculations confir med the formation of single-layer PtSe2 . The ARPES study revealed the semiconduc ting electronic structure of the single- layer PtSe2 . Together with the photocatalytic performance that was observed experimentally, the present results indicate promise for potential applications in optoelectronics and photocatalysis. Moreover, the circular polarization of monolayer PtSe2 in momentum space shows promising potential for valleytronics devices. Thus, our results are a significant step forward in expanding the family of single- layer semiconducting TMDs and exploring the application potentials of thin TMDs in photoelectronic and energy-harvesting devices.

Methods Sample preparation. Single-layer films of PtSe 2 were grown in an ultrahigh vacuum chamber, with a base pressure of 3 x 10-10 mbar, equipped with standard MBE capabilities. The Pt(111) substrate was cleaned by several cycles of Ar + ion sputtering followed by annealing until sharp (1x1) diffraction spots in the LEED pattern and clean surface terraces in the STM images were obtained. High-purity Se evaporated from a Knudsen cell was deposited onto the clean Pt(111) surface at room temperature. The sample was subsequently annealed up to 270℃ to achieve 7

Page 8

selenization and crystallization. The growth process was monitored by XPS and LEED. After growth, the sample was transferred to an STM chamber for imaging and to an ARPES chamber for measuring the electronic band structure.

STEM characterization. The cross-section image shown in Fig. 2d was obtained from a JEOL JEM-ARM 200 operating at 200 kV with a sample prepared by a JEOL Ion Slicer. Practically identical images were also obtained from a Nion UltraSTEM 200 operating at 200 kV using a sample prepared with a Zeiss dual beam Focused Ion Beam instrument and a Fischione Nanomill. For the UltraSTEM 200, the probe-forming angle was ~30 mrad, and the inner angle of the HAADF detector was about 63 mrad. For the JEOL JEM-ARM 200, the probe-forming angle was 30 mrad, and the inner angle of the HAADF detector was about 90 mrad.

ARPES and XPS measurements. The ARPES data were measured using both synchrotron radiation (BL-1 at HiSOR, Japan) and an ultraviolet lamp with He Iα spectral line. For the synchrotron data the sample was held at 20 K. The energy analyzer was a VG Scienta 4000/8000 with total energy resolution set to less than 20 meV. The synchrotron XPS data were taken at a photon energy of 105 eV.

First-principles calculations. DFT calculations were performed within the local density approximation (LDA) by using the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP) 26 . The projector augmented wave method was employed27 . The periodic slab models included four layers of Pt substrate, one layer PtSe 2 , and a vacuum layer of 15 Å. All atoms were fully relaxed except for the bottom two substrate layers until the net force on every atom was less than 0.01 eV/Å. The energy cutoff of the plane-wave basis sets was 400 eV, and the k-points sampling was 5 x 5 x 1 with the Monkhorst-Pack scheme. Photocatalytic experiment. The photocatalytic activity of as-prepared monolayer PtSe 2 films was tested by catalytic degradation of methylene blue serving as a standard model dye under visible-light irradiation at room temperature. The ultraviolet/visible-light source was a 150 W Xe lamp located at a distance of 15 cm above the solution. A set of appropriate cutoff filters was applied to determine illumination wavelength and ensured that the photocatalytic reaction took place just under visible light. The as-prepared monolayer PtSe 2 films were exfoliated from the Pt(111) substrate by ultrasonication in aqueous solution. Then MB molecules and ethanol (0.01 mL) were added to the solution containing peeled PtSe 2 monolayers. Before photocatalytic reaction, the solution was kept in darkness for 1 hour in order to achieve an adsorption-desorption equilibrium between the PtSe2 film and MB molecules. After that, the suspension was exposed to visible-light irradiation. At time intervals of 4 min, solution samples were collected and their absorbance measured by a commercial Ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) spectrophotometer. Accordingly the intensity changes of characteristic absorbance peaks of the MB molecules were recorded. The photocatalytic performance of PtSe 2 monolayers was thus evaluated by the time-dependent degradation rate Ct /C0 shown in Fig.4b.
