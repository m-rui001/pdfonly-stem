Abstract The increasing demands on energy storage requires significant improvement of current Li-ion battery electrode materials and development of advanced electrode materials. This necessitates in-depth understanding of the reaction processes, degradation mechanism, and thermal decomposition mechanisms of electrode materials under realistic operation conditions. This can be achieved by in

situ/operando characterization techniques that provide information of the structure evolution, redox mechanism, solid-electrolyte interphase (SEI) formation, side reactions and Li-ion transport properties under operating conditions. In this review, recent progress of in situ/operando techniques employed for the investigation of the structural stability, dynamic properties, chemical environment changes and morphological evolution during electrochemical processes are described and summarized in detail. The experimental approaches reviewed in this paper include X-ray, Electron, Neutron, Optical, and Scanning probes. Each advanced technique has unique capabilities to study specific properties of electrode materials within specific limitations. The experimental methods and operating principles, and especially the in situ cell designs are described in detail. To illustrate the possibilities of each technique representative studies making use of these in situ/operando techniques are discussed and summarized. Finally, the major current challenges and future opportunities of the in situ/operando techniques are outlined. Several important battery challenges are anticipated to profit from these in situ/operando techniques, including the inhomogeneous reactions of high energy density cathodes, the development of safe and reversible Li metal plating and the development of stable SEI on electrodes.

1. Introduction Rechargeable lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) are the most widely used energy storage devices for portable electronics and electrical vehicles. These applications demand more advanced LIBs with higher energy and power density, longer calendar and cycle life, and better safety characteristics. The development of such advanced LIBs needs the optimization of current electrode materials, as well as the discovery and utilization of new novel electrode materials with better electrochemical performance. All of these require in-depth understanding of the relationship between the structural 2

Page 5

changes of the battery materials and the performance of the batteries during electrochemical processes, the degradation mechanism during cycling and aging, the thermal decomposition mechanism during heating in multi-length scales using various characterization techniques and their combinations. Recent years, tremendous progress has been made towards the development of advanced characterization techniques for LIBs under ex situ and in situ/operando conditions These techniques and studies provide information about the structure evolution, redox mechanism, solid-electrolyte interphase (SEI) formation, side reactions and Li-ion transport properties in testing cells under operating conditions. Although ex situ characterization techniques can provide valuable information about the battery materials, the post-process nature limits their ability in studying the kinetic properties of the materials, such as the detailed structural changes and intermediate stages during charge-discharge cycling and heating. In addition, due to the sensitivity of working electrodes to air and moisture, results from ex situ measurements about electrochemical processes such as valance changes, surface and interfacial reactions may not fully reflect what is really taking place. Therefore, obtaining information under realistic battery operating conditions is of vital importance for the development of LIBs. In situ and especially operando measurements can reveal more valuable information of electrode materials without disassembling the testing cells and explore the correlation between structural properties and electrochemical performances. Here, “In situ” means “in the natural or original position or place” while “operando” implies that the material is behaving as it would do in “real life”, in other words, the experiment is carried out under the operating conditions. Each advanced technique has unique capabilities to study specific properties of electrode materials. The electrochemical performance of electrode materials is not only related to the structure of the pristine materials but also to the structure stability and structure evolution during electrochemical processes. X-ray diffraction is a powerful tool to investigate the structure of electrode materials. The structure 3

Page 6

information of pristine crystalline electrodes can be obtained by ex situ X-ray powder diffraction. In contrast, the structure evolution during cycling can be obtained by in situ/operando XRD using specially designed in situ cell. The atomic positions and detailed structure information can be obtained by performing Rietveld refinement. In addition, small angle X-ray scattering provides information about particle size and shape of the electrode materials. Beside the structure evolution, the redox activity of various elements of the electrode materials is also a key factor for the specific capacity. In order to investigate the reaction mechanism of electrode materials and optimize their electrochemical performance, it is critically important to monitor the changes of valance state and coordination environment of each element in the electrode materials during the electrochemical processes. X ray absorption (XAS) has the ability to monitor the changes of the electronic structure and chemical bonding, as well as the valance state during cycling. For electrodes containing multiple elements, it is crucial to investigate the contribution of each individual element, which is possible by XAS as it is element specific. An vivid example of this are the Li-rich layer structured cathodes, which have attracted a lot of attention based on to their high specific capacity. XAS has been applied to study the redox process of both the transition metals and oxygen. Although for crystalline electrodes, the structure information can be obtained by XRD, monitoring structural changes in amorphous materials can be achieved by X-ray Pair Distribution Function (XPDF or just PDF) analysis, which allows to obtain the local ordering of materials. For PDF the total scattering signal is acquired, including Bragg reflections and diffuse scattering, where the amorphous electrode structure evolution is obtained after Fourier Transformation. Like X-ray diffraction, neutron diffraction (ND) is a powerful structural analysis tool for crystalline materials, in particular useful for the detection of specific light elements such as Li and H, and for elements that have a similar atomic mass. The complementary sensitivity of X-ray and Neutron diffraction, makes that in many cases combined refinement is advantageous. 4

The strain evolution at the single particle level can be used to evaluate the degradation processes of electrode materials, including active material cracking, electric disconnection from current collector, and the increase in impedance. In this case coherent X-ray diffraction imaging (CDXI) in Bragg geometry is a unique probe for the strain evolution at the nanoscale.

The structural and chemical environment changes are usually accompanied by morphological changes, impacting the electrochemical performances of materials. Therefore, various in situ imaging techniques have been developed that demonstrate great potential in studying the microstructure transformation and ion diffusion at various length scales and high temporal resolution. In situ TEM and SEM measurements can reveal the morphology evolution of the electrodes such as volume expansion and crack formation. More recently, transmission X-ray microscopy (TXM) has been applied to investigate the electrode materials and provides multi-dimensional information coupled with in situ X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES). Compared with other imaging techniques, which can only investigate the thin sample or surface characteristics, TXM is able to study the large, thick samples since it is based on the third-generation synchrotron sources. In addition, Raman spectroscopy, AFM, Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) are surface sensitive techniques, therefore they are able to probe the surface and interfacial evolution of electrode.

In this review, recent progress in operando/in situ characterization techniques will be described and discussed for each technique, including in situ cell designs, experiment set-ups, the materials studied and exemplary results, as well as the relevant references. X-ray techniques have been represent a powerful family of tools to investigate the electrochemical mechanisms of electrode materials. X-ray characterization techniques provide electronic and crystal structure information through scattering, spectroscopy and imaging techniques. The most commonly used in situ/operando X-ray characterization techniques for lithium ion battery research are: X-ray scattering techniques, including X-ray diffraction (XRD) and X-ray pair distribution function (XPDF); X-ray spectroscopy including hard X-ray absorption spectroscopy (hXAS) and soft X-ray spectroscopy (SXAS); X-ray imagining techniques including transmission X-ray microscopy (TXM), scanning X-ray transmission microscopy (STXM) and coherent X-ray diffraction imaging (CXDI). 2.1 In situ/operando X- ray characterization techniques XRD is based on the scattering of X-ray’s, the interference of which produces diffraction patterns from crystalline or partial crystalline structured materials. XRD is widely used to study the crystal structures and phase transformations in electrode and solid electrolyte materials and in situ/operando XRD is used to monitor the structure changes of LIB materials during (lithiation/delithiation) cycling or during temperature changes (heating/cooling). Compared to lab-sources, synchrotron-based X-ray sources provide higher intensities and larger photon energies resulting in larger penetration power, shorter measurement time and better signal-to-noise ratio all favorable for in situ/operando studies, however care should be taken for beam damage of the battery materials.

Like XRD, XPDF is a scattering technique, but in contrast it uses the total diffracted signal (Bragg and diffuse) from which, via Fourier Transformation, the pair distribution function can be obtained representing the probability to find any two atoms at a certain distance. Because it takes into account the diffuse XPDF allows to study the short range order, which may deviate from the long range order observed by XRD. The peak position in the PDF spectrum corresponds directly to atom-atom distance

(bond length) within the material. The intensities of peaks within the PDF are related to the relative abundance of each atom-atom distance and the coordination number is related to the area of the peaks. The highest distance at peaks provides insight into the size of particle and coherent domain. Thereby, XPDF can be used to study materias with low or no crystalinity, such as the conversion type electrode materials, which is difficult to investigate by XRD. In addition, the structure models can also be refined using the PDF data, witch can be used to benchmark computational simulations [1].

XAS is based on X-ray photon induced electronic excitations from the core level to an empty electronic state for the element being probed. This makes it an element specific probe of the local atomic arrangement and electronic structure of a materials. There are two distinct XAS energy regions relative to the absorption edge, the latter defined as the edge where there is a sharp rise (discontinuity) in the (linear) absorption coefficient of X-rays for the specific element. This occurs when the energy of the photon corresponds to the energy of a core level of the atom (K, LI, LII, LIII, etc. corresponding to the creation of electron holes in the 1s, 2s, 2p1/2, 2p3/2 etc. atomic core level). The region about 30 eV near the absorption edge is defined as X-ray absorption near edge structure (XANES), and the region above 40 eV extending to serval hundred eVs is called extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS). XANES provides information about the electronic structure, for instance the oxidation states, and the site-symmetry of the absorbing atoms, while EXAFS provides quantitative local structural information, such as bond length, degree of ordering, and the coordination number around the adsorbed atoms. XAS can also be divided into hard XAS (hXAS, > 5 keV) and soft XAS (sXAS, < 3 keV). The hXAS are used to study the K edges of various transition metal elements, while the sXAS is used to measure K-edges of light elements like C, N, S and O, and the L-edges of transition metal elements. In situ XAS can provide the electronic structure evolution of specific element and structural changes of materials in both crystalline and amorphous phases. For soft XAS, there are two 7

different detection modes: Electron yield (EY, including total and partial electrode yield modes: TEY, PEY) is able to provide more surface information by probing less than 5 nano meters (nm) in depth, while Fluorescence yield (FY) mode provides the bulk information with a probing depth of several hundred nm. In situ XRD and XAS are usually combined to study the phase transition process and reaction kinetics during (de)lithiation of electrode materials [2-4].

There are three commonly used X-ray imaging techniques: TXM (full-field non-scanning) is similar to conventional visible light microscopy and allows the rapid acquisition of images which is ideal for tomographic imaging and kinetic studies; STXM (scanning transmission microscope) is a scanning technique having a much smaller X-ray beam size, which is scanned through the sample area of interest. Data acquisition of STXM is considerably slower than TXM, but with the advantages of flexible field of view and less radiation damages to the sample. More detailed theory and experimental description of X-ray microscopy is given in several review papers [3, 5]. During an in situ/operando experiment, (S)TXM imaging coupled with oxidation state analysis by XANES is usually used to monitor the morphology evolution, oxidation state and chemical phase changes, and composition changes during the (de)lithiation process at the same time [6]. Most of these studies are 2D rather than 3D in space, which limits their ability to give quantitative assessment of particle phase evolution, volume expansion or fracturing [7]. 3D microstructure imaging can be realized by X-ray tomographic techniques (XTM), where the 3D structure image can be reconstructed by using the series of 2D images collected when rotating the in situ cell through 180° [8]. XTM is usually combined with either XAS or XRD to provide spatially resolved images of the oxidation state, chemical phase and structural evolution of electrode materials [9, 10]. The 3D chemical phase evolution can also be obtained by using TXM with the implementation of in situ XANES nanotomography, which reconstructs the 3D structure through acquisition of 2D projections over a complete angular range of 180° with 8

multiple energy points [11].

Coherent X-ray diffraction imaging (CDXI) is another form of X-ray imaging which is fundamentally different from other forms of microscopy as it relies on the interference produced by coherent X-rays and phase-retrieval algorithms to reconstruct the 3D electron density mapping and atomic displacement fields in nanocrystals. CDXI is a powerful tool to study the strain or dislocation evolution, electron density distribution and lattice distortions under operando conditions [12, 13]. Recently, extensive efforts have been devoted to the understanding of the phase transformation of LiFePO4 during Li insertion/extraction at different rates and the underlying mechanism. During (dis)charge, LiFePO4 undergoes a first-order phase transformation leading to a two-phase equilibrium composed of a lithium-rich Li1-βFePO4 (LFP) phase and a lithium-poor LiαFePO4 (FP) phase. However, the detailed LFP/FP phase transition mechanism has been debated for several years, especially for high rate cycling. The formation of intermediate phases is considered essential to understand this mechanism. However, monitoring the formation and changes of these intermediate phases at high cycling rates is challenging as it requires data collection in the range of seconds and excellent cell performance. Taking advantages of the high flux X-ray source provided by the synchrotron, Orikasa et al. [28] applied time-resolved (TR) XRD to detect a metastable crystal phase between LFP and FP with increasing current density for the first time. Their result demonstrate the presence of a metastable LixFePO4 (x=0.6-0.75) phase during the first discharge and subsequent charge-discharge process. Lattice parameters of the new phase are similar to the thermally formed metastable. The metastable phase disappears in approximately 30 minutes during electrochemical relaxation. The authors proposed a phase transition path through the metastable phase, which could decrease the nucleation energy and account for the excellent rate capability. In order to determine the phase transition mechanism dependence on the current rate, Wagemaker and co-workers [30] investigated the phase evolution of LiFePO4 electrode during a rate range of C/5 to 60 C using a pouch cell. At the different current rates, operando XRD data showed an obvious difference in the evolution of the [002] reflection. At a low rate of C/5, the electrode materials are at a near-equilibrium state, where as expected the Li-rich phase gradually transfers to the Li poor phase. In contrast, at high current rates, the larger overpotentials where shown to drive LFP through a solid solution reaction occurring in a fraction of the electrode, proposed to be the consequence of the larger free energies 13

The TXM and XANES spectra show that, at a low rate of 0.1 C, the multiple LiFePO4 particle system shows homogeneous phase distribution and ‘concurrent’ intercalation pathway. 3.2 TEM Transmission electron microscopy (TEM relies on an electron beam that can transmits through a thin sample to form images with ultra-high spatial resolution. In addition to the morphology, TEM enables to collect structural, and chemical information of electrode materials at the nano-meter and even at the atomic scale, by combining it with electron diffraction (ED), electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). To ensure the high vacuum environment for TEM operation required to maximize resolution and transmitted signals, a few in situ cells were designed, either in an open-cell configuration or a sealed-cell configuration (SLC). 3.2.1 In situ/operando TEM characterization experiment set up In situ TEM characterization for battery studies was first designed by using an open-cell configuration, which avoided the usage of volatile electrolytes to satisfy the high vacuum requirement in an electron microscope. Three types of early stage cells with different electrolytes have been developed, having no conventional electrolyte, a solid electrolyte or an non-volatile liquid electrolyte. These types of cells allow the in situ study of the structure of materials with atomic scale resolution [91, 92]. However, as shown in Figure 9a, in an open-cell configuration, only a fraction of the nano-electrode under investigation is connected to the electrolyte and the results obtained may have limited validity. In addition, the geometry limiting factors could distort the results and the phenomena observed may not fully reflect the situation of the real bulk electrode. A slightly different approach is shown in Figure 9b. In this case FeF2 nanoparticles are loaded on the amorphous carbon film, which acts as support and medium for electron and ion transport, to form a Fe2F-C cathode [93]. The open-cell battery allows real-time atomic scale imaging and concurrent composition analysis, which is most widely used to investigate the (de)lithiation mechanism [92, 94, 95]. However, the open cells have disadvantages: (1) Point contact with the electrolyte; (2) The electrolytes used in these cells are not commonly used in 36

Page 39

real battery cells; (3) Ionic liquid polymerization under electron beam radiation can significantly reduce the cycle numbers ; (4) The solid electrolyte causes large overpotentials [96].

To address the shortcomings of the open cells, Wang and his coauthors [97] developed an operando TEM electrochemical liquid cell, suitable for typical liquid electrolytes, to study the lithiation/delithiation behavior of single Si nanowires (NW), which is schematically illustrated in Figure 9c. They used a Si NW as working electrode and a Li metal foil as counter electrode which was sealed with an electrolyte in a cell having a SiNx membrane on Si chips as windows for TEM imaging. Muller and his coauthors [98] independently developed an electrochemical liquid flow cell and holder which significantly improved the electrode design. They used the glassy carbon as a substrate for working electrode instead of the high-atomic number materials used before. The carbon substrate scatters electrons weakly and enhances the resolution and contrast of the images. The patterned electrodes on the electrochemical chip are shown in Figure 9d. Using this cell design and in combination with valence energy-filtered TEM (EFTEM), the real-time lithiation/delithiation process of LiFePO4 in an liquid electrolyte was observed. Compared to most-widely used core-level spectroscopy using larger energy loss electrons with lower energy, this technique provides higher resolution for thicker liquid layers, which is attributed to the lower sensitivity to plural scattering due to the higher energy of the electrons [98, 99]. Zeng and her coauthors [100] demonstrated the use of an non-flow version of the electrochemical liquid cell shown in Figure 9e. They observed the SEI layer formation on one side of the electrode and the lithiation, dendritic Li growth, as well as the subsequent stripping of Li and thinning of Li−Au layer on the other side of electrode. Thereby the correlation between cyclic stability and the passivating film formed in real LIB was understood. Following Muller group’s work, Unocic and his coauthors [99, 101] utilized a commercial microfluidic electrochemical cell with a three-electrode configuration as shown in Figure 9f which is composed 37

Page 40

of microfabricated devices with glassy carbon and platinum microband electrodes. They demonstrated that quantitative electrochemistry experiments can be performed with these small-scale cells. Although the majority of SLCs use SiNx membranes as window, some other materials such as graphene has also been used to replace SiNx window in order to enhance the atomic-level imaging of lithiation dynamics for Si nano-particles as shown in Figure 9g [102]. Platforms with a sealed liquid cell (SLC) are compatible with most relevant liquid electrolytes, however the disadvantages are: (1) The cost of high-precision nanofabrication, the lower flexibility for analytical TEM techniques, and the difficulty of active material manipulation; (2) Thick liquid layers induce poor image contrast and resolution, and the risk of liquid electrolyte leakage. Considerable technical details can be found in previous specialized reviews [103].

In addition to the liquid cell, experimental setup for in situ characterization of the interfacial phenomena in all solid-state batteries were developed by Meng and coauthors [104]. The Li-ion movement and the charge transfer at the interfacial layer were investigated based on a LiCoO2/LiPON/Si thin-film micro-battery by in situ TEM coupled with EELS as shown in Figure 9h. Compared with the open-cell and liquid-cell configuration, the-all solid-state micro-battery is still in its infancy and many problems need to be addressed:(1) the current resolution is challenging to meet the small dimensions of micro batteries;(2) instability of the electrolyte;(3) electron beam induced damage [105].

TEM studies of batteries in their native environment are problematic, because many battery components, e.g. lithium-containing electrode, organic liquid electrolytes, solid electrolyte interphase (SEI), are chemically reactive and sensitive to electron-beam irradiation. Cryogenic-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) has emerged as a structural biology characterization technique. The intrinsic 38

Page 41

structure features of frozen samples could be preserved and viewed with high resolution at low temperature (100 K) and under low-dose conditions (<100 e Å–2). Cryo-TEM can potentially be a powerful technique to image beam-sensitive battery electrode materials, in particular, reactive Li metal anode. Li et al. in Cui's group developed a cryo-transfer method as shown in Figure 9i: with electrochemically deposited Li onto copper grid and washed with electrolyte, they immediately flashfreeze the specimen in liquid nitrogen. The dendrites will retain their electrochemical state with the relevant structural and chemical information preserved. Meng et al. designed Li metal deposition cell for cryo-TEM imaging as schematically shown in Figure 9j, in which a grid was placed above the Cu foil on the cathode side and used as a part of the substrate with conventional carbonate electrolyte. After deposition and rinsed with dimethyl carbonate (DMC), the grids were then loaded in a covered cryogenic dewar with continuous Ar flow; the holder was pumped down to 10–5 bar and quickly loaded into a vacuum-transfer chamber, which process minimizes the potential damage and contamination during sample transfer.

3.2.2 Examples of materials and processes studied by in situ/operando TEM In situ TEM has been widely applied to the study the evolution of the morphology, structure, and chemisty during (de/)lithiation as well as interface related phenomena, including SEI formation, dendrite evolution. It has increasingly attracted attention over the past several years. A large number of the studies has been reported on anode materials, such as SnO2, Si and Ge, exhibiting large changes in morphology during lithiation/delithiaion. A summary of the most relevant papers utilizing in situ/operando TEM characterization of different electrode materials/cell/electrolyte together with the main processes studied can be found in Table 3. Because several review papers have been published here the focus will be on recent advances in the study of anode materials during the (de)lithiation, especially focusing on studies of promising materials with an innovative coating/doping or heterostructure, as well as the studies of interface phenomena.

The most commonly used anode material is carbon based, typically graphite. Recently, Dong et al. [108]

developed a novel carbon based anode material with 3D branched foam constructed from a

tubular N-doped graphitic network and investigated the (de)lithiation real time with in situ TEM. It was reported that the lithiated junction branched portions have a more disordered structure and display short-range ordering. Compared to the lithiated straight wall domains, the junction domains can store more Li+ and promote rapid Li+ diffusion due to widening of the lattice spacing. For some anode materials, such as spinel transition metal oxides, the volume change upon (de)intercalation reactions is negligible and directly visualization is challenging. Effective doping/coating of electrode materials, as well as composite or heterostructure electrode materials can potentially improve the electrochemical performance, typically attributed to improved electronic conduction and lithium ion transport as well as the effective protection of the active materials. For metal oxides, recent efforts have been focusing on using nanocomposites or hybrid systems where the nanosized metal oxides are integrated with conducting additives. Tan et al [112] designed a specific hierarchical structure by oxidative growth of CuO on a Cu substrate followed by sputtering of CNx, to form three-dimensional arrays with a core-shell nano-architecture. The coreshell nano-architecture accommodates the volume change during lithiation/delithiation and the threedimensional arrays provide abundant electroactive zones and electron/ion transport paths. Through in situ TEM and quantum calculation, it was reported that the 3D NiCo2O4 nanosheets in this material can provide high electron conductivity, without participating in the lithiation/delithiation reaction and that the thickness of the layered V2O5 plays a key role for the Li diffusivity and contributes to the capacity of the electrode.

Si is an attractive anode material for next-generation lithium ion batteries because of its high theoretical capacity. However, it suffers from large volume changes of silicon over charge–discharge cycles. The Cui’s group [114] designed a hierarchical Si anode with a pomegranate like, where Si nanoparticles are encapsulated by a conductive carbon layer with enough room for expansion and contraction during (de)lithiation. A series of silicon pomegranates with different gap sizes were synthesized to find a sufficient and well-defined internal void space, which is necessary to maintain structure integrity. With in situ TEM observation of the structure change on lithiation, it was found that with the initial size of the nanoparticles (~80 nm) and their size distribution, silicon pomegranates with a ~30–40 nm gap size are most promising. The spatially confinement in the pomegranate structure shows superior cyclability, where the lower electrode-electrolyte contact area resulted in a higher coulombic efficiency, larger volumetric capacity and better cycling stability. Recently, Li et al. in Cui’s group [115] introduced another method to encapsulate Si microparticles with multilayered graphene cages. The mechanical deformation of this material was observed in situ with external load applied as shown in Figure 11a. The graphene cage exhibits resilience to the external load with its superior mechanical strength and flexibility, as compared with the fragile amorphous carbon. The lithiation process of graphene-encapsulated SiMP is observed directly during battery operation. The volume expansion is highly anisotropic. The flexible and strong cage is able to accommodate the 44

4. Neutron Unravelling the basic processes, such as the crystal structure evolution during (de)lithiation, the surface change of electrodes in real time and even tracking the lithium distribution and transport path, are important contributions of Neutron scattering techniques. Such information is critical to understand the origin of capacity decay, charge rate-phase change relationships, and safety. Operando and in situ Neutron based techniques display their unique advantages by their complementary scattering cross sections compared to that of X-rays. The operando and in situ neutron techniques reviewed here are neutron diffraction, neutron depth profiling, neutron reflectometry and neutron imaging used to investigate the surface, bulk structure and morphology changes in battery materials. Table 4 list most relevant papers of in situ/operando neutron characterization of different electrode materials together with the main processes studied. 4.1 In situ/operando neutron techniques Neutron diffraction (ND): Neutrons interact with the nucleus of atom, which is nondestructive given the low neutron energy, and the weak interaction also leads to high penetration depths, which is favorable for probing the bulk region of crystalline materials, which has proven to be suitable for operando and in situ LIB studies. Compared with X-ray scattering, neutrons are more sensitive to light elements like H, Li, and O, particularly in the presence of heavier transition metal elements as often present in electrode materials. Neutrons also facilitate to distinguish between elements with a similar atomic mass like Mn, Co and Ni, having a similar scattering strength for X-rays. Thereby, operando and in situ neutron diffraction provides complementary structural information compared to X-ray diffraction for LIB studies.

Neutron Depth Profiling (NDP): Neutron depth profiling (NDP) making use of the large cross section 53

Page 56

capture reactions of thermal neutrons by a number of specific isotopes, including 6Li. Thereby NDP allows quantitative measurement of 6Li and which was employed under in situ and operando conditions in Li-ion batteries quantify the lithium concentration and distribution as a function of electrode depth during (de)lithiation in LIB. The working principle of NDP for Li is based on neutron capture reaction with 6Li to form an α-particle (4He) and a triton (3H) according to: 6

Li + n → 4 He + 3 H

The initial energy of α-particles and tritons are well defined amounting 2055 KeV and 2727 KeV, respectively. The reaction depth of the original isotopes can be deduced by measuring the residual energies of emitted particles, resulting in the Li concentration as a function of depth perpendicular to the surface of the electrode [180, 181]. In situ NDP provides information on the Li reaction kinetics, transport and irreversible trapped Li behavior, in detail described in a recent review[182]. The maximum depth that can be probed depends on the stopping power of the material, which for battery current collectors and electrode materials typically amounts 30 to 40 m, with a resolution in the order of 10 to 60 nm.

Neutron reflectometry: Neutron reflectometry (NR) is a scattering technique where the specular reflection of neutrons from the surface or interface is measured as a function of the wave vector transfer perpendicular to the sample surface expressed by equation Q = [4π sin(θ)]/λ, in which θ is the angle of incidence neutron beam with sample surface and λ is the wavelength of neutrons. Fitting the reflectivity with a suitable layered model results in the layer thickness, roughness and the layers scattering length density (SLD), which can be used to determine the layer composition. NR is sensitive to interface morphological and compositional change over a length scale extending from 5A to thousands of angstroms. NR is usually used for in situ measurement of the interface in LIB,

54

Page 57

especially looking at SEI formation processes which allows quantitative determination of the SEI properties as a function of electrolyte composition during (de)lithiation, providing direct insight of the intercalation kinetics and intercalation pathways with high spatial and temporal resolution. It is important to realize that NR requires very smooth surfaces/interfaces.

Neutron Imaging: Neutron imaging has a similar working principle as X-ray radiography. A detector is placed behind the illuminated cell to measure neutron attenuation, by which parameters such as the Li concentration and gas generation volume can be calculated via the Lambert-Beer Law [183]. Neutrons are strongly attenuated by some light elements like hydrogen and lithium, which is complementary to X-ray imaging [184]. In situ neutron imaging has been applied to visualize the lithium distribution and diffusion during (de)lithiation [185], electrolyte degradation induced gas evolution [183], and electrolyte wetting behavior during battery production [186]. The current limitation of neutron imaging is the limited by the spatial resolution amount 15 to 25 m. Nevertheless, it provides a basis for comprehensive in situ studies of complete cells for instance. 4.2 In situ neutron characterization experiment set up In situ set up for NPD: For in situ Neutron Powder Diffraction (NPD), commercial batteries are often applied. However, despite the large penetration depth of the neutrons, measurements on commercial cells are complicated by the many cell components and the resulting additional undesired Bragg reflections and increased background intensity. In addition, the large amount of hydrogen in the electrolyte and polymer separator will induce a strong background in NPD data due to the large incoherent neutron scattering cross section of hydrogen. To minimize the incoherent scattering, the use of deuterated electrolytes and special fluorinated separators are a commonly adopted strategy. Commercial batteries are not directly suitable for in situ NPD without proper modifications. The

5. Optical techniques 5.1 Raman The Raman effect results from the inelastic scattering of monochromatic probing light when it interacts with a material. A typical Raman spectrum is a plot of the intensity of the scattered light as a function of its frequency difference from the incident probing light, and such a frequency difference is called the Raman shift. The Raman shift corresponds to the vibrational energy levels of the system, which are characteristic of the Raman-active modes for certain molecules or crystals [238, 239]. Parameters like crystal symmetry, bond or structural (dis)order, and strain can affect the vibrational modes of the molecule. Therefore, many aspects of the structural, mechanical and chemical changes in the electrodes, the electrolyte and the electrode/electrolyte interfaces during electrochemical cycling can be studied and understood via in situ Raman spectroscopy.

There are many advantages

to investigating Li-ion batteries under operating conditions using in situ Raman. It can be a nondestructive and noninvasive technique, which would allow a continued investigation of the same electrode sample using other techniques. Since Raman spectroscopy does not require long-range structural ordering, it allows the study of

‘amorphous’ compounds, or cycled electrode materials

having poor crystallinity which are otherwise difficult to study with XRD. With the introduction of the confocal scanning microscope by Minsky in the 1960s [240], some of the drawbacks of the conventional optical microscope, including low contrast and low spatial resolution, were significantly improved. Different from a conventional microscope, the confocal microscope measures the intensity of the light reflected by a very small area of sample, which bestows on the material a Raman enhanced spatial resolution and the capability of collecting optical sections from thick samples. When combined with a spectrometer, a confocal microscope can be used to measure spectra from a specified spot of a sample [241]. 69

The Li dendrite growth mechanism is related to the ionic transport in electrolytes, the electrode reactions and the SEI formation. It is very important to understand how ion distribution and depletion affects the Li deposition and morphology. Raman spectroscopy can detect local lithium concentrations [Li+] by measuring the Li+-solvent interactions or anion concentrations based on ‘electroneutrality’. Therefore it is widely utilized for investigating the solvation structure and SEI composition. However, conventional spontaneous Raman microscopy suffers from an intrinsically weak signal and poor temporal resolution, making it difficult to follow the rapidly changing electrolyte concentrations. On the other hand, in comparison with spontaneous Raman microscopy, stimulated Raman scattering (SRS) utilizes two spatially and temporally synchronized picosecond laser pulse trains [268]. This gives several advantages including high sensitivity, fast imaging speed, fine spatial resolution (~500 nm), a label-free nature and intrinsic 3D optical sectioning. Yuan Yang 77

Page 80

et al. [269] successfully utilized SRS microscopy for investigating the relationship between the Li dendrite growth mechanism and the ion concentration at a Li metal anode surface. In order to minimize the convection and electro-osmotic effects present in a liquid electrolyte, a Li/gelelectrolyte/Li symmetric cell was used. As shown in Figure 20, three distinct dynamic stages of Li growth were observed during the charging process. In the first stage (0 to17 min), the electrode surface is nearly flat, and mossy lithium was formed with two growth rates: a slower average (vave) rate of ~0.6 µm min-1 and a maximum (vmax) rate of ~1.2 µm min-1, respectively. Dendritic Li protrusions started to grow with a higher vave and vmax of 0.9 and 2.1 µm min-1 and Li+ depletion partially occurred near the electrode during the second stage (17 to 26 min). After full depletion, rapid growth of dendritic Li with the highest vave and vmax values of 2.0 and 3.7-5.2 µm min-1 was observed during the third stage (after 26 min), indicating that dendritic growth dominated this stage. Further analysis has revealed a positive feedback mechanism between the lithium dendrite growth and the heterogeneity of the Li+ concentration: in the regions with fast Li growth, once depletion starts, the growth rate speeds up and the Li+ concentration at 10 µm from the Li growing front, or [Li+]10µm, simultaneously increases, indicating that Li dendrites approach the region with a higher [Li+]; and thus, growth is accelerated. In contrast, if [Li+]10µm is low, Li growth is always slow. Based on these observations, they suggested that homogenizing the ion concentration and flux on a Li surface might suppress the growth of dangerous Li dendrites in partial and full depletion stages. Such results provide new insight into the Li dendrite growth mechanism and show that SRS microscopy is a very powerful technique to investigate the effects of designed additives and artificial SEI layers on Li morphology and deposition.

5.2 FTIR Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) is based on the absorption of an infrared beam over a wide spectral range (14300 and 20 cm-1). This is done through the measurement of light intensity before and after interaction with the sample. The absorbance is calculated as a function of wavelength with a series of absorption peaks, which correspond to excitations of certain molecular vibration, rotation/vibration or lattice vibration modes, or a combination thereof [288]. The corresponding frequencies can be used to identify the specific functional groups and the intensities of these infrared peaks are linearly correlated to the amount of species in the sample. FTIR provides important information about the molecular composition and structure. Attenuated total reflection (ATR) is a powerful technique to collect FTIR spectra. It is based on the multiple-internal-reflection geometry in an IR transparent crystal which reflects the infrared beam several times at the interface between the crystal surface and the sample. The IR signal collected after several reflections contains the spectrum of the sample within the penetration depth of the IR beam. FTIR is a usable method for the analysis of interfacial phenomena because it can detect species involved in the electrochemical processes at molecular level. Due to its high sensitivity to organic molecules, FTIR has been widely applied to analyze interfacial reactions between the electrode and electrolyte in Li-ion batteries, including to study electrolyte decomposition (salt, solvent, and electrolyte additive), reaction mechanisms, gas production, solvent intercalation and SEI formation for many different electrode materials. In situ FTIR has been demonstrated to be an effective and non-destructive technique for the direct real-time study of interfacial reactions. Scanning probe microscopy Scanning probe microscopy (SPM) includes a series of microscopic techniques that form high spatial resolution images of surfaces by scanning a physical probe over the sample. SPM can be operated under various environments such as liquid or electrochemical environment, offering unique advantages compared with other techniques, e.g. electron microscopies. The SPM techniques are based on either force or electric current detection. 6.1 Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM) AFM is a typical force-based technique, more precisely, based on probes consisting of a tip placed at the extreme of a cantilever, where a laser beam reflection from the backside serves to gauge the forces exerted on the probe [300]. The scanning images are obtained by recording the phase differences between the drive signal and cantilever response [301]. 6.2 Scanning electrochemical microscopy (SECM) Scanning electrochemical microscopy (SECM) is a current-based SPM techniques, with the capability to provide electrical or electrochemical information about the electrode surface at the nanoscale [323]. SECM consist four essential components: a low current bipotentiostat, a high resolution three-dimensional (3D) positioning system, an ultramicroelectrode (UME) tip, and a data acquisition system as shown in Figure 25a. SECM is based on the measurement of the current through the tip or at the apex of a micropipette electrode, when it is held constant or moved in a solution in the vicinity of a substrate. The presence of a substrate perturbs the electrochemical response of the tip/micropipette electrode and this perturbation provides information about the nature and properties of the substrate with micrometer and nanometer resolution. SECM operation modes mainly include generation/collection and feedback modes. Generation/collection modes include substrate generation/tip collection and tip generation/substrate collection, where the main difference is the origin of redox reaction. The feedback mode is the most widely operational as shown in Figure 25b. A biased tip is scanned through the substrate of interest, and a redox mediator R is oxidized to O at the tip. If the substrate is inert, diffusion of redox mediator R is hindered, resulting a decrease in the current, which is called negative feedback. If the substrate is conductive, it allows for the reduction of species O back to R, resulting in an increase in the tip current. This is called positive feedback response [324, 325]. Recently, SECM has been successfully employed in the field of LIBs for the investigation of several processes occurring at battery electrodes with particular focus on the kinetics of Li+ intercalation/deintercalation as well as the SEI formation and evolution [326]. 7. Conclusion and perspectives During the past several decades, great progress has been made in developing and applying advanced in situ/operando characterization techniques for battery research. These techniques provide new approaches to study the structural, morphological and chemical characteristics of electrode materials and components of batteries, as well as their correlations with the electrochemical battery performance.

In this review paper, important and widely applied in situ/operando experimental probes are reviewed including: X-ray, Neutron, Electron, Optical and Scanning techniques. The applicability and uniqueness of each technique is summarized, and the experimental approach and procedures are described, especially including the operation principles and in situ/operando cell designing. Finally, to illustrate the application of the in situ/operando techniques several representative studies of electrode materials using in-situ/operando techniques are discussed.

X-ray: The structural, chemical, and interfacial properties of electrode materials can be probed by Xray and neutron-based characterization methods (averaged over larger sample volume but lower spatial resolution), or by the electron techniques (smaller sampling volume but much higher spatial resolution). The tomographic techniques for X-ray or electron imaging can provide images of particles or even entire battery components in three dimensions. Experiments designed to probe the depth profiles of electrode materials along the cell stacking direction using neutron techniques have also been successfully carried out. Time resolved characterization techniques such as TR-XAS, TRXRD have been successfully developed for investigating the dynamic processes of electrode materials. These measurements can record the structural changes with very high temporal resolution. Energy materials and devices are complex systems, and a multi-technique scientific approach is necessary to

103

Page 106

characterize them accurately. Both hard X-ray and soft X-ray techniques, both imaging and spectroscopy tools, both ex situ and in situ conditions are needed to truly understand these complex systems and efficiently tailor their properties[346]. Compared with tender X-ray or soft X-ray experiments, it is easier to conduct in situ and operando measurements using hard X-ray sources, since their penetration power is larger. On the other hand, it is more challenging to design in situ cell for soft X-ray measurement because it requires high vacuum environments for the sample chamber. Therefore, the in situ cells for soft X-ray measurement are specially designed with ultrathin silicon nitride windows to separate the liquid electrode from the vacuum chamber.

Neutron: Operando Neutron Powder Diffraction (NPD) has several advantages over XPD, such as higher sample penetration, sensitivity to low atomic number elements and the low neutron energy makes this a non-destructive and non-invasive technique. However in situ NPD experiments are challenging as it requires a large amount of sample and the typical large hydrogen content (present in electrolyte and binder) reduces the signal to background ratio and the conductive carbon and binders lead to broad features in the diffraction patterns. Therefore, in situ NPD experiment should be carefully designed. For example, replacing hydrogen in the battery components with deuterium (e.g. deuterating the electrolyte) can significantly improve the signal to noise ratio. By the development of in situ/operando cells, Neutron depth profiling is increasingly used to monitor the Li-density as a function of depth in the electrodes, providing insight in Li-ion transport and reversibility of the reactions. Temporal resolution is challenging, however, further development in dedicated setup’s and cell design will make this technique increasingly powerful for Li-ion based battery research in the future.

Electron: Compared with X-ray and Neutron, electron techniques, such as TEM, can provide a 104

Page 107

combination of nanoscale down to atomic level spatial resolution and millisecond temporal resolution that is not achieved by any other technique. However, typically realistic electrolytes are not suitable in the vacuum environment required for these techniques and beam damage is a major concern. The nanobattery configurations used for in situ TEM investigations are quite different from the configuration of practical batteries. Working with liquid electrolyte in situ/operando cells is limited to low-resolution imaging and solid state battery systems are inherently more suitable. Improvement of temporal resolution are suggested by the development of fast electron detection cameras, integrity of highly sensitive and fast chemical detection and mapping, and enhanced cell design.

Optical techniques are known as non-destructive, non-contact and non-vacuum analysis tools, which show less restriction on the in situ cell design. In situ Raman spectroscopy is a powerful tool to study the lithiation/delithiation related phase changes occurring at electrode surface [347]. By in situ Raman, it's possible to investigate the electrode and electrolyte simultaneously. However, the analysis species are limited due to the spectroscopic selection rules, which can only analyze those that are Ramanactive. For interface analysis, Raman and FTIR can be analyzed from the same location at the same time with specially designed cell, which can lead to more accurate analysis. In addition, Raman are incorporated with non-optical microscopes, such as AFM and SEM, to improve the spatial resolution substantially and to correlate Raman spectra to local morphological features [241]. The advanced in situ SRS microscopy with excellent spatial resolution can be applied to investigate ion transport/intercalation inhomogenies, dynamic ion movement in confined channels, material separation, and catalytic process products, which will guide the further design of high-performance materials [269]. The ultimate step for a significantly improved and wider application of in situ Raman spectroscopy will consist in systematically combining a rigorous experimental approach with appropriate lattice dynamics simulations. This should make the in situ Raman spectroscopy more 105

Page 108

efficient, in terms of both analytical and mechanistic analysis, and it prompts the interest of this technique for many researchers implicated in materials science applied to the field of energy storage [238]

.

Scanning probe techniques can provide real-time and less-destructive monitoring of the interfacial topography/morphology and electrochemical properties at nanoscale. It performs well in a liquid environment, and doesn't require special sample preparation or ultra-high vacuum environment to operate. AFM offers the great potential in the observation of morphological and mechanical changes of electrodes surface with information from multiple channels and minimal destructive impact[313, 348]. However, AFM is a surface technique which limits its application to characterize sample topology and surface properties. The versatile SPM techniques and their various operation modes have been utilized to measure the morphology and other properties of the electrode interface at high resolution [302]

. SECM can provide electrochemical and topographic information of the interface. Its

electrochemical imaging at nanometer scale is an interesting alternative to optical or electron-beam methods. Since the detection with SECM is not limited to specific fluorescent or Raman active molecules, it can be used with a wide range of insulating/conducting entities, and differs from AFM as the tip never contacts the surface. However, nanometer-level characterization is challenging because the tip size and distance from the substrate largely determine the resolution [349]. Compared with AFM, the resolution of SECM are relatively low and its application to LiBs is still not very well developed. Improvement in the probe fabrication, maintenance, and stability will broaden the application of SECM. In addition, the implementation of distance control between the tip-to-substrate is also an important issue [324, 350].

For specific Li-ion battery topics it is expected that the described in-situ/operando techniques will 106

Page 109

contribute with essential understanding:

(i). Optimized cathode materials that enable higher battery energy densities are very important for the development of future LIBs. Using thicker cathodes is one simple effective way to increase the practical energy density of battery systems, effectively reducing the relative weight of inactive battery parts. However, this introduces Li-ion transport problems and inhomogeneous electrochemical reactions, especially at high rate cycling. Therefore, it is relevant these processes with depth resolved techniques, will provide valuable information for the design of optimized electrode architectures. In situ NDP technique has been successfully developed for investigating the lithium transport or lithium concentration within cathodes. However, limited work has been reported on depth resolved studies of electrode materials using XRD, XAS or XAS. XRD and XPDF can be used to monitor the structure and lattice parameter evolution, mechanical strain distribution, as well as the local structural changes of cathode materials, while XAS can provide information about the chemical and local atomic environment of cathode materials during cycling. Taking the advantage of micron- even nano-focused beam size from synchrotron light sources, in situ work using these techniques to obtain depth resolved information in electrodes appears a promising future development.

(ii).Another important research field is the development of safe and reversible lithium metal anodes. Lithium metal is considered the ultimate anode for Li-based high-energy density rechargeable batteries[351]. However, unsafe dendritic deposition of Li and poor cyclability are hindering the practical application of Lithium metal batteries. Up to now, the fundamental mechanisms including dendrite nucleation/growth and decomposition reactions have not been well characterized and explained under realistic in situ and operando conditions. To achieve this it is essential to develop suitable in situ techniques to investigate the formation mechanism of dendritic and porous lithium 107

Page 110

structures as well as the degradation mechanisms of the lithium metal anodes. AFM and SECM are effective in evaluating the interface properties, which can provide topography, mechanical and electrochemical properties. Recently, Raman using SRS technique has been applied to capture Li ion concentration [269]. In addition to the surface properties, the application of XRD could help to extract the structure evolution and NDP to provide spatial distribution/density of Li [205]. For the detail nanostructure and crystallography analysis, conventional TEM is limited due to severe beam damage. The application of cryogenic TEM could help to solve this problem [106, 107], however so far it's not possible to realize experiment under in situ/operando condition for LIBs research.

(iii).Forming a stable solid-electrolyte interphase (SEI) or cathode/electrolyte interphase (CEI) is an important to enhance the electrochemical performance of battery systems [352]. Generally, a stable and uniform SEI layer can prevent continuous parasitic reactions between the electrodes and the electrolyte, which is essential to achieve a high coulombic efficiency. Stable CEI layers can prevent the occurrence of unfavorable phase transformation through surface reconstruction of cathode materials. Therefore, the development of advanced characterization techniques to systematically investigate the formation and properties of SEI/CEI layers, such as composition, uniformity and the thickness are essential to assist the development of optimized of anode, cathode and electrolyte combinations. As pointed out by Khalil Amine et al, many surface-sensitive techniques, such as FTIR, Raman spectroscopy, and AFM have been actively applied for in situ characterizations to probe the surface chemistry, for example, the formation and evolution of SEI [353].

As pointed out by C. P. Grey and J. M. Tarascon, it is vital to continue on the development of new analytical approaches, optimized to probe specific battery chemistries. Important aspects that should be taken into account are that these advances should be non-invasive, and allow realistic battery 108

Page 111

assemblies and operation [354]. The development of combining different characterization techniques is another important direction, which will provide multi-dimension, multi-modal information about the materials and component of lithium batteries. Clearly, in situ/operando techniques developed for lithium batteries can be expected to be easily adopted for studies of other electrochemical systems such as Na-ion, Li-S and Li-O2 batteries, making these development of general importance for the field of batteries.
