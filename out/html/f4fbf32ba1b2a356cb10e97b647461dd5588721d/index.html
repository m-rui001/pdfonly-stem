An Artificial Interphase Enables Reversible Magnesium Chemistry in Carbonate Electrolytes Abstract To make magnesium (Mg) battery chemistry practical, a crucial need is a noncorrosive and low-cost electrolyte that allows reversible Mg deposition/stripping at the Mg anode while supporting high-potential cathodes. In lithium-ion batteries, the successful strategy is to form a passivation interphase to ensure reversible intercalation of lithium into graphite anodes. However, this strategy cannot be applied to Mg chemistry because most passivation interphases would block Mg2+ ions from crossing the electrode/electrolyte

interface,

completely

inhibiting

the

reversible

Mg

deposition/stripping. Thus, to avoid passivating the Mg anode, highly reduction-resistant electrolytes based on organometallic Mg salts and ethereal solvents have been used for Mg deposition/stripping. These electrolytes, however, are quite vulnerable to oxidation at cathode surfaces and essentially preventing the possibility of high-voltage Mg batteries. Here, we develop a new approach that differs fundamentally from the pursuit of lowvoltage oxidation-vulnerable electrolytes. By engineering a Mg2+-conductive artificial interphase on the Mg surface, we demonstrate highly reversible Mg deposition/stripping chemistry—even in the high-voltage, oxidation-resistant, carbonate-based electrolytes— which has not been achieved to date. Surprisingly, the artificial interphase enables the reversible cycling of an Mg/V2O5 full-cell in carbonate-based electrolyte even with a 3 molar water-additive. We believe that this approach successfully decouples the anodic and cathodic requirements for Mg electrolytes for the first time—providing a new avenue for Mg electrolyte research that could eventually make this promising battery chemistry practical.

Rechargeable lithium-ion (Li-ion) batteries based on intercalation chemistries are approaching their energy-density ceiling, so there is an urgent need to explore new battery chemistries that offer high energy density at reduced cost. The rechargeable magnesium (Mg) metal battery is one such “beyond Li-ion chemistries” whose divalent nature exhibits a lower gravimetric capacity (2,205 mAh g-1) compared to that of metallic lithium (3,862 mAh g-1), but a volumetric capacity (3,832 mAh cm-3) nearly twice as high as lithium (2,061 mAh cm-3). These high theoretical values would significantly promote the applications of Mg metal batteries for vehicle electrification and large-scale energy storage.1-5 Additional enthusiasm for a Mg battery is sparked by the abundance of Mg in the Earth’s crust and its non-dendritic nature when crystallizing under electrochemical conditions (in sharp contrast with Li).6,7 However, the reversible deposition/stripping chemistry of Mg can only be realized with a solid-state electrolyte (SSE) that provides a wide voltage window but is kinetically slow,8-10 or when highly corrosive and oxidationlabile electrolytes are used. The most representative examples include the Mg salts based on carbanion (such as Grignard reagents) and hydride anions (such as boron-cluster hydrides) dissolved in ethereal solvents.1-4,11-15 Unlike the working mechanism for current Li-ion electrodes (in which the reduction of the electrolyte is required to form the conductive solid-electrolyte interphases), these exotic electrolyte components do not form passivating layers on Mg anodes and thus permit reversible operation of a Mg anode.16-18 These electrolyte components, which are strongly reduction-resistant but oxidationvulnerable and highly corrosive,3,4,14,19-22 basically constitute an intrinsic barrier to the possibility of a high-voltage Mg battery operating above 3.0 V.23

In this work, we report that an artificial Mg2+-conducting interphase can be engineered on the Mg anode surface that facilitates a reversible Mg deposition/stripping process. More importantly, the low electronic conductivity of this artificial interphase prevents the electrochemical reduction of the electrolyte, thus allowing the use of carbonate solvent that is more resistant to oxidation. The Mg anode protected by such an interphase demonstrated markedly improved performance of both symmetric Mg cells and full Mg/V2O5 batteries using non-corrosive Mg salts (Mg(TFSI)2) and carbonate solvent. Furthermore, by using the interphase-protected Mg electrode, we have demonstrated the reversible cycling of the Mg/V2O5 full cell in an electrolyte containing 3 molar water. Mg intercalation into V2O5 has been known to be facilitated by the presence of a small amount of water; however, water would passivate the Mg surface and shut down the reversible Mg chemistry.24,25 Only under the protection of an artificial solid-electrolyte interphase (SEI) can reversible Mg plating/stripping at the anode and intercalation/deintercalation at the cathode be made possible in the presence of water. As a proof of concept, we demonstrated that, using this artificial Mg2+-conducting interphase, it is possible to decouple the anodic and cathodic requirements for a Mg electrolyte so that it can support both Mg deposition/stripping at the anode and high-potential intercalations of Mg2+ at the cathode. This discovery will provide a new avenue for Mg electrolyte design and synthesis that could eventually lead to a high-voltage (> 3.0 V) Mg battery. The artificial interphase is a Mg2+-conducting polymeric film made from thermalcyclized polyacrylonitrile (cPAN with a molecular formula of (C3H3N)n) and Mg trifluoromethanesulfonate (Mg(CF3SO3)2), also known as Mg triflate. Conclusions In summary, we have presented a new approach to resolve the dilemma faced in Mgbattery research, i.e., the conflict between an ethereal organometalic electrolyte and the desired oxidation stability, by engineering an artificial Mg2+-conductive interphase on the surface of a Mg metal electrode, so that reversible Mg deposition/stripping chemistry can be enabled in electrolytes once deemed impossible for Mg batteries. The elastic and Mg2+-conducting but electronic-insulating polymeric interphase on the surface of Mg metal can effectively prevent the electrochemical reduction of the electrolytes and the water therein, while still allowing Mg2+ to migrate (ionic conductivity 1.19×10-6 S cm1

)—thus making it possible to use electrolyte components that are oxidation-resistant and

non-corrosive. Superior performances of full cells using the interphase-protected Mg electrodes have demonstrated the feasibility of constructing high-energy Mg-metal batteries with high-voltage oxide cathodes and oxidation-resistant electrolytes; such characteristics have been precluded by the conventional approach of ethereal-based Mg electrolytes. Above all, the compatibility of the artificial-interphase-layer protected Mg anode with water has been well verified in a full-cell test, rendering it feasible to 16

Methods Fabrication of Mg electrodes. The Mg electrode with an artificial interphase was composed of Mg powder (Alfa Aesar, -325 mesh), carbon black, PAN, and Mg(CF3SO3)2 in a weight ratio of 77%–10%–10%–3%. The presence of the native oxide layer is the intrinsic characteristic of the Mg metal. It is difficult to remove the native oxide layer, due to the reactivity of the pure Mg metal and the stability of the magnesium oxide. In our study, we did not do any treatment for the as-received Mg particles. The prepared mixture was dissolved in dimethylformamide (DMF) and stirred using a magnetic bar for 5 hours. The Mg electrode was prepared by casting the obtained slurry on SS foil. Then the coated electrode was annealed at 300ºC for 1 hour under Ar to trigger the cyclization of PAN units. The bare Mg electrode comprised 80% Mg, 10% carbon black, and 10% polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) binder. The slurry in N-methylpyrrolidone (NMP, Sigma Aldrich) was thoroughly mixed before coating. An applicator was used to blade the slurries onto SS current collectors. All handling of Mg electrodes was conducted in a glove box filled with Ar.
