Li-ion synaptic transistor for low power analog computing

Increased demands on computing performance have led to growing interest in new computing paragdigms that could be more energy efficient or more suitable for solving certain classes of problems. Algorithms based on analog neuromorphic architectures show promise for tasks such as pattern recognition, but realizing physical device elements with both the required functionality and performance has been challenging. Here we show that by taking advantage of materials and phenomena from solid-state batteries, it is possible to realize three-terminal analog switching devices that possess all of the required attributes for a neuromorphic architecture: stable multi-level analog states, write linearity, low write noise, and potential for scalability and low power dissipation. Furthermore, we show that simulations of backpropagation using our experimentally measured device properties achieve the highest classification accuracy of any device to date. Simulations of the device physics establish the factors governing performance and serve to assess scalability and energy dissipation. Concerns regarding the demise of Dennard’s power density scaling in CMOS has generated growing interest in alternative computing devices and architectures, such as neuromorphic computing1,2. While the use of CMOS to mimic neuromorphic core architectures3 has made promising leaps forward in the areas of pattern recognition and machine learning, it requires a large volume of memory at a high hardware cost4. On the other hand, neuromorphic architectures based on resistive memory devices in a crossbar array are predicted to achieve orders of magnitude greater memory density and energy efficiency by further reducing data movement during computation and by taking advantage of multi-level analog states5. 1

Page 2

Resistive memory crossbars are estimated to reduce the energy required to perform computations in neuromorphic algorithms by as much as six orders of magnitude when compared to a conventional CPU6,7. For data intensive applications, energy consumption in a conventional CMOS architecture is dominated by data transfer between the processor, the SRAM (static random access memory), and the DRAM (dynamic random access memory). Embedded crossbar memory eliminates the need for this data movement as processing and memory storage are co-located, mimicking the high-density interconnectivity found in the brain. In addition to acting as embedded memory, the crossbar can be used to perform neural computations. Specifically, there are two key neuromorphic computational kernels that are more efficient on a crossbar: vector-matrix multiplication and parallel rank-1 weight updates. These operations are carried out on a crossbar through updates to the array of resistive memory elements. A key advantage is that updates in a crossbar are executed in parallel5, whereas a conventional CMOS architecture must perform these operations sequentially for each weight resulting in a higher energy cost and delay. Despite the possibility of tremendous gains in energy efficiency and numerous reports of resistance switching behavior in a range of materials systems, to date no memristor-based crossbar architecture has emerged as a clear competitor to CMOS8-12. Recently, prototype neural networks based on crossbar arrays of filamentary type metal oxide memristors13 as well as phase change memories (PCM)11 were reported, demonstrating that these networks can be trained to perform simple image recognition and file classification. However, both device types currently suffer from several performance limitations that reduce their accuracy and scalability: excessive write noise14-16, write nonlinearities11,17-20, and high switching currents10,21,22. To overcome these limitations we present a Li-ion synaptic transistor for analog computation (LISTA). LISTA is an all solid-state, three-terminal electrochemical transistor with a resistance switching mechanism based upon the intercalation of Li into a conductive channel of Li1-xCoO2. For LISTA, resistance switching does not induce large structural transformations that are common for memristive devices, such as filament formation or amorphous-to-crystalline phase transformation. The intercalation of Li in Li1-xCoO2 only weakly strains the crystalline lattice, and Li diffusion in Li1-xCoO2 has an activation energy of ~0.25 eV23,24. In contrast, metal oxide memristors require high fields (E > 106 V cm-1) and PCM requires high 2

Page 3

currents (I > 106 A cm-2) to overcome energy barriers of ~1eV for filament formation and 2-4 eV for nucleation of the crystalline phase, respectively25,26. Here, we demonstrate that the structurally stable, diffusion-driven switching mechanism of Li intercalation allows LISTA to operate with a low write noise, a near linear response to write operations, and orders of magnitude lower switching currents when compared to metal oxide memristors or PCM. LISTA is the first electrochemical transistor to operate using Li-ions with a solid electrolyte. Previously, three-terminal, electrochemical transistors with liquid electrolytes have been demonstrated using Li-ions and oxygen vacancies as dopants27-29, but an all solid state device has far greater potential for scaling to dense arrays since liquid gates are difficult to scale below 100 nm dimensions or to integrate with existing solid state device architectures. A two-electrode device based on Li intercalation was demonstrated previously, but displayed only ~4 conductance states with non-linear response30. Figure 1a is a false color SEM micrograph of a LISTA device in cross section that shows the device structure. Here, Pt source and drain electrodes (yellow) contact a 120 nm thick Li1-xCoO2 channel (red) with a spacing of 2 m. A 400 nm thick lithium phosphorous oxynitride electrolyte (LiPON) layer (green) separates the channel from a 50 nm Si gate electrode (purple). The LiPON solid electrolyte was chosen for scalability (~ 20 nm)31, a large chemical stability window, and a high electrical resistivity (>1015  cm)32. Figure 1b is a diagram illustrating the resistance switching mechanism in a LISTA device. Here, voltage applied to the gate, VG, determines the source-drain conductance, GSD, by controlling the Li content in the channel. Applying voltage VG negative of the open circuit potential (OCP ≡ VG at IGD = 0) electrochemically drives Li-ions from the channel into the gate electrode through the solid electrolyte. Similar to oxygen vacancies, Li-ions act as dopants in a variety of transition metal oxides, contributing mobile electrons (LixWO3, etc.) or holes (LixNiO2, etc.) as their concentration within the lattice is varied33,34. 

The layered, pseudo-hexagonal ( R 3 m ) Li1-xCoO2 was chosen because of its well-known reliability and endurance in electrochemical cycling in solid state Li-ion batteries35. For Li1-xCoO2, the removal of Li oxidizes Co from valence 3+ to 4+ and generates positively charged polarons 36,23. As the fraction x in Li1-

This process is highly reversible, and the application of a positive voltage VG with respect to OCP acts to re-intercalate Li ions (reducing Co from 4+ to 3+) and return the channel to its initial conductivity. To demonstrate modulation of the Li1-xCoO2 conductivity and to quantify the full range of conductance window available for computation, we recorded GSD as a function of the OCP over a full charge-discharge cycle (Fig. 1c, black). For the charging branch in Fig. 1c (negative IG), GSD increases from 4.5 µS to 270 µS and shifts the OCP from 0 V to -4.2 V. After charging, a subsequent positive IG discharges the device to near 0 V and returns it to the low-conductance state. The voltage hysteresis between charge and discharge is primarily due to the use of Si for the gate electrode, which undergoes structural changes during lithiation24,37. The voltage range depends on the choice of gate and channel materials, the possible Liion concentrations in each electrode, and the Li potential in each electrode24. For example, for Li1-xCoO2, oxygen loss and an irreversible transition from layered to spinel structures limit the potential to 4.2 V vs. Li (i.e. OCP < -4.2 V, corresponding to a fraction x > 0.47 in Li1-xCoO2)38. To demonstrate the multi-level states required for analog computation, a LISTA device is operated by sending current or voltage pulse trains to the gate electrode. The pulse train consists of a ‘write operation’ (finite gate current or gate voltage pulse) and a corresponding ‘read operation’ (zero gate current or gate voltage) where the conductance state is measured with the source and drain electrodes. A plot of GSD(t) under current-controlled operation (alternating pulses of -350 nA for 2 s and 0 nA for 2 s) is shown in Fig. 1d. Here, the pulse train results in a stepped increase in GSD(t) from 257 S to 266 S. During each write operation, an increase in the recorded GSD(t) is observed due to Li leaving the channel, while during each read operation GSD(t) remains constant at a value corresponding to the new conductance state. This process is reversible, and a subsequent train of positive current pulses (IG = +350 nA for 2 s and 0 nA for 2 s) returns the device to the initial conductance level. In voltage-controlled mode (Fig. 1e), a train of negative voltage pulses (VG = -75 mV for 2 s and 0 mV for 2 s) relative to the OCP results in a similar stepped increase in

GSD(t) from 276 S to 290 S and subsequent recovery. Of the 4 LISTA devices tested all exhibited nearly identical behavior and performance in conductance switching. In addition to excellent performance in resistance switching, LISTA devices were found to maintain conductance levels for several weeks after write operations and may hold their state of charge up to several months. Since electrons are needed to drive the electrochemical reaction at the LiPON/ Li1-xCoO2 interface, the high electrical resistivity of LiPON ensures that the resistance switching is non-volatile. Indeed, solidstate batteries with LiPON electrolyte have demonstrated self-discharge rates < 3 percent/year39. Neural applications generally do not require the 10-year retention needed by NAND, and often target training periods of less than one week after which the weights can be read and stored digitally if needed. While non-volatility is essential for low energy operation, a high write-endurance is also required for neuromorphic applications14. LISTA was found to perform with high endurance over a voltage window of 3.0 to -4.1 V (inset, Fig. 1c). Figure 2a depicts the conductance GSD(t) while the OCP was cycled between 3.0 to -4.1 V a total of 40 times (writing 200 states on average). For all 40 cycles GSD(t) exhibits predictable, saw-tooth-like conductance ramps without degradation (or decrease) in the overall conductivity. Voltage pulsing produced a nearly identical response (see supplementary information, Fig. S5.1). In order to demonstrate the power of LISTA’s linearity and low noise, we simulated the performance of a neural network based on backpropagation using the experimentally measured LISTA device properties. Simulating performance in spike-timing dependent plasticity was also considered, but backpropagation was chosen because quantitative performance metrics that have been characterized very thouroghly, such as classification accuracy. In our simulations, a simple two-layer network (one hidden layer) is used for backpropagation as depicted diagrammatically in Fig. 3a. Here, the numerical weights in the network layer are mapped directly onto the LISTA device conductance states to simulate performance as a resistivememory element. The measured non-linearity and write noise from Fig. 2 b,e are fed directly into the simulation by updates to the various numerical weights: first, the initial state of a device G0 is determined, second the state is updated by the average change ∆GM from Fig 2b-e with a sampled noise value from the corresponding probability distribution. Using the measured device properties, two matrix operations are performed on a simulated crossbar: a vector matrix multiply, and parallel rank 1 outer product update. In this scheme, N×M devices are simulated in a N×M crossbar array, as depicted for voltage controlled operation in the circuit schematic in Fig. 3b. In order to carry out the weight updates for matrix operations, the conductance states are programmed by the product of the voltage signal along the rows (blue horizontal lines) and the columns (black vertical lines). To prevent the LISTA device from discharging after programming, a two terminal access device is needed40. A LISTA access device will require orders of magnitude smaller current capacity 6

Page 7

than existing metal oxide or PCM access devices. This is a significant advantage, as engineering access devices with high current capacity remains a barrier to commercialization.41 In addition to write operations, reading from the crossbar is carried out by applying voltages along the rows (black vertical lines) and measuring currents from the columns (black horizontal lines). Here, the crossbar is considered as part of a ‘neural core’ that is used to accelerate the matrix operations while external electronics are used to process the inputs and outputs as part of a ‘digital core’, as described previously5,11. More details on the implementation of LISTA in a crossbar for both voltage and current controlled operation can be found in the supplementary information (Section S3). LISTA was found to perform with the highest classification accuracy in backpropagation simulations that has been demonstrated by any resistive memory device to date and was found to match ideal accuracy in some cases. Our network simulations were used to analyze three different data sets: a small image version (8×8 pixels) of handwritten digits from the “Optical Recognition of Handwritten Digits” dataset42; MNIST, a large image version (28×28 pixels) of handwritten digits43; a Sandia file classification dataset44 (256 bytepair statistical attributes to classify 9 file types). The results of the training for voltage-controlled device operation (green) are plotted alongside simulations of CMOS with ideal, 32-bit floating point numerical accuracy (blue) in Figs 3c,e. Although neuromorphic algorithms are highly inefficient using CMOS, weight updates proceed with nearly ideal accuracy. After training, the resulting classification accuracy approaches neuromorphic algorithm limit and provides an important benchmark to compare with resistive memory. For small digits and file types LISTA classification accuracy is nearly indistinguishable from the ideal numerical training and approaches 90% within the 2nd training epoch. Simulations with large digits had similarly impressive performance but with 0.4% deviation from the ideal numerical training. The network performed almost identically for current-controlled operation, although with ~0.2% greater accuracy (see supplementary information Fig. S3.5) for larger digits due to a higher linearity. In contrast to LISTA performance, other types of resistive memories have yet to approach the ideal numerical limit of classification accuracy. In Figure 3c,e (red) the performance of two-terminal TaOx memristor devices45 approach only 20-50% accuracy in identical simulations. Here, the network training is 7

severely limited as there are not enough multi-level states to adequately support learning. For other devices (not shown), training was not possible as severe nonlinearity resulted in the numerical weights decaying towards a constant value. Higher performance has been demonstrated using state of the art PCM devices11, where accuracy reached 82.2%, although still more than 10% lower than LISTA performs in a similar neural network on comparable data sets. In addition to high-performance, it is critical that a resistive memory device and corresponding architecture operate with a competitive energy efficiency when compared to CMOS. While there exist large energy efficiency gains inherent to a crossbar memory architecture, the device itself must also be energy efficient to provide an overall advantage. Currently, the reported energy cost per write operation for resistive memories varies by many orders of magnitude with the lowest predicted values approaching 0.1 fJ46, the lowest measured values of 115 fJ from a TaOx device47 and more typical values ranging from 1 – 25 pJ14. In order to be competitive with CMOS in backpropagation, the energy per write-operation must be lower than ~ 100 pJ, the typical efficiency of a floating point operation in a state-of-the-art computing system. However, an even lower energy cost is desirable and should approach 1 aJ in order to match the energy efficiency of the human brain48. In addition to energy efficiency, scalability of neuromorphic elements to the few tens of nanometers is also necessary to compete with CMOS, as well as scaling to high pulse rates. In order to understand the factors determining scalability, energy efficiency, and performance, we performed numerical simulations of the devices, including the Li-ion transport in the electrolyte and the two electrodes, and the electrochemical charge-transfer reactions at the LiPON/Li1-xCoO2 and LiPON/Si interfaces. The model follows the theoretical approach developed for solid-state batteries presented by Danilov et al.49, implemented using a commercial differential equation solver. To benchmark the model, we used a Si/LiPON/Li1-xCoO2 2-electrode system (essentially a battery) and measured its potential under current cycling. Figure 4a shows an example of the excellent agreement between the model and the data after adjusting the materials parameters. Our simulations indicate that the device behavior is governed primarily by the diffusion of Li in Li1-xCoO2, and by the reaction rate at the LiPON/ Li1-xCoO2 interface. A full description of the model can be found in the supplementary information (Section S2). 8

Since the LISTA device measures the Li1-xCoO2 conductivity, which in turn depends on the Li concentration in Li1-xCoO2, we extracted the Li concentration as a function of time. Figure 4b shows a simulated pulsing sequence for a device with the physical dimensions of the experimental LISTA device, where the Li concentration in Li1-xCoO2 increases for each current pulse, and recovers partially during the read phase, in agreement with experiment. By running the simulations for a scaled device (see Fig. 4b for LiPON and Li1-xCoO2 thicknesses of 50 nm) we surmise that the conductance modulation is maintained even at such small dimensions. Of course, other statistical effects will arise when only a few Li-ions are transferred, and additional energy will be dissipated during the read phase and to charge the interconnects. Still, the favorable physical and energy scaling of LISTA-type devices is promising for future neuromorphic applications. In conclusion, we have demonstrated a new type of neuromorphic device that satisfies the requirements for an energy efficient and reliable neuromorphic architecture, with low-noise, high write linearity, low-energy operation and scalability to dense arrays. The performance improvements over existing devices lies primarily in the diffusion driven, structurally-stable resistance switching mechanism of Li-ion intercalation. Similar switching phenomena using alkali metal ions or protons can be implemented in other material systems that exhibit oxidation state dependent electronic conductivity such as numerous transition metal oxides and semiconducting polymers. Our results suggest an alternative direction for developing neuromorphic architectures based upon a new class of resistive memory devices that operate with highly mobile ions acting as dopants.

Neural network simulations The performance of a neural network based on backpropagation was simulated using the measured properties of a LISTA device. Backpropagation is one of the most numerically demanding and energy inefficient neuromorphic algorithms used in a CMOS architecture, as each synaptic weight collects small updates from thousands of examples. During these updates, data movement between processing and storage is limited by bus size and is energetically costly (over 100 of pJ per bit to access main memory). Therefore, backpropagation is an algorithm where a neural architecture has a clear potential for performance gains in terms of energy and computational efficiency. To simulate backpropagation, a two-layer network (one hidden layer) was used, as shown in Fig. 3a. The architecture used for simulations has been described previously5. For simplicity, a single network configuration was considered, but more generally there may be a optimizable tradeoff between network size and noise. Using this scheme, three different data sets were analyzed as summarized in Table S3.1. 1st 2nd training test Layer Layer data set network size examples examples Weight Weight Range Range UCI Small images 3,823 1,797 64×36×10 ±1.31 ±2.90 File types 4,501 900 256×512×9 ±0.33 ±0.66 MNIST large images 60,000 10,000 784×300×10 ±0.33 ±1.58 Table S3.1 | A table of the data sets used for back propagation simulations with relevant simulation parameters. Each layer of the network was mapped onto a simulated crossbar. The crossbar was used to perform two key operations, a vector matrix multiply, and parallel rank 1 outer product update, as described in Ref.6. Different analog updates are applied to each device by varying both the length of the update and either the voltage or current of the update pulse. Here, it is approximated that the magnitude of the weight update is linear in time, current and voltage, based upon the measured linearity in Fig S3.1. This means that the time, current or voltage can be linearly varied to linearly modify the amount of conductance change, ΔG. Sigmoid neurons with a slope of 1, a learning rate of 0.1 are used, and the weights are initialized following the scheme in Ref.7.

The circuit for programming (c) a single device and (d) for a full crossbar is shown The three terminal LISTA device can be arranged into a crossbar architecture as illustrated in Fig. S3.2 and Fig. S3.3 for voltage controlled and current controlled programming, respectively. In order to do a parallel update on a crossbar, the state programmed should be the product of a signal along the rows and a signal along the columns. The product is achieved by changing the voltage or current on the gate and the length of the pulse on the source. As both the LISTA device and the access devices can be fabricated in the back end of the line, the crossbar can be densely integrated with multiple crossbars stacked on top of each other, as with ReRAM memory. For voltage controlled operation (Fig. S3.2), the read path is indicated by black arrows and the write path is indicated by blue arrows. In order to read, voltages are applied on the rows and currents are read from the columns. The transpose operation (apply voltages on the columns, and read currents on the rows) is also possible. Voltage controlled programming does not require additional transistors per cell. For current controlled operation (Fig. S3.3), the current mirrors are split so that only two transistors are required on the gate of each LISTA device, while the other two transistors are only needed once per column. The transpose read operation (apply voltages on the columns, and read currents on the rows) is also possible, but not shown. In order to control the current into the transistor, two current mirrors are required, one for adding current and one for removing current. The LISTA device is programmed by the magnitude of the current source. The programming time is controlled by the width of the pulse between the two current mirrors.

The LISTA crossbars can be used as part of a neural core, as illustrated in Fig S3.4. The neural core represents a single layer of a neural network and is used to accelerate the key matrix operations 6 while a digital core can be used to process the inputs and outputs to the crossbar. This allows a N×N crossbar to accelerate O(N2) operations, while only O(N) operations (the inputs and outputs) are processed in digital. In order to simulate a system based on the LISTA device, we simulated the nonlinearity and write noise represented in Fig 2(b-e). We also simulated finite A/D and D/A precision. The first step in mapping backpropagation to the analog hardware was to map the LISTA device’s 160 µS to 246 µS conductance range to numerical weights that the algorithm can use. As illustrated in Fig. S3.4, a reference conductance can be subtracted from each weight in analog so that negative weights can be represented. As the physical device has a min and a max conductance, the network has a min and max weights it can store. Choosing the weight range correctly is important to optimize the usage of the resistive memory’s dynamic range while minimizing nonlinearity. Consequently, we follow the method in ref. 8 to choose the weight range. The range for each dataset and neural net layer is given in Table S3.1. Due to LISTA’s extremely low noise, we found that mapping the weights to the center 20% of the conductance range (195 to 210 µS) improved the accuracy by concentrating the weights in the center portion of the conductance range where the average conductance change ΔG is nearly constant for different initial starting states. During training a few weights go outside the targeted center 20% range, but most stay in the targeted range.

The inputs to the crossbar are converted from digital to analog and the outputs are converted back from analog to digital. Next, we describe the method by which A/D’s and D/A’s drive and read the crossbar. In order to read the crossbar, input vectors are converted to variable length pulses proportional to the magnitude of each element. The resulting current is integrated at the output op-amps. The inputs along the rows come from a previous layer of sigmoid neurons which have outputs in a 0 to 1 range. The initial input data is also normalized to be centered at zero and have a standard deviation of 0.5. Given this, the row D/A inputs can be clipped to a -1 to 1 range without losing accuracy. The column outputs feed into a sigmoid slope of 1. Consequently, clipping the column outputs to +/-6 is sufficient to saturate the sigmoid. During training, the transpose operation (inputs along columns and outputs along rows) is also needed. Here the inputs and outputs can be clipped to +/-1 and +/-4 respectively, without losing accuracy. For all these A/D’s and D/A’s we quantize the inputs to 8 bits (7 bits + sign bit). During backpropagation, the crossbar is updated by the outer product of two vectors. The columns are driven by output of the previous layers’ neurons. This means the output will be in a 0 to 1 range. The rows will be driven by the product of the learning rate, α, the derivative of a sigmoid, and a backpropagated error. The sigmoid derivative has a maximum value of 0.25 and the backpropagated error was already clipped to +/-4. Consequently, the column update will fall between +/- α 0.25 4 = +/-α. In practice the updates end up being much smaller and so clipping the update to a 10X smaller range, +/-0.1α, provides better accuracy for a given device dynamic range. In analog, the columns will be driven by a pulse of varying voltage or varying current, while the rows will be driven by a pulse of varying time. As it is more efficient to design a D/A that accurately converts inputs to varying pulse lengths than voltage levels, we quantize the row input to 7 bits (6 bits + sign bit) while quantizing the column input to 5 bits (4 bits + sign bit). Given the clipped and quantized row and column updates, we can find the weight change calculated by the learning rule for each weight. Using the experimental ΔG vs G plots from Fig. 2 (b-e), we can to determine how much the mean update will be and how much noise to add to that update given the update requested by the algorithm. The plot in Fig. 2(b) is generated for only a single pulse size that has a mean update around 0.5 µS, with the exact mean value slightly higher or lower depending on the initial conductance, Go. For a particular training example, a different update, perhaps 0.1 µS may be desired. To find the update, first we determine what state, Go, the device is in, find the average update at Go from Fig 2(b) and sample a noise value from the probability distribution at Go in Fig 2(b). Then we scale the mean value and the noise value according to the desired 0.1 µS update. For instance, if G o=170 µS, the mean update is 0.3 µS. Sampling from the probability distribution at G0, we might get an update of 0.35 µS, giving a write noise of 0.05 µS that was added to the mean update of 0.3 µS.Since all the updates are done in parallel, we can only use a single constant scaling factor for all the updates. The average update over the conductance range is 0.53 µS, while we wanted an update of 0.1 µS. Consequently, the mean update of 0.3 µS at G0=170 µS should be scaled by 0.1/0.53 giving a new mean update of 0.057 µS. (The average update of 0.53 µS was only averaged over the center 20% of the conductance range, 195 µS to 210 µS, as the system was designed to have most of the weights be in the center half of Fig 2b). The noise cannot simply be scaled by the same constant scale factor. Typically, the noise increases relative to the size of the update as the update gets smaller. This can be understood by thinking of the noise as a random walk. If we use one pulse or two pulses to change the conductance from one state to another, the noise will be the same as long as the average initial and final states are the same. This implies that one longer or higher voltage pulse will cause the same physical change as two sequential pulses that end at the same average conductance. Hence, we assume the noise is the same for a given ΔG regardless of the number of pulses required to get a given ΔG, and therefore ΔG must be proportional to the variance of the noise, σ2. After multiple pulses, the variance of the noise in each pulse is additive. Therefore, 𝜎 ∝ √∆𝑔. This means that the noise should be multiplied by the square root of the scaling factor. Thus the 0.05 µS of noise is scaled by √0.1/0.53giving a write noise of 0.022 µS. Thus the final update is the mean update of 0.057 µS + the write noise 0.022 µS, giving an update of 0.78 µS for the requested 0.1 µS updated.
