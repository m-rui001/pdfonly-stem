2. Molecular Phosphorescent Material Design

In theory, octahedral Ir complexes will have more flexibility of ligand design than square, planar Pt complexes due to more coordination sites inside the metal complexes.[11-13] This should result in more molecular configurations and more degrees of freedom in the material design principles. However, Ir complexes cyclometalated with tridentate ligands or tetradentate ligands are sparsely reported due to either synthetic challenges or less desirable photophysical properties, as is the case for Ir(dpyx)(dppy), where dpyx is dipyridyl-xylene and dppy is di-phenyl-pyridine.[13] Thus, the research on Ir-based phosphorescent materials has focused on the class of cyclometalated Ir complexes employing bidentate ligands, varying among homoleptic metal complexes such as facIr(ppy)3 or heteroleptic metal complexes such as fac-Ir(ppz)2(ppy) or Ir(ppy)2(acac), where ppy is phenyl-pyridine, ppz is phenyl-pyrazole, and acac is acetyl-acetonate.[11,12] These complexes have demonstrated photoluminescence (PL) quantum yields close to unity and a short luminescent

Efficient phosphorescent metal complexes emitting in the green and red spectral regions have been well developed and incorporated into efficient and stable OLEDs.[4] On the other hand, blue phosphorescent materials and their devices have demonstrated much less success, with each performance metric (color, efficiency, and stability) being progressively more challenging. Achieving any appreciable deep-blue emission from phosphorescent complexes at room temperature is comparatively rare; efficient devices emitting in the blue region are even more scarce, and blue devices with long operational lifetimes are virtually non-existent at this moment.[20] There are many potential reasons for this deficiency, the most fundamental of which is the competition between the population of desirable radiative excited states and nonradiative states that reduce emission efficiency and can result in molecular dissociation.[21] As the emission energy increases, the radiative processes increasingly compete with thermally accessible nonradiative pathways such as metalcentered (MC) transitions or the thermal relaxation of “soft” ligands, which distort the geometry of the excited-state molecule relative to its ground-state form.[22] This competition is at the crux of current efforts in molecular design which seeks to raise the excited-state energy level while kinetically and thermodynamically suppressing the involvement of these nonradiative pathways. 3.3. Narrow-Band Phosphorescent Emitters In many ways, phosphorescent metal complexes are ideal emissive materials. They can exhibit deepblue emission, have short luminescent lifetimes and high photoluminescence quantum yield, and are compatible with OLED device structures and fabrication techniques. However, one major drawback is that they exhibit relatively broad emission spectra. This poses a number of challenges, including compromising color purity in displays, reducing outcoupling efficiency in resonant microcavities, and lowering the luminous efficacy of white OLEDs.[57] Thus, to fully realize the benefits of phosphorescent materials, it is necessary to improve their spectral purity. It is well documented that the emission of phosphorescent materials originates from the lowest triplet state with varying degrees of 3LC and 1,3MLCT characters. Emitters with primarily 3MLCT character show broad Gaussian-like emission, whereas emitters with dominant 3LC character show strong vibronic features. However, until recently, no class of materials could achieve such a combination of properties. Fluorescent materials, which lack a heavy metal center and do not undergo efficient intersystem crossing, can have a low triplet energy since they emit rapidly directly from the lowest singlet excited state. However, fluorescent materials are unable to efficiently harvest electrogenerated triplet excitons, and therefore have low internal electron-to-photon conversion efficiencies. On the other hand, phosphorescent materials are capable of harvesting 100% of electrogenerated excitons but require a high triplet energy to achieve blue emission.[3] Recently, reports of thermally activated delayed fluorescence (TADF) demonstrate certain classes of organic materials that are capable of harvesting triplet excitons through thermally activated intersystem crossing from the lowest triplet state to the singlet excited state and subsequent fluorescence.[9] The energy difference between the singlet excited state and the lowest triplet state allows higher-energy emission from TADF materials with a low triplet energy, yet the thermal equilibrium dictates that some fraction of excitons will exist as triplets that have no radiative pathway and will relax nonradiatively. The dual-channel emission process allows excitons to be thermally promoted to the higherenergy singlet state and efficiently emit via TADF while the remaining triplet excitons can emit via the available efficient phosphorescent pathway. Single-Doped White OLEDs An interesting consequence of the square, planar molecular geometry of Pt complexes is the tendency for many of these emitters to form either dimers or excimers.[65] The former is an aggregate species that exists in the ground state and consequently may serve as a sink for charges and excitons. Thus, dimers often lead to significant losses in efficiency and poor control over emission characteristics.[66] Excimers, however, exist only as excited-state species and lack groundstate absorption. Thus, these lower-energy emissive pairs can coexist with higher-energy monomers without significant energy transfer between them.[30-31] With judiciously designed emitters, it is possible to generate blue emission from individual molecules alongside broad yellow to red emission from excimers, resulting in a white-light generation based on a single emissive material. Conclusion In summary, square, planar platinum and palladium complexes have demonstrated the ability to serve as efficient, high-color-quality, and stable emitters for the development of next-generation OLED displays and solid-state lighting. The wide variety of potential configurations using bidentate, tridentate, or tetradentate ligands distinguishes Pt complexes from those of their octahedral Ir analogs, which, for the most part, depends on bidentate cyclometalating ligands.
