Electrochemical conversion of CO2 into liquid fuels, powered by renewable electricity, offers one means to address the need for the storage of intermittent renewable energy. Here we present a cooperative catalyst design of molecule:metal catalyst interfaces with the goal of producing a reaction-intermediate-rich local environment, which improves the electrosynthesis of ethanol from CO2 and H2O. We implement the strategy by functionalising the copper surface with a family of porphyrin-based metallic complexes that catalyse CO2 to CO. Using density functional theory calculations, in situ Raman and operando X-ray absorption spectroscopies, we find that the high concentration of local CO facilitates carbon–carbon coupling and steers the reaction pathway towards ethanol. We report a CO2-to-ethanol Faradaic efficiency of 41% and a partial current density of 124 mA cm–2 at –0.82 VRHE. We integrate the catalyst into a membrane electrode assembly- based system and achieve an overall energy efficiency of 13%. The renewable-electricity-powered CO2 reduction reaction (CO2RR) offers a means to store intermittent electricity as dispatchable fuels and valuable chemical feedstocks1,2. Among the various products (CO, formic acid, methane, ethylene, ethanol, 1-proponal) formed from CO2RR, ethanol – a liquid fuel or fuel additive – is desired because it is energy dense by volume, and because it levers extensive existing infrastructure for the storage and distribution of carbon- based fuels3,4. However, electrochemical CO2-to-ethanol conversion relies on multiple proton/electron transfers that involve multiple intermediates,5-7 making the development of more efficient electrocatalysts an important but also challenging problem. Cu-based heterogeneous materials are distinctive among metal catalysts in their ability to reduce CO2 to products with two or more carbon atoms (C2+). Ethylene has, in past reports, been favoured 2× – 4× over ethanol8-11. To steer the selectivity towards ethanol, the focus has been on

Page 4

the tuning of binding strength of reaction intermediates on Cu via doping using elements such as silver12,13, and through the creation of grain boundaries14 and vacancies15. These advances have increased the Faradaic efficiency (FE) towards ethanol to an impressive 30%. Technoeconomic assessment4,16 suggests that further progresses on FE, as well as on lowering the overpotential (today above 1 V at current densities above 10 mA cm–2), are urgently needed: indeed, it is desired to increase half-cell cathodic energy efficiencies (CEEs) from today’s ≤ 15%11-13,15,17-20 to > 20% and then beyond. Scaling relations account for the fact that binding strengths of different intermediates on the same site (i.e. Cu) are correlated, which means that optimising for one adsorbed species will typically take the other steps away from their optima21,22. We sought therefore to add a further degree of freedom in catalyst design that would influence predominantly one step. Specifically, we strove to increase the reaction rate of one step without strongly modulating the others – by judiciously increasing the coverage of one key intermediate, yet not interfering with the electronic structure (hence binding strength) of Cu – with the goal of circumventing the scaling relations. Cu-based bimetallic catalysts have been reported to generate, on the doping metal sites, high- concentration CO, a key intermediate along C2+ pathways23-25, to spill over to the Cu sites for improved carbon–carbon (C–C) coupling and further reduction to C2+ products20,26,27. However, the ratio of the heteroatom (Au, Ag, Zn) to Cu on the catalyst surface is low (for example, < 2% in the Au/Cu case27), resulting in a limited proportion of interfaces that offer local enrichment of intermediates. Further increasing this ratio modulates the electronic structure of Cu, and then reduces selectivity towards C2+ products27-29.

Page 5

Here we present instead a molecule:metal composite material in which the molecular adsorbate generates a high concentration of the key early intermediate, CO; yet does not modulate the metallic active sites germane to the crucial C–C coupling step. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations indicate that the increased coverage of CO on Cu – achieved by increasing its concentration near the Cu surface – not only decreases the reaction energy for the C–C coupling step, but also steers the selectivity from ethylene to ethanol. We implement this concept experimentally by functionalising a family of porphyrin-based metallic complexes capable of catalysing CO2RR to CO on the Cu surface. The near-unity coverage of the complexes maximises the local concentration of CO for ensuing reduction to ethanol on Cu sites. As a result of this strategy, we report CO2-to-ethanol conversion with an FE of 41% at a partial current density of 124 mA cm–2. We build a full-cell system, demonstrating the electrosynthesis of ethanol via CO2 reduction coupled to the water oxidation reaction, with a full-cell energy efficiency (EE) of 13%. DFT calculations We applied DFT calculations to predict the effect of local CO on the C–C coupling step. We first ascertained which type of CO (surface- bound vs. unbound) accounts for this effect on a Cu(111) surface (3 × 3 unit cell) – a facet suggested by micro kinetics studies30,31 to have low *CO coverages during electrocatalytic turnovers, offering us greater freedom to tune the coverage. We found that the presence of unbound CO does not affect the reaction energy of the coupling of two adsorbed *CO on the Cu surface (Supplementary Note 1, Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). Instead, the concentration of the unbound CO tunes the coverage of *CO on the Cu surface via Langmuir isotherm model and then changes the reaction energy of the C–C coupling step (Supplementary Note 1). We then introduced various additional *CO coverage onto the Cu(111) surface with two

Page 6

*CO already adsorbed and compared the reaction energy of the C–C coupling step (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Fig. 3). The reaction energy is lowered when additional *CO is introduced, and the reduction in energy increases with further increase in *CO coverage with a coverage of 3/9 showing the largest margin (Fig. 1b): the adsorbate–adsorbate interaction becomes more significant with the increase in surface coverage32. We further calculated reaction energies associated with the hydrogenation of the intermediate *CHCOH towards either *CHCHOH or *CCH (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Fig. 4), a step found by Goddard and co-workers25,33 critical to controlling the differential production of ethanol vs. ethylene. We found that – in the presence of *CO – the reaction energy decreases more for the formation of *CHCHOH (ethanol path) compared to that of *CCH (ethylene path). These reaction energies differentiate further with higher *CO coverage (Fig. 1c), indicating that high *CO coverage steers selectivity from ethylene to ethanol. We found that the coverage of *CO also tunes the energy profiles of reaction intermediates on the Cu(100) surface, though the changes are not as significant as those on the Cu(111) surface (Supplementary Figs. 5–7). Noting the Cu(111) is typically the dominant facet of polycrystalline Cu substrates – an approach commonly employed in the study of Cu-based catalysts – we based our calculations on Cu(111). CO2RR performance We pursued the intermediate enrichment strategy experimentally by introducing a molecular catalyst on the surface of Cu (Fig. 2a), one that we posited would increase local CO concentration via its own electrocatalytic turnovers. Molecular catalysts have achieved near- unity FE for CO, commonly in non-aqueous solutions34,35. Their immobilisation on conductive carbon substrates or covalent organic frameworks has enabled the translation of water-insoluble

Page 7

molecular catalysts to operate instead in aqueous solutions, and has enabled significant increases in the turnover frequency for CO production36-38. The use of molecular complexes is expected to create CO-rich interfaces via conformal contact with Cu surfaces, without affecting the CO2-to- C2+-active electronics of Cu. We used 5,10,15,20-tetraphenyl-21H,23H-porphine iron(III) chloride (FeTPP[Cl], Fig. 2b) – which exhibits a high FE for CO at low overpotentials in both homogeneous and immobilised forms35,39 – as the molecular complex. We first examined CO2-to-CO conversion of FeTPP[Cl] immobilised on carbon substrates via the π–π interaction37, studying it in a flow cell (Supplementary Fig. 8) with a gas diffusion electrode (GDE) and employing 1 M aqueous KHCO3 as the electrolyte (used throughout this study). The immobilised FeTPP[Cl] shows a clear increase of the reductive current under CO2 atmosphere compared to N2 atmosphere (linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) curves, Fig. 2c), indicating a catalytic response towards CO2RR. To evaluate quantitively the CO2RR ability of the FeTPP[Cl], we analysed the products using gas chromatography (GC). We found that the FE of CO is above 60% over a wide potential range with an onset potential (defined as the most positive potential at which CO product is identified by GC) of –0.41 V vs. the reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE, all potentials are with respect to this reference) (Fig. 2d), which matches with the applied potentials typical for CO2RR on Cu, thus promising sufficient CO supply for the ensuing C–C coupling step. We then immobilised the FeTPP[Cl] on a Cu electrode sputtered on a hydrophobic porous polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) substrate (Supplementary Fig. 9)10. The surface-functionalised FeTPP[Cl]/Cu assembly was sufficiently stable to carry out electrochemical studies over extended operating times under reductive applied potentials – a feature we attribute to the insoluble/hydrophobic nature40 of the FeTPP[Cl] molecule. The hydrophobic nature of the

Page 8

FeTPP[Cl] permits the use the air brushing to tune its loading amount on Cu. The calculated binding of CO to the FeTPP[Cl] is weaker compared to that to the Cu(111) substrate by a margin of 0.2 eV (Supplementary Fig. 10), suggesting that the locally produced CO on the FeTPP[Cl] sites is readily spilled over to the CO2-to-C2+ active Cu sites. The modification of the FeTPP[Cl] molecular layer does not block reactants to reach the Cu surface, which we confirmed using Raman spectroscopy and using water as the probe molecule (Supplementary Fig. 11). The morphology of Cu did not change, nor did we find the aggregation of FeTPP[Cl] to particles, when we investigated the materials using scanning electron microscopy, transmission electron microscopy, and energy-dispersive X-ray elemental mapping (Supplementary Fig. 12). We evaluated the CO2RR performance of the FeTPP[Cl]/Cu catalyst in the electrochemical flow cell system10. The catalyst shows an onset potential for ethanol at –0.42 V, at which potential we started to identify ethanol with the aid of 1H NMR spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. 13). In comparison, ethanol was detected in the case of the bare Cu electrode only when the applied potentials were more negative than –0.50 V (Fig. 3a). The selectivity of C2+ products including ethanol increases when the applied potentials become more negative, a finding we attribute to the increased electrochemical driving force30,41. The ethanol FE for the FeTPP[Cl]/Cu catalyst is higher than that for bare Cu electrode across the entire applied potential range and achieves a peak value of 41% at –0.82 V (Fig. 3a). In contrast, the peak ethanol FE for the bare Cu reaches only 29% at a more negative potential, –0.84 V. We confirmed, using labelled 13CO2, that the carbon source of ethanol is CO2 (Supplementary Fig. 14). The lower onset potential and higher FE result in higher ethanol current densities for the FeTPP[Cl]/Cu catalyst compared to those for pure Cu: indeed, greater-than-100 mA cm-2 was achieved (Fig. 3b). We ascribe this increase in reaction rate to the enhanced CO2-to-ethanol kinetics, in addition to much more

Page 9

abundant CO2/electrolyte/catalyst three-phase interfaces in the GDEs compared to traditional H- cells42. The low overpotentials and high ethanol FE result in high efficiency for ethanol production. The maximum half-cell CEE (see Methods for calculation details) for the FeTPP[Cl]/Cu catalyst is 23% (Fig. 3c), 1.4× higher than that for pure Cu under similar conditions (16%) and 1.5× higher than the value previously published for the case of neutral electrolyte20. The FeTPP[Cl]/Cu catalyst outperforms the reported catalysts in onset potential, overpotential, FE, partial current density and half-cell CEE (Fig. 3d, Supplementary Table 1). The CO2-to-ethanol activity normalised to the electrochemical surface area is also one order of magnitude higher than that in prior reports (Supplementary Table 1), in part benefitting from the flow cell we used herein. The use of neutral electrolyte circumvents the carbonate formation issue encountered in CO2RR electrolysers using alkaline electrolytes42. We further integrated the CO2 reduction and H2O oxidation reactions in a system employing a membrane electrode assembly (MEA, Supplementary Fig. 15) to carry out the full electrosynthesis of ethanol from CO2 and water via the reaction: 2CO2 + 3H2 O → C2 H5 OH + 3O2 Eo = 1.14 V (1) We operated the system for an initial 12 hours at a full-cell voltage of 3.7 V. The system delivered a stable current of 0.6 A and an average ethanol FE of 41% (Fig. 3e). Thus, we obtained a full-cell EE (see Methods for calculation details) of 13%. This value is reported without the benefit of ohmic resistance (iR) corrections. We observed a decrease of ethanol selectivity after the initial 12 hours (Supplementary Fig. 16), which we attributed to the mass loss of the FeTPP[Cl] molecules on the Cu substrate (Supplementary Note 2, Supplementary Figs. 17–18). Nevertheless, we observed no structural

damage of the FeTPP[Cl] molecules (such as decomposition to nanoparticles) following electrolysis when we studied the catalyst using UV-vis absorption and X-ray absorption spectroscopies (Supplementary Fig. 18). Mechanistic investigations To investigate the origins of the improved ethanol selectivity of FeTPP[Cl]/Cu, we reported the combined FE of major C2 products (ethanol + ethylene) against applied potential (Fig. 4a). We found that the combined C2 FE for the FeTPP[Cl]/Cu catalyst is higher than that for the pure Cu electrode. Further analysis of the product distribution (Supplementary Fig. 19) indicates that the C2 selectivity increases mainly at the expense of CO. We further compared the ratio of ethanol FE to ethylene FE. We found that, across the entire applied potential range, the FeTPP[Cl]/Cu electrode favours ethanol over ethylene (Fig. 4a): The ratio of ethanol to ethylene increases from around 0.5 for pure Cu to around 1 for the FeTPP[Cl]/Cu. The ratio is also larger than the values previously reported for Cu catalysts (Supplementary Table 1). These results are consistent with DFT calculations, in which high CO coverage favours the ethanol pathway over the ethylene pathway. To explore whether the local high-concentration CO directly contributes to the enhanced ethanol selectivity, we modified the Cu electrode with the molecule NiTPP, whose CO2-to-CO conversion onset potential is more negative than that of the FeTPP[Cl] molecule (Supplementary Fig. 20). We found that only after the NiTPP started to generate CO did the ethanol FE show significant improvement on the NiTPP/Cu catalyst (Supplementary Fig. 20). This agrees with the view that improved ethanol selectivity arises from the local CO enrichment. We further investigated the role of the FeTPP[Cl] molecule by varying its loading on the Cu substrate. We found that both the combined C2 FE and the ratio of ethanol to ethylene gradually

Page 11

increase against the surface coverage (quantification details in Supplementary Note 3, Supplementary Fig. 21) of the FeTPP[Cl] on the Cu and reach the maximum values when the coverage is near 1. This trend suggests that the locally concentrated CO provided by the FeTPP[Cl] indeed enhances the ethanol selectivity in CO2RR. A further increase of the FeTPP[Cl] coverage leads to decreased ethanol selectivity, which we account for by noting that too few Cu sites are now available for C–C coupling, in agreement with the observed increased CO FE. We highlight here that the additional layer of molecular catalyst on Cu allows the ratio of *CO-producing sites to C–C coupling sites to approach unity. In contrast, the heteroatom doping strategy is limited to an analogous ratio of approximate 0.02 in the Au/Cu bimetallic catalyst27. We note also that the *CO-enrichment strategy is expected to substantially leave the electronic properties of Cu unchanged, in contrast with the elemental alloying approach29. We carried out control studies to examine whether ethanol selectivity is influenced by the porphyrin framework itself. Using in situ Raman spectroscopy, we found that the TPP framework does not tune the binding properties of the Cu substrate to CO (Supplementary Fig. 22). We found no enhancement of ethanol selectivity for the TPP/Cu electrode (Fig. 4b). These observations agree with the main role for the FeTPP[Cl] complex as CO2-to-CO conversion enhancer. This view is further supported by the fact that a family of CO2-to-CO catalytically active porphyrin-based complexes with different ligand structures and cobalt centres also show enhanced performance for CO2-to-ethanol (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Fig. 23). The range of molecular enhancers demonstrated here showcases the wider applicability of the homogeneous– heterogeneous cooperative strategy in reprogramming CO2RR. Taking these experimental findings together with the DFT calculations, we conclude that the functional complexes in this study generate a CO-rich environment at the molecule:metal

Page 12

interface, increasing the coverage of *CO on the Cu surface. This lowers the barrier to C–C coupling and steers selectivity towards ethanol, resulting in increased control over CO2RR. To gain molecular-level insight into the cooperative role of the heterogenised FeTPP[Cl] and Cu substrate, we interrogated the FeTPP[Cl]/Cu surface using in situ Raman spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. 24). We acquired Raman spectra on both Cu and FeTPP[Cl]/Cu electrodes at a range of applied potentials from the open circuit potential (OCP) to –0.57 V (Figs. 4c,d). Under negative applied potentials, the peaks associated with surface copper oxide (CuOx) disappear, a finding we attribute to the reduction of the oxide to metallic Cu. On bare Cu electrode, three bands are identified associated with surface-adsorbed *CO at 280, 365 and the range of 1900 – 2130 cm–1 (Fig. 4c). These bands emerge at –0.45 V and correspond to the Cu–CO frustrated rotation, the Cu–CO stretch, and the C ≡ O stretch, respectively43,44. On the FeTPP[Cl]/Cu catalyst, the same bands emerge at an earlier onset potential of –0.41 V (Fig. 4d); this is consistent with the notion that CO concentration is enhanced on the FeTPP[Cl]/Cu electrode. We also observed an emerging band at 535 cm–1, which we attribute to the Fe–CO bending vibration due to the interaction between CO and Fe in iron porphyrin segment45-47. This finding supports the view that immobilised FeTPP[Cl] produces CO, which is then converted to C2+ products on a nearby Cu site, as evidenced by the Cu–CO feature and by the fact that the FE of CO on FeTPP[Cl]/Cu does not increase compared to that on bare Cu at similar applied potentials. This view agrees with the picture of cooperative electrocatalytic CO2-to-ethanol conversion by the heterogenised FeTPP[Cl] on the Cu substrate in Fig. 2a. We confirmed, carrying out Ar and 13CO2 controls (Supplementary Fig. 25), that the Raman peaks corresponding to Cu–CO and Fe–CO as discussed above are indeed from CO2RR.

Page 13

The band at 535 cm–1 is ruled out as from CuOx by operando X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS, vide infra). We investigated further the chemical structure and coordinating environment of FeTPP[Cl] under operating conditions using operando XAS. The operando Fe K-edge X-ray absorption near edge structure (XANES) spectra of the FeTPP[Cl]/Cu show that the central Fe ions of the FeTPP[Cl] molecules are slightly reduced under catalytic turnover conditions (Fig. 4e). Using linear combination fitting, we calculated the average oxidation state of Fe to be ~ +1.7 – +1.8 under operating conditions (Supplementary Fig. 26), consistent with the reported mechanism for the electrochemical CO2-to-CO conversion by FeTPP[Cl], where the porphyrin-coordinated Fe2+, Fe1+ and Fe0 are supposed to be intermediate species48,49. We also observed a higher pre-edge feature compared to that for the dry sample, a finding suggesting a more distorted structure by coordination of the metal centre with CO2RR intermediates50. We examined the Fe K-edge extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) spectra of the FeTPP[Cl]/Cu catalyst. Over the applied potential range relevant to CO2RR, we identified only the Fe–N bond while the Fe– Fe bond was not observed (Fig. 4f). This finding suggests that the FeTPP[Cl] maintains its original complex status instead of being reduced to iron nanoparticles or nanoclusters under operating conditions. The stability of the FeTPP[Cl] molecule is further supported by its reversibility following electrolysis: the oxidation state of Fe recovers to the same value as that before reaction, and there is no Fe–Fe bond formation – findings we obtained examining the post-electrolysis catalyst using XAS (Supplementary Fig. 18). With the aid of both Cu K-edge XANES and EXAFS spectroscopies, we confirmed that Cu remains in the metallic state, and that the porphyrin does not coordinate with Cu under catalytically active conditions (Supplementary Fig. 27).

Conclusions In summary, we present an intermediate-enrichment-enhanced electrocatalytic CO2-to-ethanol conversion strategy by cooperative catalysis at the molecule:metallic interface. Using this strategy, implemented using a family of porphyrin-based metallic complexes on a Cu catalyst, we reported a CO2-to-ethanol conversion with an ethanol FE of 41% at a partial current density of 124 mA cm–2 in neutral media. We achieved a full-cell EE of 13% for the electrosynthesis of ethanol from CO2 and water. The findings suggest a wider strategy for improving CO2 conversion into value-added liquid fuels using renewable electricity with the aid of cooperative effects between adsorbed molecular and heterogeneous approaches. Methods DFT calculations. All the DFT calculations in this work were carried out with a periodic slab model using the Vienna ab initio simulation program (VASP)51-54. The generalised gradient approximation (GGA) was used with the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE)55 exchange-correlation functional. The projector-augmented wave (PAW) method56,57 was utilised to describe the electron-ion interactions, and the cut-off energy for the plane-wave basis set was 450 eV. In order to illustrate the long-range dispersion interactions between the adsorbates and catalysts, we employed the D3 correction method by Grimme et al.58. Brillouin zone integration was accomplished using a 3×3×1 Monkhorst-Pack k-point mesh. All the adsorption geometries were optimised using a force-based conjugate gradient algorithm. For the modelling of Cu(111) and Cu(100), the crystal structure was optimised; Cu(111) and Cu(100) were modelled with a periodic four-layer p(3×3) model with the 2 lower layers fixed and 2 upper layers relaxed. FeTPP[Cl]/Cu(111) was modelled with a periodic three-layer p(9×9) model with the 1 lower

layer fixed and 2 upper layers relaxed. The atomic coordinates of the optimised models are provided in Supplementary Data 1. Electrode preparation. All chemicals were purchased from Sigma Aldrich and used without further purification. The PTFE electrode was prepared by sputtering 200 nm Cu onto a piece of PTFE membrane (pore size of 450 nm, with polypropylene support on backside) using a pure Cu target (99.99%) at a sputtering rate of 0.67 Å s–1. The porphyrin-based complexes were dissolved in a mixture of tetrahydrofuran and acetonitrile (6:4 by volume) at a concentration of 0.1 mM. The complexes were then sprayed coated on either GDE (Sigracet, Fuel Cell Store) or the PTFE electrode. The nominal concentration was estimated based on the concentration of solution used for spray coat and the true loading concentration was quantified by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS, Supplementary Fig. 21). The optimised loading of the FeTPP[Cl] molecules for the GDE was ~5× higher than that for the Cu/PTFE electrode due to larger surface area of the GDE. CO2RR and product analysis. Most CO2RR measurements were conducted in an electrochemical flow cell setup (details in Supplementary Fig. 8). The applied potentials were converted to the RHE scale with iR correction through the following equation: ERHE = Evs. Ag/AgCl + 0.059 × pH + 0.210 + iR (2) where i is the current at each applied potential and R is the equivalent series resistance measured via electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) in the frequency range of 105 – 0.1 Hz with an amplitude of 10 mV. The MEA (details in Supplementary Fig. 15) system was used to carry out the full electrosynthesis of ethanol. The full-cell voltage was gradually increased from 3 V to 3.7 V and kept constant over the electrolysis course.
