Abstract: Tremendous efforts have been made to design the cathode of Li-S batteries for the improvement on their energy density and cycling life. However, challenges remain in achieving fast electronic and ionic transport while accommodating the significant cathode volumetric change, especially for the cathode with a high practical mass loading. Here we report a cathode architecture, which is constructed by burning lithium foils in a CS2 vapor. The obtained structure features crystalline Li2S nanoparticles wrapped by few-layer graphene (Li2S@graphene nanocapsules). Because of the improvement on the volumetric efficiency for accommodating sulfur active species and electrical properties, the cathode design enables promising electrochemical performance. More notably, at a loading of 10 mgLi2S cm−2, the electrode exhibits a high reversible capacity of 1160 mAh g−1S, namely, an area capacity of 8.1 mAh cm−2. Li2S@graphene cathode demonstrates a great potential for Li-ion batteries, where the Li2S@graphene-Cathode/Graphite-Anode cell delivers a high capacity of 730 mAh g−1S as well as stable cycle performance.

Introduction The lithium−Sulfur (Li-S) battery represents one of the most promising solutions to the range anxiety of electric vehicles due to its extremely high theoretical energy density of 2600 Wh kg−1, three to five fold higher than the state-of-the-art Li-ion batteries (LIBs) (1-8). Commercialization of Li-S batteries, however, has been hindered by a series of obstacles (915). The insulating nature of sulfur (S8) and lithium (di)sulfides (Li2S and Li2S2), the fully charged and discharged products of sulfur electrodes, respectively, requires heavy dosages of conductive additives. Meanwhile, lithium polysulfides (Li2Sx, 3 ≤ x ≤ 8) (LiPS), the cycling intermediates that are highly soluble in aprotic liquid electrolytes, act as internal redox shuttles that lead to the low Coulombic efficiency and rapid capacity fading of the cell. In addition, sulfur electrodes experience significant volumetric changes, with an expansion up to 180% when the denser S8 is reduced to Li2S during discharge. In an attempt to tackle the above problems, the primary approach has so far been to infiltrate sulfur into nanostructured conductors, where both electronic and ionic conductivity are enhanced. In these sulfur-containing nanocomposites, some empty space is usually preserved to accommodate the volume expansion of the sulfur species. Tremendous progress has been made towards this direction; however, there are some intrinsic drawbacks of this approach (1417). Firstly, the open porosity of nanostructured conductors is necessary to infiltrate sulfur during preparation; yet, the open porosity also causes difficulty of retaining solvated LiPS species inside the electrodes during cycling. Secondly, the preserved empty space in the composites may not be efficiently utilized by the expansion of active species since the precipitation of solvated LiPS into Li2S2 or Li2S is often not controllable. Furthermore, most nanostructured sulfur electrodes severely suffer from their fluffiness, it is difficult to fabricate dense and thick electrodes with these materials on metal foil current collectors with the current manufacturing technologies. Indeed, there has been some tradeoff between the high mass loading and excellent electrochemical properties, which constitutes a bottleneck for translating fundamental discoveries to practical applications. Consequently, modest mass loadings, i.e., less than 2 mg cm−2 have been ubiquitous. To address all above technical challenges, alternative solutions must be pursued for sulfur electrode architectures. One approach could be forming capsules of small Li2S particles (instead of sulfur) tightly encompassed by a very thin conducting layer, such as graphene, as the active cathode material, which bears several intrinsic advantages. First, as the least dense phase of the sulfur-containing species, no volume expansion is expected for the Li2S-based composites

during cycling (18-20). This renders the electrode structure stable during cycling, thus enhancing cycling life. Second, benefiting from its compactness and much enhanced conductivity, such a composite would allow for high mass loading—one of the key factors for practical applications of Li-S batteries. In addition, a Li2S cathode can be coupled with nonlithium-metal anodes, which could avoid the dendrite problem and low Coulombic efficiency issue of lithium metal anode (21-23). However, formation of a superior Li2S/conductor composites has been a non-trivial task because Li2S does not melt below 900 °C, exhibits very low solubility in organic solvents, and is very sensitive to moisture. Therefore, conventional approaches to infiltrate sulfur into nanostructured conductors has been unsuccessful for making a Li2S/conductor composite. It’s interesting to notice that when burning Mg in dry ice of CO2, a typical chemistry textbook reaction, a nanocomposite can be formed with graphene nanocages tightly wrapping MgO nanoparticles. (24, 25), Inspired by these findings, we report in this work a Li2S/graphene nanocapsule configuration design. In this approach, we burn Li foil in CS2 vapor (carried by Argon) so that both Li2S nanocrystals and graphene layers are generated simultaneously, which allows the graphene layers to encompass Li2S nanocrystals (referred to Li2S@graphene). This reaction is thermodynamically highly favorable, as at 923 K, the Gibbs free energy (ΔG) is calculated to be −796.95 kJ mol−1 (See detailed calculation for ΔG in Supplementary Table 1). We conduct the reaction by flowing Argon through room-temperature CS2 liquid to a tube furnace, where a piece of lithium foil is heated molten at 923 K. The resulting product is directly used for characterization and electrochemical measurements without any further treatment. As a result, the obtained materials exhibit unique structural and electrochemical properties, including compact structural integrity, good electrical conductivity, low activation barrier, and remarkable electrochemical performance.

Characterization of Li2S@graphene We first characterized the atomic and nanometric structures of the Li2S@graphene by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and selected area electron diffraction (SAED) (Figs. 1A−C). Li2S@graphene adopts an “ice cube chocolate” structure, where the “chocolate” is Li2S nanocrystals, and the tight wrapper is few-layer graphene. The Li2S@graphene particles, sized of 50−80 nm, have a rhombic shape, where the crystalline Li2S cores display highly ordered 3

Page 4

lattice fringes. A contour coating spanning about 10 to 20 graphene layers is observed around the Li2S core, thus forming a capsule-like nanostructure (Fig. 1A and inset). Both the SAED pattern (Fig. 1B) and low-dose high resolution TEM image (Fig. 1C) confirm the nearly-single crystalline nature of the Li2S cores. SEM images (Supplementary Fig. 1) reveal that the resulting Li2S@graphene comprise micron-sized secondary particles due to the agglomeration of Li2S@graphene nanoparticles. This finding confirms that the composite can achieve a high active mass loading for electrode preparation. High-energy X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns (Supplementary Fig. 2A) reveal the cubic phase of Li2S cores (space group: Fm3m) (26). Raman spectra (Supplementary Fig. 2B) display an intense G band and a weak D band, at 1585 and 1357 cm−1, respectively, with a large IG/ID band ratio. Note that the G band is attributed to the E2g phonon, which is proportional to the number of sp2-bonded carbon, whereas the D band is from the A1g breathing mode of six-carbon rings, which is only activated when defects exist (27, 28). Typically, a strong broad 2D band at 2680 cm−1 indicates that the wrapping carbon layers are few-layer graphene but not single-layer graphene or graphite (29). In addition, the Raman results reveal the vibrations of Li2S in Li2S@graphene with a characteristic T2g band at 373 cm−1, the same as commercially available bulk Li2S (30, 31). The chemical compositions of Li2S@graphene are further confirmed by energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDXS) and electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS). According to the reaction stoichiometry (1), Li2S is calculated to be ~ 88.5 wt% in the nanocapsules. The EDXS result (Supplementary Fig. 2C) confirms such a high active mass percentage, where the Li2S:C ratio is determined to be 88:12 by weight. The EELS result (Supplementary Fig. 2D) further confirms the presence of Li element in the Li2S@graphene composite. The most significant advantage of this structure is its extreme compactness, where nearly every bit of the available space underneath the graphene wrapping is occupied by Li2S nanoparticles. As Li2S is the least dense form of the active species throughout the battery cycling, the capsules allow for the maximum expansion. The compact, uniform, and seemingly complete graphene coating will help confine active materials in the nanocapsules during cycling.

Electrochemical Performance of Li2S@graphene We evaluated the electrochemical performance of Li2S@graphene cathode by using both lithium metal (half-cell) and graphite (full-cell) as anode. Bare Li2S electrodes were also tested in the same fashion for comparison. Specific capacity values of all the composite electrodes were calculated based on the mass of sulfur. In addition, we evaluated the impact of active mass 4

Page 5

loading levels (from 2 to 10 mg cm−2) on cell performance. Fig. 2A shows the typical voltage profiles of the Li2S@graphene/Li cells with a high Li2S loading of 10 mg cm−2. The cell delivers an initial charge capacity of 1600 mAh g−1 when being charged to 3.5 V, corresponding to nearly complete delithiation of Li2S. In the following discharge, it delivers a capacity of 1160 mAh g−1, with an initial Coulombic efficiency of 72.5%. An activation phenomenon of Li2S is observed upon the first charging, where the voltage quickly rises to 2.74 V, then declines to 2.6 V, followed by a long charge process near 2.8 V. In general, a pristine Li2S electrode must overcome a large over-potential barrier at the beginning of delithiation (charging), where the outer surface layer of solid Li2S is oxidized into short-chain PS species. These PS species are further oxidized into longer-chain PS that can also oxidize solid Li2S through a so-called comproportionation reaction (32). Thus, the PS species act as redox mediators to facilitate the activation of internal Li2S. Comparatively, in the case of the bare Li2S electrode (Supplementary Fig. 3A), the initial barrier is much higher due to larger charge-transfer resistance in delithiating Li2S due to the poor electronic conductivity and long Li-ion diffusion paths. Others have also observed this similar activation phenomenon for Li2S or composite electrodes (Supplementary Fig. 4) (18, 23, 31, 33-38). To the best of our knowledge, all prior reported Li2S-based electrodes show activation barriers of longer activation plateaus with higher voltages (corresponding to the low Coulombic efficiency values) than that of Li2S@graphene. The above results demonstrate that this Li2S@graphene nanocapsule structure enhances the electronic conductivity and lowers the charge-transfer resistance of the electrode. After activation during the first charge, the high over-potentials are not observed over the subsequent cycles. The charge-discharge profiles become very similar to those when employing elemental sulfur cathodes. The second cycle delivers a charge capacity of 1103 mAh g−1 when being charged to 3.0 V, while the following discharge capacity maintains at 1047 mAh g−1. The third cycle delivers a charge capacity of 1063 mAh g−1 and a discharge capacity of 988 mAh g−1. In addition, cyclic voltammograms (CV) (Fig. 2C) show high electrochemical reversibility at different scan rates, consistent with galvanostatic charge-discharge profiles (Fig. 2A). A broad oxidation peak with an onset potential at ~ 2.7 V is observed in the first anodic scan, which corresponds to the aforementioned initial activation process of Li2S@graphene. Then two obvious reduction peaks can be seen with peak potentials at 2.25 and 2.0 V, corresponding to the transitions of sulfur/high-order PS to low-order PS and then to the end discharge product Li2S, respectively. The following anodic sweep accompanies a broad peak centered at 2.45 V with a shoulder peak at 2.55 V, which is believed to be a feature of confined active materials (23). In addition, as the scan rate increases, the curves show no obvious peak shift, as well as 5

Page 6

no noticeable peak decrease when at the same scan rate, indicating the excellent electrochemical stability of the nanocapsule structure. It is noteworthy that a relatively rapid capacity fading takes place during the initial cycles. We monitored the structural change of the cathode after the first charge, where it appears that elemental sulfur is formed on the outer surface of graphene capsules, as indicated by green arrows in the TEM image of a Li2S@graphene capsule first charged to 3.5 V (Fig. 1D). Most likely, there is a portion of active species that managed to escape the capsules during the initial cycles, which is responsible for the capacity fading. However, during the long-term cycling, impressive cycling stability is demonstrated by the Li2S@graphene electrode. In contrast to the bare Li2S electrode, where the capacity fades from 430 to below 40 mAh g−1 in just 70 cycles (Supplementary Fig. 3B), the Li2S@graphene electrode (Fig. 2B) shows dramatically enhanced cycling stability. After 200 cycles, a reversible capacity of over 600 mAh g−1 remains, demonstrating that the Li2S@graphene electrode delivers a high areal capacity of more than 4.2 mAh cm−2 for over 200 cycles, which has never been reported for Li2S or S electrodes coated on metal foil current collectors. The performance demonstrates overwhelming evidence of the structural advantage of Li2S@graphene capsules. The compact nanocapsule structure plays a crucial role in suppressing the PS loss, thus boosting long-term cycle stability. We also investigated the cycling life as a function of different active mass loadings (Fig. 2D), where lighter Li2S electrodes exhibit higher reversible capacity and better cycling performance. For example, at the loading of 5 mg cm−2, the reversible capacity after 200 cycles improves to 702 mAh g−1. At the loading of 2 mg cm−2, which is common in the literature, the reversible capacity further improves to 762 mAh g−1 after 200 cycles. High power capability is required for hybrid electric vehicles (HEV) and often for electric vehicles (EV). We tested the rate performance of Li2S@graphene/Li cells. Fig. 2E displays the charge-discharge curves at different current rates, where Li2S@graphene electrode exhibits good rate capability with high discharge capacities of 1230 (0.1 C), 1105 (0.2 C), 984 (0.5 C), 802 (1.0 C), and 600 mAh g−1 (2.0 C). Such a rate capability is quite impressive for the heavy mass loading of 5 mg cm−2. Fig. 2F shows the long-term rate cycling performance of Li2S@graphene electrode at high current densities. Over 300 cycles, including the first 100 cycles at 1 C (5.56 mA cm−2), from 101 to 190 cycles at 2 C (11.13 mA cm−2) and from 191 to 300 cycles at 1C, the overall capacity retention is 57%, where the decay rate is only 0.14% per cycle at such high areal current densities. In addition, we observed the SEM morphology of the cycled Li anode after such long rate-cycling (Supplementary Fig. 5), which showed a relatively smooth surface morphology covered by glassy SEI layers, without obvious dendrite formation. 6

These results are, indeed, relevant, and are among the best cycling performance of Li2S cathodes reported to date. Another significant advantage of the Li2S@graphene cathode over conventional sulfur electrodes is that Li2S@graphene can serve as a source of lithium, which allows for the use of anodes other than lithium metal that has safety challenges. Here, as a proof of concept, we investigated a full cell system of Li2S@graphene/graphite to demonstrate such an advantage. As Fig. 2G shows, the Li2S@graphene cathode (2 mg cm−2) delivers a high initial charge capacity of 1600 mAh g−1 when being charged to 3.5 V, but it shows a discharge capacity of 730 mAh g−1 when being discharged to 1.4 V, relatively low compared to the lithium metal half-cell. This is mainly caused by the formation of a solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) on the graphite anode surface. For the subsequent cycles, the charge-discharge profiles and capacity values are well retained, and the Coulombic efficiency increases to above 90%. Due to the higher average lithiation/delithiation potential of the graphite electrode (~ 0.1 V vs Li/Li+) (Supplementary Fig. 6), the Li2S@graphene/graphite cell has lower voltage plateaus during activation and subsequent cycles and larger polarization voltage differences. Despite the relatively low reversible capacity, the cell still shows good long-term cycling stability (Fig. 2H). After 200 cycles, a discharge capacity of 440 mAh g−1 remains with an average Coulombic efficiency of 92.5%. In addition, Supplementary Fig. 7 shows the voltage profiles and rate cycling performance at different current densities. The full cell also exhibits good rate capability, where a discharge capacity of 700 mAh g−1 is achieved at 0.2 C; even at 2.0 C, the capacity is still retained at about 300 mAh g−1.

Electrochemical reactions of the Li2S@graphene electrode In-situ electrochemical impedance spectra (EIS) were collected to analyze the electrochemical behavior of the Li2S@graphene electrode during the charge-discharge process. Fig. 3A shows the in-situ time-lapse EIS profiles of Li2S@graphene during the first cycle. The freshly made cell shows a small semicircle at the high-medium frequency associated with the interfacial and change transfer resistance (13, 23, 31, 39), indicating the excellent electrochemical environment in such nanocapsule structure. The fitting line (see Fig. 3B) of a typical Nyquist plot at the state of charge (SOC) of 52% clearly shows the impedance spectrum, which consists of the resistances of the electrolyte solution (Rs), interfacial nanolayer (Rsuf), and charge transfer (Rct). A plot of all the resistances against the SOC and depth of discharge (DOD) is shown in Fig. 3C along with the resistances of the bare Li2S for comparison (the EIS profiles 7

of bare Li2S are shown in Supplementary Fig. 8.). It is noteworthy that Rs of the bare Li2S dramatically decreases to 66% when the SOC reaches 54%, implying the severe dissolution of PS species into the liquid electrolyte. However, for the Li2S@graphene, Rs maintains stable throughout the entire cycle, suggesting that the capsule structure can suppress the PS’ outflow. In addition, Rsuf of the Li2S@graphene is much smaller than that of the bare Li2S, demonstrating that the nanocapsule structure facilitates lithium diffusion from electrolyte to surface of active material. More importantly, the Rct of the Li2S@graphene is very much reduced by an order of magnitude compared to that of the bare Li2S. This result confirms the faster charge transfer kinetics within the Li2S@graphene, thanks to the high electrochemical activity by uniform coating of graphene nanolayers. In order to further understand the electrochemical properties of the Li2S@graphene electrodes, we performed in-situ synchrotron XRD and ex-situ X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) analysis to evaluate the local structural and chemical evolution of the encapsulated active species during the charge-discharge process in Li2S@graphene/Li cells. During the first charge, the XRD peaks associated with Li2S phase (marked by black arrows) are gradually weakened, while the sulfur (S8) phase (marked by red arrows) appears at the same time. Li2S→S8→amorphous Li2S/Li2S2. To further understand charge-discharge processes, we monitored the structural evolution of the Li2S@graphene electrode by TEM studies. Figs. 1D and 1E show the ex-situ TEM images of Li2S@graphene particles initially charged to 3.5 V and then discharged to 1.5 V, respectively. When charged to 3.5 V, the inside Li2S crystal is converted to S8 nanoparticles, confirmed by the SAED pattern (Supplementary Fig. 10). During the first delithiation, Li-ion extraction from Li2S causes volume contraction, thus leading to particle pulverization inside the graphene shells. Meanwhile, a few sulfur nanoparticles indicated by green arrows are deposited on the outside surface of graphene layers after delithiating, which implies that a certain portion of LiPS species escapes from the graphene capsules. This is mainly responsible for the capacity fading (Fig. 2A) observed in the Li2S@graphene/Li half-cell. During the first discharge to 1.5 V (Fig. 1E), the lithiation process converts the internal scattered sulfur nanoparticles into integral cores, where the expanded phase fills up the capsules. A primary challenge of sulfur electrodes in LiS batteries is the dissolution of PS species into electrolyte, leading to the serious loss of active materials. As the TEM image (Fig. 1F) shows, Li2S@graphene electrode still contains significant amount of active materials inside capsules even after 100 cycles. The results clearly demonstrates that the in-situ grown Li2S@graphene capsule structure with the integral graphene shell can entrap LiPS well inside to minimize their diffusion to the electrolyte. More direct evidence for the structural stability during (de)-lithiation was confirmed by insitu TEM measurements. Fig. 1G shows the in-situ TEM set up for real time observation of the (de)-lithiation in a Li2S@graphene particle with the bias of 3.0 V. The particle has an original diameter of about 430 nm. After the first delithiation, the particle is reduced to 395 nm with the volume shrinking ratio around 20%. During the following cycles, the particle size shifts between 390 and 405 nm with a very small volume variation ~ 10%. Such excellent structural stability is believed to be responsible to the observed good cycle performance of the Li2S@graphene. In contrast, the bare Li2S shows a severe structure vulnerability during the (de)-lithiation under in-situ TEM observations (Supplementary Fig. 11), where the active particle can hardly survive after three cycles. This leads to drastic decomposition and severe mass loss of the electrode and therefore, the quick fading of the cell. The in-situ TEM snapshots are also shown in Supplementary Movie S1 and S2. Furthermore, we applied a larger bias (6.0 V) to a Li2S@graphene particle to investigate its electrochemical behavior during fast (de)lithiation cycling (Supplementary Fig. 12) (40). Surprisingly, even under such a high rate, the active material can maintain the good structural integrity for about dozen cycles. However, it 9

should be pointed out that a fracture (blue circle) starts to form and becomes larger in the late cycles, leading to an instantaneous formation of “dead S” (yellow and red circles, Supplementary Fig. 12), which are almost inactive during the following cycles. To this end, when the material cycles in a liquid cell, the PS species will easily diffuse from the capsules into liquid electrolyte through these cracks, thus resulting in severe mass and capacity loss. This result also explains the capacity fading after the long cycling, especially at the high rates. Clearly, the structural stability of the Li2S@graphene is the key factor to guarantee the good electrochemical performance. Another possible reason for the mitigation of the dissolution of LiPS into the electrolyte is the chemical binding effect between the active species and defective graphene layers in the Li2S@graphene system. Fig. 5A schematically illustrates the microstructure of Li2S@graphene, where the extremely compact encapsulation will act as a robust physical barrier to entrap Li 2S and the as-formed LiPS species within the graphene shell during cycling. Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations were carried out to elucidate the energetics of the interactions between Li2S and LiPS species with graphene capsules. The simulation results indicate that Li2S can strongly bind to defects in the graphene shell. Fig. 5B depicts the binding between the Li atoms of Li2S and a graphene monovacancy, where the binding energy was calculated to be 1.21 eV, suggesting strong binding between encapsulated Li2S and the defect sites. Carbon vacancy defects on graphene can allow some species, such as Li+ ions, to diffuse through the capsules. However, diffusion of larger species, such as the LiPS species, will be prohibited if the defects are too small to allow these species to pass through. Alternatively, if the binding energies between the species and graphene defects are sufficiently strong, their diffusion will be inhibited as well. Therefore, we calculated the binding energies between the smallest LiPS species, Li2S3 (molecular structures of all LiPS species shown in Fig. 5C (41)), and vacancy defects on graphene, as shown by DFT calculations in Fig. 5D and Supplementary Fig. 13. We evaluate the possibility for these LiPS species to diffuse through different size graphene defects, illustrated by the number of carbon vacancies. The calculations show that smaller defects, 2-C, 4-C and 6-C defects, are physically too small for the smallest LiPS, i.e., Li2S3 molecule, to penetrate, even though their binding energies with Li2S3 are generally below 1 eV. On the other hand, the larger defects, 10-C, 12-C and 24C defects, show extremely strong binding with Li2S3 (over 3 eV). The values of binding energies are shown in Supplementary Table 2. This suggests that although the graphene defects are physically large enough for Li2S3 to escape, the strong binding interactions between these defects and Li2S3 would limit this molecular species from diffusing into the electrolyte. A 10

similar situation for other larger LiPS species would be expected, considering that it is even more difficult for those larger LiPS to diffuse through the graphene defects. In addition, the stacking of multi-layer graphene that wraps Li2S particles further blocks the pathways through graphene defects, which are mostly likely not aligned from layer to layer (42). These calculations indicate that the graphene shell acts as an effective capsule structure to entrap the LiPS species. Overall, the dual effect of physical entrapment and chemical binding would enable graphene to be effective in confining Li2S and LiPS within the shell during cycling. The Li2S@graphene nano-capsule electrode structure developed here shows the promise for the use in advanced Li–S batteries and Li-ion batteries. However, an essential weakness of the capacity fading under ultra-long cycling, especially at the high rates, has yet to be improved. Further work will be focused on tuning the electrode structural and optimizing the electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 14) to further improve the cell performance.

Conclusions In conclusion, a highly scalable and cost-effective lithiothermic reaction of CS2 has been shown to form Li2S@graphene capsules. Both graphene encapsulation shells and crystalline Li2S cores are nucleated simultaneously to generate composite cathode materials with high compactness, high conductivity and excellent structural stability. Most importantly, these architecture features facilitate the high mass loading of Li2S, where, a high areal loading of 10 mg cm−2 is realized with excellent electrochemical performance in Li–S cells on conventional metal foil current collectors. The Li2S@graphene capsules exhibit superior electrochemical performance even in graphite/Li2S full-cells, demonstrating high reversibility of the material. Such a simultaneously grown capsule design has multiple advantages: (1) the nature of Li2S prevents any volume expansion; (2) No Li2S particles will be formed without the presence of co-formed graphene layers; the graphene framework functions as a ubiquitous electrical conduit so that nearly all Li2S nanoparticles are electrochemically active; (3) the compact graphene shells exhibit good physical properties, which preserves the structural integrity of the composites. The graphene shells also mitigate the loss of internal PS into electrolyte during cycling. This work overcomes the major constraints associated with traditional sulfur electrodes and previous reported Li2S composites, which involved high activation potential, complex fabrication process, and high cost. It may shift the paradigm for designing high-performance sulfur electrode materials. Moreover, the facile and scalable fabrication strategy makes this electrode material potentially viable for commercialization in Li–S batteries. 11

Methods Materials Synthesis. Li2S@graphene nanocomposites were synthesized by a one-step solidstate reaction, as described in the equation (1). In a typical reaction, 0.4 g lithium metal foil was used to react with CS2 vapor carried by Argon gas at 650 ºC for 5 h in a tube furnace. Product of 1.0 g can be obtained for one batch, where Li2S nanocrystals and the tight wrapper of fewlayer graphene were simultaneously generated, thus forming the Li2S@graphene nanocapsules.

Materials Characterization. To prevent moisture contamination of Li2S, special precautions were taken during the characterization. TEM, EDX and EELS were performed using a FEI Titan 80−300ST with a spherical and chromatic aberration imaging corrector working at 80 kV. The samples were first tightly sealed in foil/polybags before being transferred into the microscope chamber via an argon-filled glove bag. Raman spectra were obtained using an inVia microscope spectrometer (Renishaw 2000) with a HeNe laser at 633 nm wavelength, after the samples were tightly sealed in glass holders. High-energy XRD was carried out at the 11-ID-C beamline of Advanced Photon Source, Argonne National Laboratory. The samples were tightly sealed using Kapton tape. The XRD patterns were collected in the transmission mode using a Perkin Elmer large area detector. The S K-edge XAS measurements were performed at the 9ID-B beamline of Advanced Photon Source, Argonne National Laboratory. The samples were protected by the Kapton tape to prevent the contamination form air. The XANES spectra were recorded in transmission mode.

Electrochemical Methods. Owing to the sensitivity of Li2S to moisture, all the electrode preparation and cell assembly procedures were carried out in an argon-filled glove box with moisture and oxygen levels below 0.5 ppm. The Li2S@graphene nanocomposites were mixed with conductive acetylene black and poly(vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF) binder in a weight ratio of 90:5:5, followed by dispersion in N-methyl-2-pyrrolidinone (NMP) to form slurry, which was then pasted onto aluminum foil and dried at 60 oC under vacuum. Based on the Li2S content in Li2S@graphene capsule (Supplementary Fig. 2C), we can determine the weight percentage of Li2S in the electrodes to be ~80%. For comparison, bare Li2S electrodes were also prepared by mixing ball-milled Li2S with acetylene black and PVDF binder in the weight ratio of 50:45:5 and coating the slurry onto aluminum foil. For assembling cells with high cathode mass loading (10 mg cm–2), we used a thick Li chip with the thickness of 500 um (~ 48.4 mg) as anode, and added ~ 80 mg D2-DOL as electrolyte. The mass ratio between electrolyte and cathode was about 8:1. For rate capability tests, the cathode mass loading was ~ 5 mg cm–2, the Li anode thickness was ~ 250 um (~ 24.2 mg), and the amount of added electrolyte was ~ 40 mg. For Li2S@graphene/Graphite cells, the cathode mass loading was ~ 2 mg, and the graphite anode mass loading was ~ 9 mg, where the theoretical capacity ratio between anode and cathode was about 1.3:1. Cells were charged and discharged using a MACCOR battery tester, the cathodes were first activated at 1/20 C (1 C = 1600 mA g−1S) by charging to a high cutoff voltage of 3.5 V (4.0 V for the pure Li2S electrodes) versus Li+/Li. Galvanostatic cycling was then carried out at the specified C-rate from 1.5 to 3.0 V versus Li+/Li. The specific capacities were calculated based on the active mass of S in the whole electrode. Cyclic voltammograms were recorded on a Solartron Analytical 1400 System between 1.5 and 3.5 V versus Li+/Li at specified scan rates.

In-situ EIS Measurements. In-situ EIS experiment was performed on a Solartron Analytical 1400 System, using an EL-Cell three-electrode cell with metal lithium as counter and reference electrodes. The cell was assembled in an argon-filled glove box, which was then aged until the open-circuit voltage (OCV) turns stable. The charge-discharge test for Li2S@graphene electrode was carried out at 1/10 C rate between 1.5 and 3.5 V, while for the bare Li2S electrode, it was carried out at 1/20 C rate between 1.5 and 4.0 V. After every 1 h charge or discharge, we hold the cells stable for 30 min before performing the AC impedance. The AC amplitude was ± 5 mV, and the applied frequency range was from 100 kHz to 0.05 Hz.

In-situ TEM Measurements. In-situ TEM experiment was conducted on a JEOL 3010 equipped with a Nanofactory holder capable by applying bias at the two metallic rods within the imaging area inside TEM. The targeted material was attached to one metallic rod using conductive glue and the other tungsten rod was handled to scratch the surface of Li metal to obtain enough Li anode inside an argon-filled glove box. The transfer of the sample into TEM chamber was exposed in air for about 30 s, and Li metal was thus oxidized to Li2O on the surface, which can be regarded as the solid electrolyte. The reversible (de)-lithiation cycling was controlled within 3.0 or 6.0 V. As expected, the (de)-lithiation rate with the bias of 6.0 V was much faster than that within 3.0 V. We targeted on the large Li2S nanoparticles for in-situ 13

observation as smaller particles less than 100 nm in diameter were mostly embedded beneath the uneven terminate surface of the gold rod terminate and made practical observation inconvenient and less efficient.

In-situ Synchrotron Measurements. In operando time-resolved high-energy XRD measurements were performed on the beamline 11-ID-C at the Advanced Photon Source, Argonne National Laboratory. A monochromator with a Si (113) single crystal was used to provide an X-ray beam with the energy of 115 keV. High-energy X-ray with a beam size of 0.2 mm × 0.2 mm and wavelength of 0.11725 Å was used to obtain two-dimensional (2D) diffraction patterns in the transmission geometry. X-rays were collected with a Perkin-Elmer large-area detector placed at 1800 mm from the sample. The home-made coin cells were charged/discharged with a constant current of 160 mA g−1S between 1.5 V and 3.5 V. The diffraction data of the sample were collected every 600 seconds. The obtained 2D diffraction patterns were calibrated using a standard CeO2 sample and converted to 1D patterns using Fit2D software.

Theoretical Calculations. Ab initio simulations were performed using the Vienna Ab Initio Simulation Package in the framework of density functional theory (DFT). The interactions between graphene defects and Li2S and Li2S3 molecule were investigated using DFT calculations. The structures of the LiPS species shown in Fig. 5C were calculated at the B3LYP/6-31G(2df,p) level of theory using Gaussian 09 package. In the Li2S@graphene system, the Li2S nanoparticle is likely to bind the graphene shell through the defects on graphene. Thus the binding energy between a Li2S molecule and the carbon vacancy defect of graphene was investigated to estimate the interaction between the Li2S nanoparticle and the graphene shell (Fig. 5B). The binding interactions between Li2S3 and carbon vacancy defects of graphene were used as a representative system to study the favorability for the soluble LiPS species to diffuse through the defects on graphene. The binding energies were calculated using the GGA_PBE method with a plane wave basis set implemented in the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP). The energy cutoff of 400 eV was used, and the Γ-point and a 3 × 3 ×1 k-point mash were used to sample the Brillouin zones for the gas phase molecules and graphene systems, respectively. Graphene defects with different sizes of carbon vacancy were considered: 1-C (1 carbon atom vacancy), 2-C, 6-C, 12-C and 24-C.
