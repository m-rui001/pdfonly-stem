Abstract: Spontaneous emission is not an inherent property of a luminescent material; rather, it arises due to interaction between the material and its local electromagnetic environment. Changing the environment can thus alter the emission rate, with potential applications in sensing, integrated photonics, and solar energy conversion. Significant increases in emission rate require an optical resonator that stores light in as small a volume as possible, for as long as possible. This is currently achieved using two main systems: photonic-crystal microcavities and plasmonic metal nanoparticles. These two systems have largely been developed independently, but the underlying physical mechanisms are the same. Comparing the two provides insight into emission modification and illustrates some of the subtleties involved in interpreting experimental results.

Page 3

2 Spontaneous emission is responsible for nearly all the light that reaches our eyes, whether it comes from the sun, a light bulb, or the glowing display of a computer or smart phone. Spontaneous emission also plays an important role in determining the performance of many optoelectronic devices, including not only lighting and displays, but also lasers, optical amplifiers, and solar cells. The rate of spontaneous emission was long thought to be an inherent, unchangeable property of an emitter. However, we now understand it as arising from the interaction between an emitter and its electromagnetic environment. Changing this environment can thus modify spontaneous emission. Although it was first demonstrated over 40 years ago, the ability to modify spontaneous emission has recently gained new importance, as photonics has evolved into nanophotonics and the engineering of optical structures has progressed down to length scales smaller than optical wavelengths. The two structures that are at the heart of nanophotonics – photonic crystals and plasmonic metal nanoparticles – have also been the focus of efforts to modify spontaneous emission. However, these efforts have largely been pursued independently by different scientific communities who have developed different descriptions of the phenomena. In this Review, I point out the common physical principles behind the different attempts to modify spontaneous emission. I also point out some of the subtleties involved with experimentally verifying and quantifying emission modification and review a few applications that may be enabled by emission modification.

Understanding emission modification The electronic states of an atom or molecule, considered in isolation, are stationary solutions of the Schrödinger equation: there is no apparent reason for an electron in a higher-energy state to relax into a lower-energy state unless it interacts with something else. That “something else” turns out to be the quantized radiation field. A proper treatment of spontaneous emission thus requires the theoretical treatment of quantum electrodynamics (QED). The Purcell effect. The first proposal to modify spontaneous emission came in 1946 by Purcell, who suggested that nuclear magnetic transition rates could be increased by placing atoms inside a cavity.4 The ratio between the modified and free-space emission rates has thus come to be known as the Purcell factor. These two requirements are to some extent contradictory – tighter confinement means higher losses – and research into emission modification is largely a matter of developing resonators that optimize this tradeoff. The best-performing resonators that have been found so far are photonic-crystal (PC) microcavities and metal nanoparticles (MNP). In a PC cavity, light is trapped due to reflections off of periodic dielectric structures. Optimized designs of PC cavities relevant for emission modification have led to quality factors as high as 104; 5 however, the minimum possible mode volume of the cavity is set by the diffraction limit as approximately 0.1(𝜆𝜆𝑜𝑜𝑛𝑛 ⁄ )3, where 𝜆𝜆𝑜𝑜 is the free-space wavelength associated with the transition and n is the refractive index of the medium within the cavity. MNPs, by contrast, are not subject to the diffraction limit, because light is confined by coupling to electron oscillations, or plasmons, in the metal. Optical fields can thus be confined on length scales comparable to the dimensions of the MNPs themselves, generally on the order of 10 nm. Even smaller mode volumes, on the order of 10−6(𝜆𝜆𝑜𝑜𝑛𝑛 ⁄ )3, are obtained for optical fields confined in the gap between two closely-spaced MNPs. 6 (In this Review, I will use “MNP” to refer both to isolated nanoparticles with individual plasmon resonances and pairs or assemblies of nanoparticles with coupled plasmon resonances.) This strong confinement, however, comes at the cost of strong damping due to optical losses in the metals, and quality factors for MNPs are on the order of 10.7 Despite the different parameter regimes involved, however, the essential physical mechanism for emission modification is the same for both PC cavities and MNPs.

One point of view that has been popular for the description of emission modification by MNPs is based on the language of Förster resonant energy transfer (FRET).11 In FRET, two emitters in close proximity exchange energy non-radiatively through dipoledipole coupling; in modified spontaneous emission, the MNP takes the place of the second emitter. Another term that can cause some confusion is “quenching.” Following the language of FRET, it is often used to describe energy transfer from an emitter to a MNP followed by damping of the plasmon within the MNP. Sometimes, “quenching” is used to describe only coupling to higher-order plasmon modes, such as multipolar modes in MNPs or high-wavenumber plasmon modes on surfaces. However, the word “quenching” would generally not be used to describe an emitter producing a photon in an optical cavity and that photon subsequently being lost to absorption in the cavity, regardless of what cavity mode was excited. In order to have a consistent vocabulary for all emission modification, it may be best to reserve “quenching” to refer to processes that are not connected to changes in the electromagnetic LDOS. In semiconductor microcavities, these processes include charge trapping by states at exposed surfaces; for MNPs, they include charge transfer from the emitter to the metal. Resonators and antennas. Excitation of a MNP by an emitter followed by radiation from the MNP to the far field is analogous to excitation of an antenna by a transmitter, followed by radiation of a radio-wave or microwave signal by the antenna.12 However, there are important differences between conventional antennas and MNPs, apart from the different length scales involved. Perhaps most importantly, metals behave like nearly perfect conductors at radio-wave or microwave frequencies, whereas they have large kinetic inductances at optical frequencies due to inertia of electrons in the metal. 13

Page 9

8 Resonances in MNPs thus depend differently on geometry than resonances in conventional antennas, and one cannot simply scale down a radio-wave or microwave antenna in order to design an optical antenna. 14 In addition, because coupling to resonances in MNPs involves transferring energy from the fields to the kinetic energy of electrons in the metal, they necessarily experience strong losses due to electron scattering, resulting in lifetimes on the order of 10 fs.7 Despite these differences, emission modification can be modeled in the same way as conventional antennas are modeled. A localized emitter can be modeled as an oscillating current source, with a dipole moment equal to the transition dipole moment of the emitter, 𝜇𝜇12 . The power radiated by the emitter, Wtot, can then be determined by integrating the calculated Poynting vector over a surface, Ωdecay, that encloses the dipole but not the boundaries of the MNP or cavity, as illustrated in Figure 1b. The Purcell factor is simply 𝐹𝐹𝑃𝑃= 𝑊𝑊𝑡𝑡𝑡𝑡𝑡𝑡𝑊𝑊𝑜𝑜 ⁄ , where Wo is the radiated power in the absence of the MNP or cavity. This approach gives the same result as a quantum-mechanical treatment,15 except that it does not take into account non-radiative decay of the emitter or quenching. The rate at which radiation is emitted into the far field, Γ𝑓𝑓𝑓𝑓𝑓𝑓, can also be determined by placing an integration surface, Ωemission, around the entire system, and the rate at which emission goes into the MNP and is subsequently lost to damping is determined by placing an integration surface, Ωabsorption, around the MNP alone. The fields can be determined analytically for a few simple geometries, but numerical solutions are required in most cases. Several computational methods are available for these classical electrodynamics simulations, with perhaps the most popular being the finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) method.16

Systems for emission modification Plasmonic metal nanostructures. The first measurements of modified spontaneous emission were performed in the late 1960s by Drexhage, who studied dye layers precisely separated from smooth metal surfaces by molecular films, and who obtained Purcell

Page 10

9 factors of approximately 3.17,18 These exemplary experiments stood alone for about a decade, until interest in molecules on metal surfaces was provoked by the discovery of surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS): unexpectedly large Raman-scattering signals from molecules adsorbed on rough silver surfaces.19 Molecules on rough silver surfaces were similarly found to show significant increases in luminescence intensity20 and in relaxation rate. 21 Like SERS, these changes were soon understood to be due to the interaction of the molecules with plasmon resonances in the MNPs that make up the rough surfaces. These plasmon resonances are collective oscillations of the conduction electrons in the MNPs. Because of the large number of oscillating charges, excitation of plasmon resonances produces strong local fields within nanometer-scale volumes next to the MNPs. In other words, coupling between the low density of electromagnetic modes in free space and the high density of conduction-electron states in the metal results in a strong, hybrid resonance with a large LDOS in the near field of the MNP. The high LDOS, in turn, produces strong emission modification, albeit at the cost of strong losses, due to the strong energy dissipation of electrons in metals. Interest in emitter-MNP interactions accelerated in the early 2000s, motivated by advances in MNP synthesis and in optical measurement. 22 The great majority of experiments involve gold and silver nanoparticles, because they support plasmon resonances at optical frequencies, and because optical losses in these materials are lower than in other plasmonic metals. Spherical gold and silver nanoparticles have plasmon resonances at wavelengths of approximately 520 nm and 400 nm, respectively; these resonances can be tuned to longer wavelengths by increasing the aspect ratio of the nanoparticles, providing access to the entire visible and near-infrared spectral regions. Losses are lowest in chemically synthesized colloidal MNPs, because they can be nearly free of bulk defects, grain boundaries, and surface roughness. Emission modification by these nanoparticles has therefore been studied extensively.23,24,25,26,27 Especially strong emission modification occurs when the emitters are placed in a nanometer-scale gap between two MNPs; an example is shown in Figure 2a.28 Indeed, the majority of SERS signal enhancement and emission modification on rough metal

Photonic-crystal cavities. At the same time as chemists were first studying emission from molecules on rough metal surfaces, atomic physicists were independently using cavities to enhance transitions in isolated atoms. 33 , 34 These early “cavity-QED” experiments demonstrated the feasibility of modifying emission rates, but they were technically challenging, and the relatively large cavity volumes meant that Purcell factors were small (only about 1.02 for visible frequencies). These difficulties were overcome by moving to semiconductor structures: epitaxial techniques enabled quantum wells to be incorporated monolithically into micrometer-scale planar microcavities. 35, 36 Increased Purcell factors of approximately 5 were eventually obtained by confining the carriers in three dimensions within epitaxially-grown quantum dots (QD) and by confining light in three dimensions by etching the planar microcavities into pillars.37,38 Even greater Purcell factors were later obtained by confining the light in photonic-crystal (PC) cavities.39 A PC is a structure in which the refractive index is modulated periodically on a length scale comparable to the wavelength of light.40 Interference among multiple reflections in the periodic structure can prevent light propagation within a certain frequency range, known as a stop band or photonic band gap. The LDOS within the stop band is thus reduced, leading to a reduction in emission.41 This was first demonstrated in “woodpile” PC structures fabricated using a complex wafer-fusion technique (shown in Figure 3a),42 and independently in “inverse-opal” PC structures formed by a self-assembly technique (shown in Figure 3b). 43 In the inverse-opal structures, reduced emission rates were observed within the stop band and increased emission rates were observed at the edge of the stop band, corresponding to Purcell factors of approximately 1.4. This can be

Page 12

11 understood as resulting from redistribution of LDOS from within the stop band to the band edge. In the presence of disorder, the band-edge states become localized and lower in energy;44 in the presence of strong disorder, such as a localized defect in the PC lattice, a localized photonic state is formed within the stop band. The localized defect in the PC is a type of optical cavity, with the surrounding PC acting as a mirror in all directions. These defect cavities have been the main focus of research into emission modification using PCs. For example, defects were introduced into woodpile structures, and emission was coupled into resonant modes of the cavities.42, 45 Top-down fabrication of threedimensional PCs with engineered defects is highly challenging, however, so experimental efforts have primarily involved cavities in two-dimensional PCs.46,47 The most common configuration consists of a thin, suspended membrane of a high-index semiconductor material into which an array of air holes is etched. The holes form a two-dimensional PC structure within the plane of the membrane, and light is confined in the direction normal to the membrane by total internal reflection. A cavity is formed by filling in one of the air holes, and the quality factor of the cavity is often improved by modifying the adjacent air holes; an example is shown in Figure 3c. Purcell factors up to approximately 8 have been achieved in such structures.48

Measuring emission modification Many papers have been published on emission enhancement, but reported results can appear rather inconsistent. Typically, photoluminescence (PL) measurements are performed: the system is excited by a light source at the absorption frequency of the emitters, and the intensity of the resulting emission is compared to the intensity from a nominally identical set of emitters that are not coupled to cavities or MNPs. The problem is that this ratio of intensities is not in general equal to the Purcell factor. First of all, the cavity or MNP can concentrate the intensity of the incoming light at the location of the emitters, increasing the amount of light they absorb. Indeed, in most measurements, most of the increase in PL signal comes only from this absorption

Page 13

12 enhancement and not from any change in emission. It is possible to separate the effects of absorption enhancement from emission modification by determining the fluorescence intensity when absorption of excitation light by the emitter is saturated: at this point, the rate at which photons are emitted depends only on the decay rate of the emitter.49 A difficulty with such experiments is that many emitters will undergo rapid photobleaching under the high excitation intensities that are required. On the other hand, photobleaching itself presents an opportunity to measure emission modification separately from absorption enhancement: the total number of photons radiated by an emitter before it bleaches is independent of the rate at which the emitter is excited, and is proportaional to the radiative enhancement factor.50 However, the number of photons that are detected in an experiment is not necessarily a constant fraction of the number of photons that are emitted, because the cavity or MNP can change the propagation direction of the light that comes from the emitter. Since a PL measurement necessarily involves collecting light over a limited range of angles, this redirection of emission can significantly change the amount of light that is detected, even without a significant change in the total power radiated by the emitter. A quantitative measurement of emission modification thus generally requires a measurement of the relaxation rate of the emitters. Measuring the overall change in lifetime, Ftot, due to the cavity or MNP is relatively straightforward, but includes all decay processes, including the rate of nonradiative processes within the emitter, Γ𝑛𝑛𝑛𝑛 𝑒𝑒𝑒𝑒, and the rate of quenching, Γ𝑞𝑞, if present. Determining the Purcell factor, FP, thus requires access to a parameter that affects only the rate, Γ𝑔𝑔, at which emission occurs into the cavity or MNP. For example, the effect of detuning between the emission frequency of epitaxial QDs and the resonance frequency of a microcavity mode was studied by fabricating microcavities containing several QDs,37 and the effect of detuning between molecules or colloidal QDs and plasmons in MNPs was studied by coupling the emitters to MNPs with different shapes and sizes (see Figure 4a).51 In both cases, a maximum in the emission rate was observed for zero detuning. However, these measurements still involved ensembles of emitters. In any ensemble measurement, the emitters all couple differently to the cavity or MNP, even if they have

Page 14

13 the same emission frequency, because of differences in their locations and orientations. Moreover, the degree to which the lifetime of a given emitter is modified is correlated in a non-trivial way to changes in absorption and redirection of radiation, and thus to the amount that it contributes to the overall PL signal. Quantitative information about emission modification can ultimately be extracted only by modeling all of these effects, usually based on limited information. Ultimately, all these difficulties can be overcome only by making measurements on individual emitters. This was accomplished for epitaxial QDs in microcavities by changing the temperature of the sample: this changes the emission frequency of the QDs, tuning them through cavity resonances.52,53 This is unfeasible, however, for QDs coupled to MNPs, because of the broad linewidths of plasmon resonances. Instead of changing the detuning, then, the most thorough measurements involve controlling the separation between a single MNP and a single dye molecule by attaching the MNP to a scanningprobe tip (see Figure 4b).54,55

Some potential applications Sensors. The prospect of enhancing luminescence offers the attractive possibility of increasing the sensitivity of biochemical assays and sensors based on fluorescent labels. This has been extensively studied, primarily using rough or patterned metal surfaces, and significant signal improvements have been reported. 56 In many cases, the observed increase in photoluminescence is likely due primarily to absorption enhancement; this means that an equivalent increase in sensor sensitivity could be accomplished simply by increasing the intensity of the excitation source. Light-emitting diodes. An early motivation for semiconductor cavity-QED experiments was to increase the external efficiency of light-emitting diodes (LEDs). This was demonstrated using microcavities59 and plasmonic metal coatings.60 For plasmonic structures, however, the gain in extraction efficiency is offset by losses in the metal, and the overall performance improvement is modest at best.61 In any case, if the goal is to emit light more or less isotropically to free space, as in lighting and display applications, then patterning or roughening the top surface of the device may be a more straightforward solution to the extraction problem. On the other hand, the goal for other applications, such as integrated optics and optical interconnects, is to emit light with high efficiency into a single spatial mode. This can be quantified in terms of the single-mode coupling efficiency, 𝛽𝛽= Γ𝑔𝑔Γ𝑡𝑡𝑡𝑡𝑡𝑡 ⁄ . If Γ𝑔𝑔 is much larger than any of the other decay rates, then 𝛽𝛽 approaches 100%, and all the emission is coupled into a single mode. This ensures spatial coherence, thus fulfilling the requirements for optical communication without the high excitation powers that would be required for nanoscale lasers.62,63 Increasing Γ𝑡𝑡𝑡𝑡𝑡𝑡 also allows for faster modulation of the LED, enabling high-speed communication. If MNPs are used to increase the decay rate, this will come at the cost of increased losses and thus decreased device efficiency; PC cavities are thus more appropriate for these applications. However, the high quality factors of PC cavities mean that they can effectively modify emission only from emitters with narrow linewidths: emission with frequencies outside of the narrow cavity resonance will not be modified. They are thus not well suited for the broad-band emission of room-temperature, bulk semiconductors. Rather,

Page 16

15 LEDs with high 𝛽𝛽 will require emitters with narrow linewidths, such as epitaxially-grown QDs at low temperature. Even ensembles of QDs emit over a broad spectral range, due to inhomogeneities in the size, shape, and composition of the QDs; a true single-mode LED will thus need to employ a single QD as an emitter. This is challenging to achieve, but opens up new technological possibilities. Specifically, the single QD will, under the right conditions, emit one photon at a time;64 coupling this photon efficiently to a single spatial mode leads to an efficient single-photon source, an enabling technology for optical quantum communication and quantum computation. This was first demonstrated, with 𝛽𝛽 = 37%, by coupling a single epitaxial QD to a three-dimensional microcavity fabricated by etching a planar microcavity into a micropost (see Figure 5a).65 Coupling a single epitaxial QD to a PC cavity later led to a single-photon source with 𝛽𝛽 = 92%.66 Even higher efficiencies, as high as 98%, have been achieved by using a PC waveguide, rather than a cavity.67 These waveguides are formed by removing a row of air holes, rather than a single hole, from a two-dimensional PC; they have the added advantage of allowing the single-photon source to be directly incorporated into an integrated-optics structure. Single-photon sources can similarly be coupled to plasmonic waveguides, or metal nanowires.68,69

Modified thermal radiation. Most experiments on modified emission have involved photoluminescence, but emitters can be excited through many means, and the resulting emission can be modified, regardless of its origin. In particular, thermal radiation, or spontaneous emission that is excited by heat, can be modified by patterning the radiating material and changing the LDOS at thermal emission frequencies. The first systematic attempts to modify thermal radiation in this way involved patterning gratings onto metal surfaces70 and highly doped silicon surfaces.71 These one-dimensional structures were later extended into two-dimensional and three-dimensional PCs,72,73,74 which have been demonstrated to remain stable up to temperatures of at least 1,400oC.75 Stability at high temperature is of particular importance for application of modified thermal radiation to thermophotovoltaic (TPV) systems.76,77 A TPV is a photovoltaic device that is designed to convert thermal radiation into electricity, as compared to

Page 17

16 conventional photovoltaic solar cells, which are designed to convert sunlight into electricity. In a solar TPV system, the heat that is responsible for thermal radiation is produced by absorbing sunlight using an isotropic, black absorber; a particular system is illustrated in Figure 5b. The efficiency of TPVs is limited by the broadband nature of thermal radiation: any photons below the bandgap of the photovoltaic material are not absorbed, and the excess energy of any photons above the bandgap is lost as heat in the device. Modifying thermal emission has the potential to improve efficiency by narrowing the emission spectrum to match the TPV bandgap. It can also redirect thermal radiation, so that the majority of it is coupled into the photovoltaic device.78,79 Efficient solar TPV devices will require very hot emitters, with temperatures above 1000oC. Even at these elevated temperatures, thermal emission is primarily in the near-infrared spectral region, where material losses are relatively low, so efficient emission modification should be feasible.

Beyond the Purcell effect As applications emerge, researchers continue to push towards higher Purcell factors, by increasing the quality factor of PC cavities, and by localizing the fields around MNPs to smaller volumes. As this happens, new physical phenomena come into play. For plasmonic MNPs, in particular, quantum-mechanical effects begin to become important when fields are confined on the single-nanometer scale,80,81,82 and damping rates within nanometer-scale particles increase as new scattering pathways become available.7 These effects reduce the strength of localized fields,83 thereby reducing the amount by which emission is modified.84 In addition, the simple model that emission modification is proportional to the electromagnetic LDOS at a single point may need to be revised, as gradients in the confined fields become large and the LDOS varies even over the scale of a single emitter. 85 The usual electric-dipole selection rule for optical emission is a result of translational symmetry; if the LDOS varies significantly across the emitter, this selection rule may no longer apply, and electric-quadrupole and magnetic-dipole transitions may be allowed.86,87,88,89

Page 18

17 If emission modification is strong enough, photons will be emitted into the cavity mode more quickly than they escape, or plasmons will be emitted into the MNP more quickly than they decay. This means that the emitter can be re-excited by the cavity photon or MNP plasmon, and energy will coherently oscillate back and forth, in a process known as vacuum Rabi oscillation.90 The domain in which this happens, known as the strong-coupling regime of cavity QED, has been reached for single epitaxial QDs in semiconductor microcavities,91,92 but has not yet been realized for single emitters coupled to MNPs, due to the strong damping of plasmons. On the other hand, attempts to achieve strong coupling have been motivated, at least in part, by the potential for ultrastrong optical nonlinearities, and it has been demonstrated that such nonlinearities may be feasible without necessarily reaching strong coupling.93,94,95 There is thus great potential for coupled emitter-cavity or emitter-MNP systems to enable applications beyond emission modification, such as ultra-low-power optical modulation and quantum information processing. 5,96,97 Even within the realm of emission modification, many opportunities and many challenges remain, despite the long history of the field. On the technological side, the initial demonstrations of potential applications must be made more robust and reproducible. On the fundamental side, the ultimate limits of emission modification remain to be explored. In both cases, a key challenge will be to reproducibly and reliably place individual emitters within cavities or next to MNPs at the precise locations required for most efficient coupling. This will most likely require a combination of the top-down and bottom-up approaches that have been developed so far, together with new and creative ideas.98,99

Average enhancement factors for radiative decay rates (black) and nonradiative decay rates (blue) are shown as a function of the peak wavelength of the silver-nanoparticle plasmon resonance. Results are shown for three different quantum-dot samples, with different emission peaks (indicated by the shaded areas). The Gaussian fits show that the enhancement factors reach a maximum when the emission frequency and the plasmon frequency are in resonance. From Ref. [51]. b, Illustration of a gold nanoparticle (gnp) attached to a scanning-probe tip, in proximity to a single dye molecule embedded in a polymer film. Emission from the molecule is measured as a function of wavelength (using the spectrometer and chargecoupled device, CCD) and of time (using the single-photon-counting avalanche photodiode, APD), as the tip is used to precisely control the nanoparticle-molecule separation. From Ref. [54].

Figure 5: Potential applications of emission enhancement. a, Efficient generation of single photons using a single epitaxial quantum dot within a micropost microcavity. The left shows a scanning-electron-microscope image of the microcavity, formed by etching an epitaxially-grown planar microscopy. The center shows the electric field distribution in the fundamental mode of the cavity, calculated using the finite-difference time-domain method. The right shows measured correlations between photons emitted by a quantum dot in the center of the cavity, when excited by a pulsed laser. The absence of a peak at zero time delay indicates that photons are emitted one at a time. From Ref. [65]. b, A solar thermophotovoltaic system. The top shows an illustration of the system, and the bottom shows a photograph of the system in operation. Sunlight heats a black absorber, and the heat is conducted into an emitter. Modified thermal radiation from this emitter is coupled to a photovoltaic cell, which converts the radiation into electricity. From Ref. [77].
