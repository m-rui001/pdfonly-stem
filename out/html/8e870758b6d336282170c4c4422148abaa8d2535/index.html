Combining wide-bandgap and narrow-bandgap perovskites to construct monolithic allperovskite tandem solar cells offers an avenue to increased power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) beyond 30%. However, the efficiency of all-perovskite tandem solar cells is severely constrained by the performance of low-bandgap subcell and by the sputtered transparent-conductive-oxide layer in the interconnecting layer required for solution processing of bottom subcells. Here we report a metallic tin reduced precursor solution strategy that reduces the Sn4+ (an oxidization product of Sn2+) back to Sn2+ via a comproportionation reaction, and it increases the carrier diffusion length in mixed Pb-Sn narrow-bandgap perovskites from ~ sub-micrometer for reference materials; to 3 micrometers for the new materials. This enabled us to obtain a PCE of 21.1% for 1.22-eV narrow-bandgap solar cells, together with a fill factor above 80% and a short-circuit current density higher than 32 mA cm-2. We then developed a recombination junction that avoids sputtered transparent-conductive-oxide, taking advantage of robust and compact SnO2 layers processed via atomic layer deposition. These strategies allowed us to fabricate monolithic all-perovskite tandem solar cells with high stabilized PCEs of 24.3% for smallarea devices (0.073 cm2) and of 22.3% for large-area devices (1.05 cm2). The tandem solar cells exhibited promising stability and retained 90% of their initial performance after 463 hours of operation at the maximum power point under full one-sun illumination.

Owing to long carrier diffusion lengths, low trap densities, and high tolerance to defects, lead halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have recently seen rapid advances in power conversion efficiency (PCE), reaching now the certified value of 24.2% (1–7). Combining Pb-based widebandgap perovskites (~1.8 eV) with mixed Pb-Sn based narrow-bandgap perovskites (~1.2 eV) to construct monolithic tandem solar cells has the potential to achieve even higher PCEs than single-junction PSCs (8–10) (12–15). Alloying Pb and Sn in metal halide perovskites offers a route to obtain narrow bandgaps down to 1.17 eV for Sn content of 50-60% (22–24). However, a key ingredient in these perovskites – Sn2+ is prone to the ready oxidization forming Sn4+, especially in the precursor solutions and SnI2 solids, leading to high trap densities and short diffusion lengths in tincontaining perovskites (25–27). To overcome this limitation, antioxidant additives such as SnF2, SnF2-pyrazine complex, or hydroxybenzene sulfonic acid have commonly been used to reduce the Sn2+-oxidization-related defect density in Sn-containing perovskites. Device engineering strategies such as interface engineering, enlarging grains, lattice strain, and halide alloying have been implemented in mixed Pb-Sn narrow-bandgap PSCs and achieved PCEs of 17-19%. During preparation of this manuscript, Tong et al. reported very recently that guanidinium thiocyanate promoted grain-boundary passivation increases the carrier lifetime of mixed Pb-Sn perovskites and achieved an (uncertified) PCE of 20.2% in narrow-bandgap solar cells, with a moderate Jsc value of ~30 mA cm-2. This also led to the highest PCE of 23.1% (area ~0.1 cm2) for monolithic all-perovskite tandem solar cells reported to date. In spite of these advances, the overall efficiency of all-perovskite tandem solar cells is currently limited due to the lack of high-performance narrow-bandgap back cells which typically exhibit low quantum efficiencies in the near-infrared range (12–14). The performance of monolithic all-perovskite tandems still lags behind the single-junction Pb-based PSCs. In addition, monolithic all-perovskite tandem solar cells having both high efficiency and large area (≥1 cm2), required for industrialization, have yet to be demonstrated. The (uncertified) PCE is only 13.3% for best large-area tandem device, while Pb-based single-junction PSCs have demonstrated certified PCE of 20.9% for large-area devices. Furthermore, long-term device operational stability of all-perovskite tandems has remained far inferior to that of Pb-based single-junction devices. 2

The tunnel recombination junction in monolithic all-perovskite tandems relies on a sputtered indium-tin-oxide (ITO) layer to protect the underlying layers during solution processing of the back narrow-bandgap subcells (12–15). This ITO layer not only induces parasitic absorption loss in near-infrared spectral range; but it also causes shunting between subcells which severely constrains the performance of large-area devices and prevents the cellto-cell monolithic integration in thin-film modules (31). Here we report a simple and effective tin-reduced precursor solution strategy that prevents the oxidization of Sn2+ to Sn4+ in the precursor solution of narrow-bandgap perovskites by adding a small amount of metallic tin powders: the leftover tin granules are removed before film fabrication via filtering solution. Following this strategy, we are able to achieve a long carrier diffusion length of 3 μm in mixed Pb-Sn perovskite films, whose electronic quality is comparable to high-quality full Pb-based perovskites (diffusion length of 3.5 μm and PCE higher than 22%). We obtained the highest PCE of 21.1% for 1.22-eV mixed Pb-Sn narrowbandgap PSCs, together with FF above 80% and Jsc values higher than 32 mA cm-2. We further developed a recombination junction that avoids using sputtered indium-tin-oxide by taking the advantage of a robust and compact SnO2 layer processed with atomic layer deposition. These enabled us to fabricate monolithic all-perovskite tandem solar cells with PCEs of 24.5% for small-area device (0.073 cm2) and 22.3% (certified 21.5%) for large-area device (1.05 cm2). The tandem solar cells exhibited promising stability and retained 90% of their initial performance after 463 h of operation at the maximum power point under full one-sun illumination.

Fabrication of low-Eg perovskites via TRP method We reasoned that the performance and operational stability of mixed Pb-Sn narrowbandgap PSCs suffers from the high defect density (i.e., Sn vacancies) in the bulk and surfaces of mixed Pb-Sn perovskites due to the ready oxidization of Sn2+ to Sn4+, especially happening in the precursor solutions and SnI2 solids (25, 32, 33). This leads to short charge-carrier lifetime and thus short diffusion length in tin-containing perovskite films, resulting in poor charge transport/extraction and low FF values in solar cells.

Unlike the full Sn-based perovskites, mixed Pb-Sn perovskites (in their solid film form) are well tolerant to Sn2+ oxidization even in ambient air (12, 13, 34), and the oxidation of Sn2+ to Sn4+ could mainly happen before film formation in mixed Pb-Sn perovskite systems. We posited that the trap density in mixed Pb-Sn perovskites could be reduced if the undesired Sn4+ - that is already formed in perovskite precursor solution - could be avoided in the precursor solution before film formation. Reducing the lattice defects, i.e. the Sn vacancies, in the Pb-Sn perovskites also allows one to improve the operational stability of solar cells []. We observed that as-prepared yellow perovskite precursor solution, containing a mixture of SnI2+PbI2+SnF2+FAI+MAI in mixed DMF and DMSO solvent, became orange-red quickly after exposure to air (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1). This indicates that Sn2+ readily oxidizes to Sn4+ in the precursor solution, and that the commonly used reducing agent additive SnF2 does not prevent the oxidization of Sn2+ and does not reduce Sn4+ to Sn2+ once Sn4+ forms. We reasoned that a reducing agent that is insoluble in precursor solution on its own, but becomes a part of perovskite lattice once oxidized by Sn4+ to form Sn2+, would successfully suppress the formation of traps in mixed Pb-Sn perovskites. We constructed the Frost diagram for Sn of different oxidation states (Supplementary Fig. 2) and found that metallic Sn could readily reduce Sn4+ in the precursor solution via comproportionation reaction: Sn+Sn4+ → 2Sn2+; the standard redox potential of this reaction is Eo = 0.29 V, exhibiting negative Gibbs energy ΔGo = -nFEo < 0 (35). Indeed, when we added metallic Sn powders into oxidized Pb-Sn perovskite precursor solution, the solution became bright-yellow again, even in the presence of oxygen, indicating that Sn4+ had been reduced to Sn2+ in the presence of metallic Sn (Supplementary Fig. 1). The transmittance spectra of the precursor solution further confirmed the color changes at different stages (Supplementary Fig. 2). We found that the absorption edge of the pristine precursor solution slightly blue-shifted after the introduction of Sn powders, due to the reduction of any Sn4+ formed already in the solution (even in the glovebox) in the absence of Sn powders. In comparison, the precursor solution with Sn powders maintained bright-yellow color even after exposure to air for at least two weeks. We anticipate that the formation of Sn vacancies due to the presence of Sn4+ in the precursor solution could be suppressed in the perovskite film processed from Sn4+-free precursor solution, as illustrated in Fig. 1b. 4

We fabricated mixed Pb-Sn narrow-bandgap perovskite films with a composition of MA0.3FA0.7Pb0.5Sn0.5I3 from pristine (referred to as control from hereon) and tin-reduced precursor (TRP) solutions using the antisolvent method. The metallic Sn powders (microparticles with a diameter of ~150 μm) were completely removed via filtering through a 0.2-μm PTFE membrane filter; no metallic Sn was detected in the TRP perovskite films by XPS measurements (Supplementary Fig. 3). The TRP perovskite films showed substantially reduced content of Sn4+ compared to the control films (Supplementary Fig. 3a-c). The Sn4+ in control films could arise from the precursor solids (SnI2) and further oxidization of Sn2+ to Sn4+ in the precursor solution. We found that even highest-purity (99.999%) SnI2 beads from Alfa Aesar and Sigma-Aldrich exhibited a substantial amount of Sn4+ (Supplementary Fig. 3d). We also noticed that the SnI2 beads, marked as the same purity (99.999%), exhibit different colors for materials provided by Alfa Aesar and Sigma-Aldrich (Supplementary Fig. 4), and the color of beads varied from batch to batch from the same provider.

Charge transport characteristics of low-Eg perovskites To extract the charge transport characteristics, we performed femtosecond- and nanosecondresolved optical-pump terahertz-probe (OPTP) spectroscopy, a non-destructive method of probing the photo-induced conductivity and effective charge-carrier mobility (17, 36). Fluence dependent femtosecond OPTP transients exhibited accelerated decay dynamics at higher initial photo-injected charge-carrier densities due to the enhanced contributions from bimolecular and Auger recombination (Figs. 2a and b). We calculated recombination rate constants and chargecarrier mobilities of 72 and 79 cm2 V-1 s-1 for control and TRP samples, respectively. We then performed nanosecond OPTP spectroscopy and extracted the charge-carrier lifetimes of 3 ns vs. 43 ns for control and TRP perovskite films (Fig. 2c). The much longer lifetime in TRP film agrees with the picture of a significantly lowered trap density. We derived the charge-carrier diffusion lengths (LD) as a function of the charge-carrier density n following the equation (36): LD = [μKBT/(qR)]1/2, where R = k1+nk2+n2k3 is the total recombination rate, KB is the Boltzmann constant, T is the absolute temperature, and q is the elementary charge. Under one-sun solar illumination condition (n ~1015 cm-3), the diffusion lengths of control and TRP perovskite films are 0.75 and 2.99 μm, respectively. The diffusion length of TRP narrowbandgap perovskite is several times longer than the thickness (~1 μm) required for complete incident light absorption in solar cells. The TRP mixed Pb-Sn perovskites show a diffusion length comparable to the high-quality Pb-based 1.53-eV perovskite (LD = 3.45 μm) with a

Photovoltaic performance of low-Eg perovskite solar cells We fabricated a series of mixed Pb-Sn narrow-bandgap perovskite solar cells with various absorber layer thicknesses to evaluate solar cell performance (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Table 1). The devices have an inverted planar structure comprising ITO/PEDOT:PSS/mixed Pb-Sn perovskite/C60/BCP/Cu (Supplementary Fig. 6), where PEDOT:PSS is poly(3,4ethylene dioxythiophene)-poly(styrene sulfonate), and BCP is bathocuproine. For very thin devices, i.e., at the thickness of 220 nm, both control and TRP devices are not limited by their diffusion lengths and exhibit comparable performance. As the thickness of perovskite absorber increases from 350 to 960 nm, the TRP devices exhibit considerably better performance than control devices at each thickness. The average FF value of TRP devices retains almost constant among all investigated thicknesses, and the average Jsc value gradually increases with thickness due to better light absorption using thicker absorber without compromising the carrier collection, as indicated by their EQE curves in Fig. 3b. By contrast, the average FF value of control devices gradually decreases with thickness because of their relatively short diffusion length. Correspondingly, the control devices initially show increased Jsc with thickness up to 650 nm, up to which the charge transport is not severely constrained by the diffusion length. As the thickness increases beyond their diffusion length, i.e., at 860 and 920 nm, the control

The champion tandem device exhibits a high laboratory PCE of 24.5% under reverse scan, with a high Voc of 2.013 V, a Jsc of 14.9 mA cm-2, and a high FF of 81.6%. The device delivered a certified PCE of 21.51% (Supplementary Fig. 19). Stability is another crucial parameter for PSCs. We investigated the shelf lifetime of tandem solar cells by storing the devices in a glovebox under dark and tracking the evolution of PCEs. Supplementary Fig. S20 shows that there is no obvious degradation in performance after dark storage in a nitrogen atmosphere for 1650 hours. This indicates that the stability of mixed Pb-Sn perovskites can be ensured by delicate encapsulation for field applications. We also investigated the operational stability of unencapsulated tandem cells under full 1sun illumination and MPP operation in glovebox without UV-filter. The PCE of tandem cells increased slightly in the first tens of hours of illumination, which may be related to the lightinduced defect healing in the perovskites (38–40). The tandem solar cells exhibited very promising stability for Sn-containing PSCs and still retained 90% of their initial performance after operation for 463 hours under one-sun illumination, as directly derived from the MPP tracking (Fig. 4f). The robust and compact ALD-SnO2 layer prevents the interdiffusion of ions between subcells and simultaneously function as a protection layer for the wide-bandgap subcell to ensure its photostability (which is otherwise prone to phase separation for widebandgap perovskites). The efficiency of the monolithic all-perovskite tandem solar cells is still limited by the large Voc deficit in the wide-bandgap front cell and the optical loss caused by the tunnel recombination junction. The summed EQE of the tandem solar cell is considerably lower than that of single-junction narrow-bandgap solar cell over 680-1000 nm wavelength range due to the optical reflection and parasitic absorption by the PEDOT:PSS layer in the recombination junction (Supplementary Fig. 21). Further advances in wide-bandgap perovskite solar cells and tunnel recombination junction will allow achieving PCEs beyond 25%, making all14

Methods Materials. Unless otherwise stated, all materials were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich or Alfa Aesar and used as received without further purification. SnI2 was purchased from Alfa Aesar (99.999% metals basis). The organic halide salts were purchased from GreatcellSolar (Australia). PEDOT:PSS aqueous solution was purchased from Heraeus Clevios (Germany). Tin powders were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (<150 μm, 99.5% trace metals basis, 265640). Perovskite precursor solution. To make narrow-bandgap FA0.7MA0.3Pb0.5Sn0.5I3 perovskite films with various thicknesses, the precursor solution (with various concentrations of 0.8 M, 1.2 M, 1.6 M. 1.8 M, and 2.0 M) was prepared in mixed solvents of DMF and DMSO with a volume ratio of 3:1. The molar ratios for FAI/MAI and PbI2/SnI2 were 0.7:0.3 and 0.5:0.5, respectively. The molar ratio of (FAI+MAI)/(PbI2+SnI2) was 1:1. SnF2 (10 mol% relative to SnI2) was added in the precursor solution. The precursor solution was stirred at room temperature for 2 hours and then filtered through 0.20-μm PTFE membrane before use. To reduce Sn4+ in the precursor solution, tin powders (5 mg mL-1) were added in the precursor and stirred at room temperature for 10 minutes before use. The precursor solution with remained tin powders was filtered through 0.20-μm PTFE membrane before making perovskite films. XPS measurements didn’t detect any residual metallic Sn in the perovskite films. To make wide-bandgap FA0.8Cs0.2Pb(I0.6Br0.4)3 perovskite films, the precursor solution (1.1 M) was prepared in mixed solvents of DMF and DMSO with a volume ratio of 4:1. The molar ratios for FAI/FABr/CsI/CsBr and PbI2/PbBr2 were 0.48:0.32:0.12:0.08 and 0.6:0.4, respectively. The molar ratio of (FAI+FABr+CsI+CsBr)/(PbI2+PbBr2) was 1:1. The precursor solution was stirred at 50 oC for 2 hours and then filtered through 0.20-μm PTFE membrane before use. Pb-Sn narrow-bandgap perovskite solar cell fabrication. The pre-patterned indium tin oxide glass substrates were sequentially cleaned using acetone and isopropanol. PEDOT:PSS (Al 4083) was spin-coated on ITO substrates at 4000 rpm for 30 s and annealed on a hot plate at 150 °C for 10 min in ambient air. After cooling, we transferred the substrates immediately to a nitrogen-filled glovebox for the deposition of perovskite films. The perovskite films were deposited with two-step spin coating procedures: (1) 1000 rpm for 10 s with an acceleration of 200 rpm/s and (2) 4000 rpm for 40 s with a ramp-up of 1000 rpm/s. Ethyl acetate (200 µL) was dropped on the spinning substrate during the second spin-coating step at 20 s before the end of the procedure. Ethyl acetate was chosen here since it is less toxic and more environmentallyfriendly than commonly-used chlorobenzene. The substrates were then transferred on a hotplate and heated at 100 °C for 10 min. After cooling down to room temperature, the substrates were transferred to the evaporation system. Finally, C60 (20 nm)/BCP (7 nm)/Cu (100 nm) were sequentially deposited on top of perovskite by thermal evaporation.

After cooling, the wide-bandgap perovskite films were deposited on top of PTAA with two-step spin coating procedures. The first step was 2000 rpm for 10 s with an acceleration of 200 rpm/s. The second step was 6000 rpm for 40 s with a ramp-up of 2000 rpm/s. Chlorobenzene (100 µL) was dropped on the spinning substrate during the second spin-coating step at 20 s before the end of the procedure. The substrates were then transferred on a hotplate and heated at 100 °C for 15 min. After cooling down to room temperature, the substrates were transferred to the evaporation system, and 20-nm-thick C60 film was subsequently deposited on top by thermal evaporation at a rate of 0.2 Å/s. The substrates were then exposed to air and transferred to the atomic layer deposition (ALD) system to deposit a 20 nm SnO2 layer at low temperatures. After ALD deposition, the substrates were exposed to air again and transferred to the thermal evaporation system to deposit an ultra-thin layer of Au clusters layer (~1 nm) on SnO2. PEDOT:PSS layers were spin-cast on top of SnO2protected front cell and annealed in air at 120oC for 20min. After the substrates had cooled, we immediately transferred the substrates to a nitrogen-filled glovebox for the deposition of narrow-bandgap perovskite films with identical procedures used for the single-junction devices. We found that the thickness of Pb-Sn narrow-bandgap perovskite film was slightly thinner than that of the film in the single-junction device even we used identical process and precursor solution. Femtosecond and nanosecond optical-pump-THz probe (OPTP) spectroscopy. For femtosecond OPTP spectroscopy, a commercial Ti:Sapphire amplified laser (Libra, Coherent) with a pulse duration of 90 fs, center wavelength of 800 nm and repetition rate of 1 kHz was used to generate THz radiation via optical rectification in a 0.5-mm-thick ZnTe(110) nonlinear single crystal and detect it using free space electro-optic sampling in a 0.5-mm-thick ZnTe(110) crystal. Perovskite thin films deposited on z-cut quartz substrates were photoexcited at 800 nm with fluences ranging from 1.5 – 50 μJ cm-2. The change in THz amplitude was monitored as a function of the time delay between the 800-nm optical pump pulse and THz probe pulse using a translation stage. For nanosecond OPTP spectroscopy, we used a frequency-doubled subnanosecond laser (Picolo AOT MOPA, InnoLas) with a pulse duration of ~ 0.8 ns to excite the samples at 532 nm. The laser was synchronized to the THz probe pulse with an electronic delay generator (SRS DG645, Stanford Research System). All measurements were carried out in a nitrogen-purged environment to avoid potential degradation of perovskite films in ambient air. As the excitation fluence increased, the OPTP transients exhibited accelerated decay dynamics at higher initial photoinjected charge-carrier densities due to enhanced contributions from bimolecular recombination and Auger recombination. We extracted the rate constants associated with different recombination mechanisms by global fits to these transients according to the following rate equation (36): 𝑑𝑛(𝑡) = −𝑘3 𝑛3 − 𝑘2 𝑛2 − 𝑘1 𝑛 𝑑𝑡 The femtosecond OPTP transients did not exhibit obvious decay dynamics at low excitation density, which makes it difficult to extract accurate rate constant k1. To extract k1 as well as charge-carrier lifetime, we then performed nanosecond OPTP spectroscopy by changing the photoexcitation source while keeping the sample inside the measurement chamber. The samples were photoexcited at a low fluence to reduce the effects of bimolecular and Auger recombination. The nanosecond OPTP transients were fitted with a mono-exponential decay function. The mobility values were determined using the maximum value of -ΔT/T in the linear regime as described in previous literature (36). We derived the charge-carrier diffusion length LD according to the equation: LD = [μkBT/(qR)]1/2 as a function of the charge-carrier density n, where R = k1+nk2+n2k3 is the total recombination rate, kB is the Boltzmann constant, T is temperature, and q is the elementary charge. A carrier density of 1015 (one-Sun condition) was used as a representative of typical operating conditions for a solar cell. Space charge limited current (SCLC).
