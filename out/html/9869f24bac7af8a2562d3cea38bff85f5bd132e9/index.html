More, Guofeng Wang, Zhenxing Feng*, Zhenbo Wang*, and Gang Wu* Abstract: FeN4 moieties embedded in partially graphitized carbon are the most efficient platinum group metal-free active sites for oxygen reduction reaction in acidic proton exchange membrane fuel cells. However, their formation mechanisms have been remaining elusive for decades. Because the Fe-N bond formation process always convolutes with uncontrolled carbonization and nitrogen doping during high-temperature treatments. Here, we elucidate the Fe-N4 site formation mechanisms through hosting Fe ions into a nitrogen-doped carbon followed by a controlled thermal activation. Among studied hosts, the ZIF-8-dervied nitrogen-doped carbon is an ideal model with well-defined nitrogen doping and porosity. This approach is able to deconvolute Fe-N bond formation from complex carbonization and nitrogen doping, which correlates Fe-N bond properties with activity and stability of FeN4 sites as a function of thermal activation temperatures. The evolution of catalyst performance of FeN4 sites driven by thermal activation is associate with two main factors: (i) the increase of active site density due to the transformation of ultra-fine FeOx particles to atomically dispersed FeN4 sites; (ii) the intrinsic change of atomistic Fe-N bonds with enhanced strength and shortened length.

Introduction Development of proton-exchange membrane fuel cells (PEMFCs) is crucial for implementing efficient and environmental friendly hydrogen for energy sustainability. However, substantial platinum group metal (PGM) catalysts used for sluggish oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) at the cathode must be significantly reduced or replaced by earth-abundant elements for large-scale applications of PEMFCs.[1-2] Recently, tremendous efforts have been made to explore the most promising transition metal and nitrogen codoped carbon (M-N-C, M: Fe, Co, or/and Mn) catalysts,[3-11] in which atomically dispersed MN4 sites are likely the active sites for the ORR in acidic electrolytes. Despite recent advances in activity[10, 12] and stability,[13-14] the synthesis method is still limited to traditional co-pyrolysis of metal, nitrogen, and carbon sources using template (carbon blacks and silica)[15-18] and self-templated

Because of the complex synthesis procedures, the nature of such MN4 active sites and their formation mechanisms have been remaining elusive for a few decades.[9, 21] Among studied metals, atomically dispersed FeN4-rich carbon catalysts have demonstrated the most promising activity and stability in both aqueous acids and PEMFCs. However, elucidating the formation process of the FeN4 active site remains grand challenges in prior studies due to following reasons. First, the active site formation process is simultaneously accompanied with complex carbonization and nitrogen doping during hightemperature pyrolysis.[22] Second, metallic crystalline species often coexist with the FeN4 active sites in previous catalysts and the role of metal is unclear during the pyrolysis.[23-25] Third, the study of atomically dispersed FeN4 active sites requires advanced spectroscopic techniques and electron microscopy at the atomic resolution. Recently, atomically dispersed Fe-N-C catalysts with high activity were successfully synthesized via chemical doping[2628] and space-confinement strategy.[29-30] The elimination of metallic crystals significantly simplifies the catalyst system, which provides ideal platforms for understanding active site structure and developing advanced synthetic chemistry for rational design of catalysts with enhanced performance[3]. Advances in characterization techniques including X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS), Mössbauer spectroscopy, and aberrationcorrected electron microscopy offer new opportunities to study active site structures at the atomic level in catalysts.[8, 10-11, 26, 31-32]. RESEARCH ARTICLE However, the direct exploration of the formation mechanism of active sites is still hindered by the accompanied carbonization and nitrogen doping processes during pyrolysis. Here, we elucidate the formation mechanism of FeN4 sites within an ideal carbon model excluding carbonization and nitrogen doping processes. The zinc-based zeolitic imidazolate framework-8 (ZIF-8) derived nanocarbon (ZIF-NC), which possesses unique N doping especially pyridinic-N and dominant micropores, as an effective host to adsorb Fe ions followed by a thermal activation under N2 atmosphere to form Fe-N coordination in a controlled manner. Through correlating the measured ORR activity/stability with Fe-N coordination structures formed at different temperatures, we found that (i) highly active FeN4 sites with optimal coordination numbers and bond length formed at 700oC are thermally-driven from inactive Fe-N configurations formed at room temperature; (ii) ultra-fine FeOx particles (< 2 nm) trapped by the ZIF-NC transform into atomically dispersed and active FeN4 sites starting at a relatively low temperature of 400oC.

Results and Discussion 2.1 Thermally stable nitrogen-doped carbon for hosting Fe-N coordination An effective method was developed to investigate FeN4 formation in an atomically dispersed Fe-N-C catalyst at various thermal activation temperature (Figure 1). First, nitrogen-doped nanocarbon (i.e., ZIF-NC) with a 3D porous structure was obtained by carbonizing ZIF-8 nanocrystals at 1100oC. Then, Fe3+ ions were adsorbed onto or/and into the ZIF-NC carbon at room temperature, labelled as ZIF-NC-nFe3+, where the n represents the concentration (mg/ml) of FeCl3 in isopropanol solutions used for the adsorption. Next, thermal activation was subsequently conducted at various temperatures (T: 200-1100oC) under a flowing N2 gas (~100 ml/min) to prepare catalysts (ZIF-NC-nFeT) with different Fe-N coordination structures. No post-treatment such as acid leaching was performed after the thermal activation. The overall morphology of ZIF-NC hosts is displayed in Supporting Information Figure S1a-b, showing homogeneous carbon particles (~50 nm) with a polyhedral feature. The brightfield STEM (BF-STEM) image (Supporting Information, Figure S1c) revealed abundant micropores and step-edges at the surface,[8] which is crucial for hosting Fe3+ ions during the adsorption. As shown in Supporting Information Figure S1d-o, ultra-fine Fe-oxide nanoparticles with a size typically less than 2 nm formed after the adsorption step and readily decomposed at 400oC, suggesting a thermally-driven transformation of Fe aggregates to atomically dispersed Fe sites. During a thermal activation up to 1100oC, the polyhedral shape and porous structure of carbon particle hosts are remained. X-ray diffraction

(XRD) patterns of catalysts obtained at different thermal activation temperatures indicate identical carbon structure and the absence of Fe-containing crystalline phases (Supporting Information, Figure S2). The employed ZIF-NC carbon host processes a high BET surface area (403 m2/g) and dominant micropore volume (0.130 cm3/g, Supporting Information, Figure S3 and Table S1), which is ideal for adsorbing Fe3+ ions. These micropores then disappeared after the adsorption of Fe3+ ions, but recovered after a thermal activation at 400oC. This suggests that Fe-oxide nanoparticles were firstly aggregated and located in the micropores and then gradually decomposed to atomically dispersed species (Supporting Information, Figure S3). Raman spectra (Supporting Information, Figure S4) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis further indicate no obvious changes of carbon structure and nitrogen doping, respectively, during thermal activation up to 700oC (Supporting Information, Table. S2). Thus, the thermally stable carbon structure and nitrogen doping in the ZIF-NC carbon host is highly desirable to exclusively study the Fe-N coordination at different temperatures. It should be noted that slightly increased degree of graphitization and loss of nitrogen dopants occurred at 1100oC (Supporting Information, Table S3), which is discussed in later section. 2.2 Structure evolution of Fe-N coordination during thermal activation Atomic resolution medium-angle annular dark-field (MAADF) images of the ZIF-NC-Fe3+ samples (after the Fe3+ ion adsorption and before thermal activation) showed the presence of both ultrafine nanoparticles and atomically dispersed single metal atoms. (Figure 2a-b). EEL point spectrum confirmed that the ultra-fine particle (< 2 nm) is FeOx, which Fe and O coexist at atomic level (Supporting Information, Figure S5a-b). They mainly located in the micropores of carbon hosts as evidenced by N2 isothermal adsorption-desorption test and MAADF-STEM image (Supporting Information, Figure S6). Abundant bright spots associated with heavy single metal sites are apparent. However, due to their mobility under electron beam, it is hard to determine their chemical nature. When the electron probe with 1Å resolution was placed on the bright spot (Figure 2c), only showing signals assigning to carbon and nitrogen in the EEL point spectrum (Figure 2d). This indicates no stable Fe-N configurations. After a thermal activation at 400oC, all FeOx ultra-fine particles disappeared throughout the sample (Supporting Information Figure S7) and the density of atomically dispersed atoms virtually increased (Figure 2e-f). This suggests the transformation from FeOx particles to single Fe atoms is likely. The thermal activation at 400oC is sufficient and can stabilize the Fe-N configuration.

This is an important characteristic that the stable Fe-N configuration formed at 400oC. A more uniform dispersion of Fe sites was observed, when the thermal activation temperature was further increased to 700oC (Figure 2i-j). Similar to 400oC, the stable Fe-N coordination is verified by using the EEL point spectrum (Figure 2k-l). Considerable amount of atomically dispersed Fe sites were found especially at the edge of carbon planes, which is likely more active than bulk-hosted FeN4[33] (Supporting Information, Figure S8). When the temperature was increased to 1100oC, the density of atomic metal sites is significantly decreased particular at the carbon edge. The obvious nitrogen loss observed by energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) and XPS further suggests that the destruction of FeN4 active sites might start from the removal of nitrogen dopants (Supporting Information, Table S2). This proved that ultra-high heating temperatures such as 1100oC can reconstruct the carbon structure and decompose Fe-Nx active sites (Figure 2m-n). However, significant Fe-N coordination remains very stable evidenced by the EEL point spectrum (Figure 2o-p). Controlled experiments were performed by adsorbing Fe3+ ions at various concentration in solutions from 0.5 to 5.0 mg/ml (i.e., ZIF-NC-5.0Fe3+). Only limited number of ultra-fine FeOx cluster in ZIF-NC-5.0Fe3+ can transform into atomically dispersed Fe-Nx structure, very likely due to the limitation of nitrogen dopant in the ZIF-NC carbon host (Supporting Information, Figure S9, Figure S10 and Table S4). Given the observation that Fe-N interaction can prevent the metal sites from forming metal oxide

clusters, the thermal stability of Fe-N structure is higher than FeOx, which is the driving force for the transformation during thermal activation and was discussed in modeling section. To further examine the structure evolution of Fe-N coordination during thermal activation, XAS analysis[5, 27, 34-35] was performed at Fe K-edge. The XANES spectra (Figure 3a) clearly shows that the absorption edge of ZIF-NC-0.5Fe3+ aligns with that of Fe2O3, suggesting that the oxidation state of Fe in the ZIF-NC0.5Fe3+ is close to 3+. This is in good agreement with the Fe3+ salts used for the adsorption. The peak around 2~3 Å in the Fourier-transformed EXAFS of ZIF-NC-0.5Fe3+ (Figure 3b) is assigned to the Fe-Fe distance in Fe oxides,[5, 36] which is also verified by using MAADF-STEM images. As the temperature is increased to 400oC, Fe was reduced, which could be caused by the formation of Fe-N bonding. Although it is hard for EXAFS to distinguish Fe-N and Fe-O scattering unambiguously[37] The EXAFS data (Supporting Information, Figure S10) at high k (>8 Å1) becomes noise, further increasing the ambiguity of EXAFS to distinguish the Fe-O and Fe-N. However, according to our previous analysis by using Mössbauer spectroscopy [6, 38-39], Fe-N coordination is more likely. The peak intensity at 2~3 Å (assigned to Fe-Fe scattering paths) is significantly decreased at temperatures higher than 400oC. This indicates that nearly all the FeOx decomposed into atomically dispersed Fe sites, which is observed using MAADF-STEM imaging as well. The ZIF-NC0.5Fe-700 sample, after a thermal activation at 700oC, showed an increased pre-edge intensity (inset of Figure 3a) that is a sign of lower symmetry around Fe atoms or shorter Fe-N bond length in Fe-N4 configurations.[37] The Fe-N coordination number is around 4 at and above temperatures of 400oC, implying a mild thermal activation is able to facilitate the formation of FeN4 sites. However, as the temperature is up to 1100oC, the coordination number of Fe-N bond is reduced (Supporting Information, Table S5). This opposite trend is also observed in STEM-EDS experiments,

The effect of thermal activation was further studied by using XPS. The N content in the ZIF-NC host is 4.5 at.% (Table S2) and the N 1s spectrum shows two well-separated peaks (Figure 3c), which were originated from the dominated pyridinic-N (398.5 eV) and graphitic-N (401.1 eV).[36, 40] After the adsorption of Fe ions, the peak at low bonding energy become broader and a new peak (399.4 eV) corresponding to Fe-N[8, 41] appeared with a percentage of 8.5% (Supporting Information, Table S6). This indicates that the existence of Fe-N configurations in the ZIF-NC0.5Fe3+ even before any thermal activation. After a thermal activation, the Fe 2p peak shows a shift to lower bonding energy, suggesting the strengthening of Fe-N interactions (Figure 3d). The identical C 1s spectra indicated no obvious change of carbon structure during thermal activation (Figure 3e). When the temperature was increased from 700 to 1100oC, a significant loss of pyridinic-N was observed, providing a solid evidence that the destruction of FeN4 active sites is mainly due to the removal of pyridinic-N.

2.3 Theoretical studies of structural evolution of FeN4 sites We further carried out molecular dynamics simulation and firstprinciples calculations to explain our experimental observation on the conversion from FeOx ultra-fine particles to atomically dispersed FeN4 sites. The possible structural changes of FeN4 sites at different thermal activation temperature were also simulated to elucidate the gradually improved ORR activity and stability up to the optimal 700oC followed by an opposite decline at 1100oC. As shown in Supporting Information, Figure S11, DFT calculations predict that it requires 5.38 eV to remove an Fe atom from an FeOx particle. However, if the removed Fe atom is adsorbed in a vacancy surrounding a pre-existing N4 vacancy moiety, the system energy would significantly decrease by 6.44 eV. Therefore, it is thermodynamically favorable for ultra-fine FeOx particles to lose Fe atoms and form more stable FeN4 sites.

In addition, Ab initio molecular dynamics simulations at 400C indicate that a single Fe atom would diffuse at the carbon surface until it is embedded into a vacancy defect with N4 moiety forming a stable FeN4 site (Figure 4a). As indicated in Supporting Information, Figure S12, the driving force for this process is the potential energy decrease of the FeN4 as compared to that of an isolated N4 vacancy carbon defect and a Fe atom. Thus, simulations provide a mechanistic understanding on the FeN4 site formation from the decomposition of FeOx and the diffusion of adsorbed Fe ions at carbon planes driven by thermal energy. FeN4 sites are thermally stable and have been predicted to exhibit ORR activity,[42] which explains the origin of our observed ORR activity at relatively low thermal activation temperature. Regarding the structural change of FeN4 sites with an increase of thermal activation temperatures, we introduced a strain concept based on well-established knowledge of ORR electrocatalysis on precious metals.[43-44] This effort aims at further simulating the effect of FeN bond length on ORR activity. As elaborated in previous studies,[45-48] a graphene sheet has an abnormal thermal expansion behavior with a negative thermal expansion coefficient (TEC) and would form a nonplanar structure at elevated

temperatures. Given the experimental observation that the lower symmetry around Fe atom and shorter Fe-N bond length in Fe-Nx configurations at 700oC determined by using XAS analysis (Figure 3a), we therefore assume that, with the gradually increasing temperatures, the carbon layer embedded with FeN4 sites leads to off-plane ripples, therefore causing a local contraction strain in these Fe-N bonds. Modeling this thermal behavior, we constructed the FeN4 embedded graphene to have varying strains for the Fe-N bonds (Supporting Information, Figure S13). Furthermore, as shown in Figure 4b and Supporting Information Figure S14, we used the DFT calculation to predict the adsorption energies of ORR species (i.e., O2, OOH, O and OH), the free energy evolution of ORR, and the activation energy for OOH dissociation on these FeN4 sites with strained Fe-N bonds. In the Supporting Information, Table S7 and Figure S15a, we compared the off-plane distortion (∆h) of carbon layers and the oxygen adsorption energy of the modelled FeN4 sites. At first, we used the value of the limiting potential defined as the maximum applied electrode potential to make ORR thermodynamically favorable, as a parameter gauging the ORR activity of the differently strained FeN4 sites.[49-50]

In principle, a higher value of the limiting potential corresponds to better ORR activity. Supporting Information Figure S15b presents that limiting potentials of ORR decrease with a shrinking in the Fe-N bond, suggesting not being thermodynamically favorable for the ORR. However, Supporting Information Figure S15c indicates that the FeN4 sites with contracted Fe-N bonds have lower activation energy to break O-O bond, which is kinetically favorable for the ORR. As depicted in Figure 4c-d, shortening of the Fe-N bonds also leads to changes in electronic structure, such as positive shifting of the 3d-orbitals of central Fe and larger charge transfer from Fe to adjacent N, which facilitate oxygen adsorption on the FeN4 sites. Consequently, a small degree of Fe-N bond contraction would facilitate the O2 adsorption and subsequent O-O bond breaking process during the ORR, but a large degree of Fe-N bond contraction could make the ORR thermodynamically unfavorable on the FeN4 site. Hence, the theoretical calculation provides a reasonable explanation to our experimental observation that the ORR activity is increased with temperature up to 700C likely associated with a small degree of Fe-N bond contraction. We also evaluated that a very high thermal energy such as 1100°C is required to initiate the loss of N from stable FeN4 sites (Supporting Information, Figure S16). 2.4 ORR catalytic properties

Catalytic activity. The ORR activity and four-electron selectivity (H2O2 yield) of all catalysts were assessed using a rotating ringdisk electrode (RRDE) in 0.5 M H2SO4 electrolyte. Without Fe doping, ZIF-NC only showed poor activity (Figure 5a). The H2O2 yield of ZIF-NC catalyst is above 15% (Figure 5b), indicating a dominant two-electron pathway. This is in a good agreement of typical catalyst properties of metal-free CNx sites in acids.[51] After the adsorption of Fe3+ ions, the half-wave potential (E1/2) of ZIFNC-0.5Fe3+ showed a negative shift likely due to the blocking of micropores. As shown in Supporting Information, Figure S17, the ultra-fine FeOx particles introduced during adsorption step can be dissolved in acids during cyclic voltammetry tests. However, the FeNx sites formed before thermal activation are acid-resistant, showing stable and well-defined Fe2+/Fe3+ redox peaks in CVs. This is also confirmed by the positive correlation of increasing peak areas of Fe2+/Fe3+ redox with an increase of the adsorption concentration of Fe3+ ions in ZIF-NC-nFe3+ samples (Supporting Information, Figure S18). During thermal activation of the ZIF-NC0.5Fe3+, ORR activity is increased gradually when the temperature is increased to 700oC. Even a low temperature at 400oC can generate relatively high activity (E1/2 =0.77~0.79 V), which is also proved by the Tafel plots listed in Supporting Information, Figure S19 and Table S8. When the temperature is higher than 700oC, the activity gradually decreased, possibly due to the loss of active sites as discussed above (Supporting Information, Figure S20). Considering Zn residuals detected in ZIF-NC, its effect on Fe-N4 bond formation and associated ORR activity were further studied by performing controlled experiments, which verified the insignificant effect of Zn on the activity evolution during the thermal activation (Supporting Information, Figure S21). Fellinger et al.[52] also proved that Zn-Nx sites are inactive for the ORR in acid media. To elucidate the contribution of FeOx, acid leaching was performed for the ZIF-NC-0.5Fe3+ (obtained after Fe3+ adsorption and before thermal activation), which contains significant ultrafine FeOx particles. The obtained new sample was labelled ZIFNC-0.5Fe3+-AL. As shown in Supporting Information, Figure S22, the activity of ZIF-NC-0.5Fe3+-AL still showed a significant improvement during thermal activation and the E1/2 of ZIF-NC0.5Fe-AL-700 reached up to 0.81 V, which is only ~30 mV lower than that of the regular ZIF-NC-0.5Fe-700. In addition to the direct conversion from FeOx cluster to atomically dispersed FeN4 sites, majority acidic resistant FeNx species formed at room temperature can upgrade to active FeN4 sites during the thermal

activation. Moreover, the gap at E1/2 between the ZIF-NC-0.5FeAL-400 and the ZIF-NC-0.5Fe-AL-700 catalysts is 60 mV, comparable to that between the ZIF-NC-0.5Fe-400 and the ZIFNC-0.5Fe-700 (70 mV). This indicates that the transformation from FeOx clusters to atomically dispersed Fe-Nx sites already completes at 400°C and not significantly contributed to the activity enhancement from 400°C to 700°C. We also found that extending the thermal activation from one to three hours leads to no difference of ORR activity (Supporting Information, Figure S23). The effect of Fe content was also studied to generate insightful understanding on the formation mechanism of FeN4 sites. As shown in Supporting Information, Figure S24, the density of adsorbed Fe3+ into carbon hosts is not linear with the Fe3+ concentration in solution. However, it is still possible to vary the adsorbed Fe content into ZIF-NC-nFe3+, especially at relatively low concentration region (n ≤ 0.5 mg/ml). Figure 5c shows the ORR activity of ZIF-NC-nFe-700 catalysts obtained by adsorbing different content of Fe3+ ions. When n is smaller than 0.5, the activity initially showed positive correlation with Fe3+ concentration. All of Fe ions seem to be utilized for the FeN4 site formation and able to increase active site density. This is the first time to realize the active site density control for Fe-N-C catalysts without changing the carbon structure and nitrogen doping. Using

When we further increase the Fe content above n=0.5, only a part of FeOx can further convert into FeN4 during thermal activation (Supporting Information, Figure S25). Excess Fe aggregates negatively affect ORR activity.[6] The role of nitrogen doping during the active site formation was further studied by using nitrogen-free Ketjenblack (KJ), NH3 treated KJ, and nitrogen-doped reduced graphene oxide (rGO) as carbon hosts (Supporting Information, Figure S26). The types and position of nitrogen doping along with porosity of carbon are crucial for FeN4 active site formation. After optimizing the adsorbed Fe content and thermal activation temperature, the best-performing ZIF-NC-0.5Fe-700 catalyst showed an E1/2 of 0.84 V when the loading is 0.6 mg/cm2 in a RDE test, which presents one of the best Fe-N-C catalysts reported to date (Supporting Information, Table S9). Fuel cell performance. The best-performing ZIF-NC-0.5Fe-700 catalyst is further studied as a cathode in membrane electrode assemblies (MEAs) for real fuel cell performance evaluation. Under the standard 1.0 bar pressure for H2 and O2, the MEA yielded an open circuit voltage (OCV) up to 0.98 V and generated a current density of 0.030 A/cm2 at 0.9 VIR-free (Figure 5d and Supporting Information Figure S27). The value is among the highest one reported in literature (Supporting Information, Table S10), which is approaching to the U.S. DOE activity target (0.044 A/cm2 @ 0.9 V)[2]. Using practical 1.0 bar air, current densities generated at 0.8 and 0.7 V are 0.075 and 0.302 A/cm2, respectively, comparable to the record performance of Fe-N-C catalysts in MEAs.[1, 6] Catalyst stability. Stability of FeNx sites was studied using a variety of protocols to determine the effect of thermal activation on Fe-N bond strength. At first, tests by holding catalysts at a high potential of 0.85 V were carried out for 50 hours in O2 saturated 0.5 M H2SO4 (Supporting Information, Figure S28). The majority of activity loss is irreversible likely due to the possible demetalation of FeNx active sites[31, 53] and carbon oxidation[14, 5456] for the catalysts activated at different temperature(i.e., 400, 700, and 1100oC). The current loss of the ZIF-NC-0.5Fe-700 catalyst is 3.4% per 0.1 C electric charge passing the catalyst during the ORR, which is much smaller than that of the ZIF-NC-0.5Fe-400 (11.3%). Given the identical carbon structures in catalysts from 400 and 700oC (Supporting Information, Figure S29), the different degradation rate suggests that the FeN4 sites activated at 700 oC are intrinsically more stable than those at 400oC. The activity loss of the ZIF-NC-0.5Fe-1100 (3.8% per 0.1 C) is comparable to the ZIF-NC-0.5Fe-700 (3.4% per 0.1 C) suggesting similar stability of FeN4 in catalysts after thermal activation above 700oC. Moreover, accelerated stress tests (ASTs) were also performed by cycling potentials from 0.6 to 1.0 V for 30,000 cycles in both N2 and O2-saturated 0.5 M H2SO4 (Supporting Information, Figure S30). E1/2 losses of the ZIF-NC-0.5Fe-700 catalyst are 25 and 31 mV in N2 and O2 environments, respectively. In contrast, the ZIF-NC-0.5Fe-400 catalyst lost 21 mV in N2 and 40 mV in O2saturated electrolytes. Their similar losses in N2 suggest that, if

no ORR occurring, those FeN4 sites have similar electrochemical stability in acidic electrolytes. However, the larger difference in the case of O2 indicates that the higher temperature helped to stabilize FeN4 active sites, which is in good agreement with the results obtained by holding at 0.85 V. To further study the activity loss related to carbon oxidation, high potential cycling (1.0-1.5 V) in N2-saturated 0.5 M H2SO4 was also carried out (Supporting Information, Figure S31). After 5,000 cycles, capacitances of the ZIF-NC-0.5Fe-700 and the ZIF-NC-0.5Fe-400 catalysts are increased about 84%, indicating serious and similar carbon oxidation. Both their losses of E1/2 are around 30 mV, indicating degradation related to carbon corrosion is independent of intrinsic stability of their FeN4 sites. High degree of graphitization in catalyst is definitely beneficial for stability enhancement, as evidenced by the most stable ZIF-NC-0.5Fe-1100 catalyst. Thus, during the ORR, the intrinsic stability of FeN4 sites is associated with Fe-N bond length, coordination numbers, and applied strain, which is possible due to the dynamic in-plane/outplane change of FeN4 sites. High activation temperature (> 700oC) is favorable to form stable FeN4 sites with short length and optimal coordination but with less active site density due to the loss of nitrogen dopants.

Conclusion In this work, a model system was established to elucidate formation mechanism of FeN4 active sites embedded into carbon phases. Fe ions are adsorbed into a carbon host with controlled carbon nanostructures, porosity, and nitrogen dopants, which is carbonized from ZIF-8 precursors. One of major advantages of this method is able to separate the formation of FeN4 from complex carbonization and nitrogen doping during the catalyst synthesis at high temperatures. This allows us to fine tune the FeN4 site structures and density without changing carbon matrix and nitrogen doping. Through varying thermal activation temperatures, Fe content, and types of carbon hosts, the corresponding FeN4 sites were studied to correlate to their catalytic activity and stability for the ORR in acids. FeNx configurations can form at room-temperature but appear low intrinsic activity. Differing to previous understanding, only a thermal activation at relatively low temperature (400oC) is required to form active FeN4 sites, which gradually becomes optimal at 700oC with ideal Fe-N4 coordination and local structures. Overall, the evolution of activity and stability of FeN4 sites during thermal activation is associated with two critical factors: (i) the increase of active site density due to the transformation of ultra-fine FeOx particles to atomically dispersed FeN4 sites and (ii) the change of intrinsic Fe-N bonds associated with strengthening bonding strength and shortening length from room temperature to the optimal 700oC. Further increased heating temperatures often leads to the loss of doped nitrogen, causing a decline of activity. Importantly, precise control of the density of FeN4 active sites can be realized without changing the carbon structure and morphologies, providing an ideal platform for mechanistic studies. This study provides fundamental understanding of FeN4 site formation and updates our knowledge that high temperature > 800oC is not necessary for generating FeN4 active sites for the ORR. In addition, the adsorption approach is proved effective to synthesize high-performance Fe-N-C catalysts. The optimal
