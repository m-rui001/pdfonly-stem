Since the discovery of Dirac physics in graphene, research in 2D materials has exploded with the aim of finding new materials and harnessing their unique and tunable electronic and optical properties. The follow-on work on 2D dielectrics and semiconductors has led to the emergence and development of hexagonal boron nitride, black phosphorus, and transition metal disulfides. However, the spectrum of good insulating materials is still very narrow. Likewise, 2D materials exhibiting correlated phenomena such as superconductivity, magnetism, and ferroelectricity have yet to be developed or discovered. These properties will significantly enrich the spectrum of functional 2D materials, particularly in the case of high phase transition temperatures. They will also advance a fascinating fundamental frontier of size- and proximity effects on correlated ground states. Here, a broad family of layered metal thio(seleno)phosphate materials that are moderate to wide bandgap semiconductors with incipient ionic conductivity and a host of ferroic properties are reviewed. We argue that this material class has the potential to merge the sought-after properties of complex oxides with electronic functions of 2D and quasi-2D electronic materials as well as to create new avenues for both applied and fundamental materials research in structural and magnetic correlations.

Graphene ignited the current era of 2D materials, which are widely considered to be the next generation of electronic, chemical, and structural materials comprising 2D sheets, stacked heterostructures, and thin films of layered compounds. [1–4] In the past few years, a host of promising 2D materials has been extensively researched to augment the lack of electronic bandgap in graphene for device fabrication A survey of the electronic functions presented by 2D materials reveals that metal, insulating, and semiconducting properties are now represented by at least one 2D compound. However, good insulators, for the most part, are represented by clays and boron nitride, the latter with a bandgap of 5–6 eV.[5] Boron nitride is arguably the only insulator to be successfully established in 2D form, likely to be soon joined by the related compound gallium nitride.[6] Although the lack of dangling bonds and commensurability with the graphene lattice make nitrides superior to SiO2 for use as a substrate for devices, they are linear dielectrics with fairly limited functionality. Most other 2D non-metals have narrow bandgaps that make them promising semiconductors. Black phosphorus has a narrow gap of 0.3 eV and is noted for its high anisotropy within a layer where properties depend on whether they are measured along the light or heavy effective mass directions.[7] While promising for infrared optoelectronics, bulk black phosphorus is not useful for shorter wavelength applications. Similarly, transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs) also have a small bandgap that limits potential device applications at higher energies. [8] Therefore, what is missing from the picture is a range of materials with intermediate bandgaps from 1.6 to 4 eV that would occupy the space between TMDs and boron nitride. From a general point of view, these materials must have chemical bonding that is intermediate between covalent and ionic, which implies decreasing their atomic density and increasing the difference between electronegativity of constituent ions. Both of these trends would also imply that such materials attain incipient or even finite ionic conductivity at moderate temperatures. In this Review, a family of metal thio(seleno)phosphates (MTPs) are explored, in which metal cations stabilize a [P2S(Se)6]4– framework forming layers that are weakly bonded together through van der Waals interactions. Some of these compounds also exhibit ferroic correlations of atomic positions and the corresponding ferroic ground states and associated phase transitions; yet others possess magnetic ordering. The ferroic correlations of atomic positions and magnetic moments, all of which are sensitively dependent on the structural aspects and ionic substitution, bear close resemblance to sought-after properties of complex oxides. Here these properties are combined in MTPs which contain the flexibility associated with a van der Waals structure. In our view, this class of materials may contribute to the next major frontier in lowdimensional materials. As such, MTP compounds can dramatically expand the range of practically useful functionalities of 2D materials via proximity effects. 1.3 Electronic Structure of MTP Compounds

Some bandgaps of the generally semiconducting MTP compounds have been measured (Table 3), with values ranging from 1.2–3.4 eV. The gaps are smaller in the selenides than in the sulfides, as expected from the relative electronegativity of the two elements. From a survey of band structure calculations performed on the MTP compounds several general points can be made: 1) compounds with partially filled d-orbitals order magnetically and appear to be Mott insulators, 2) there is a sizeable bandgap present in all MTP materials on the order of 1.3–3.5 eV, 3) the size of the bandgap is highly sensitive to the metal cations present, and 4) the character of the bonding between the metal cation and the (P2S6)4– anion is likely mostly ionic, but not completely so. Figure 6a shows the electronic density of states (DOS) calculated for Mg 2P2S6 using the linear muffin tin orbital method and the atomic sphere approximation (TB-LMTO-ASA). Mg2P2S6 contains no d-electrons, making the DOS somewhat simpler to calculate and interpret than, say, Fe 2P2S6 or CuInP2S6. The valence band is composed primarily of S and Mg states, while the conduction band is mainly S and P in character. In materials with filled d-shells, like Zn2P2S6, the sharply localized d-levels appear well below the Fermi energy (Figure 6b).[93] When partially filled d-levels are present the details of the electronic structure near the Fermi level are determined by the crystal field splitting of the d-orbitals and the filling fractions. 2. Materials Synthesis 2.1 Synthesis Routes

The first synthesis of MTP compounds was by Friedel[9] and, later, Ferrand.[10] In the former example, the authors reacted P4S10 with Fe to produce Fe2P2S6. In the latter example, using the same technique, Zn, Cd, and Ni thiophosphates were obtained by Ferrand. These materials were largely overlooked for several decades until Klingen et al.[87,107,108] and Nitsche and Wild[109,110] began extensive studies in the mid 1960’s through the mid 1970’s.

Most syntheses of both the metal thio- and selenophosphates are accomplished through simply combining the elements in the appropriate stoichiometries in a fused silica ampoule, heating to a temperature below the material decomposition temperature for time periods of several days to several weeks or months, and then cooling at a slow rate. This type of reaction mechanism relies on vapor transport of the species and can result in the creation of thin single crystals. P, S, and Se all have low melting points and high vapor pressures (and low boiling points in the cases of P and S) and presumably act as the vapor transport agents. Se melts at 220oC but has a relatively high boiling point of 684oC, above the decomposition temperature of many of the selenium MTP compounds. Temperatures employed in the synthesis reactions depend on the material in question, specifically between the boiling points of P and S and the material’s decomposition temperature. For the sulfide compounds, they can vary from 400–850oC; [43,109,111] for the selenide compounds reaction temperatures span the range of 450–730oC.[89,111,112] In Figure 7 we present images of several different crystal specimens from the MTP family produced in our group at the Oak Ridge National Laboratory. All samples were made by loading stoichiometric quantities of the appropriate elements (or, in the case of CuInP2S6, the appropriate elements plus the precursor phase In2S3) into fused silica ampoules and heating to temperatures ranging from 600–775oC over 10–15 hrs., holding at those temperatures for up to four days, and slow cooling the samples at rates of 20oC hr–1. As these samples were fabricated using the vapor transport technique, the presence of a large temperature gradient of 50oC or so was beneficial to promoting species transport from the hot end of the ampoule to the growth area in the colder region of the furnace. 2.3 Intercalation

Intercalation of MTP compounds has been performed in order to modify their structure and thus their properties. The main research thrusts for the intercalation of MTP compounds can be grouped into two categories, both covered in detail below. The first is the intercalation of Li for the purposes of using MTP compounds like Ni2P2S6 as an electrode in Li–ion batteries. Later work (e.g.[20,123,124]) utilized alkali or alkaline earth metals to investigate the effects of intercalating these elements on the nature of potential ion exchange pathways with various charged organic

The second is the intercalation of various organic species for the purposes of either modifying the magnetic properties (i.e., the inducing of ferrimagnetism from an antiferromagnetic ground state in Mn2P2S6) or enhancing the non-linear optical properties.[125] The insertion of a charged intercalating compound into the van der Waals gap of a MTP material results in a charge imbalance of the MTP compound. For insertion of Li into Fe 2P2S6 or Ni2P2S6 charge balance is maintained by the reduction of one of the metal cations (M2+) to a an M0 state.[126] This charge reduction is accompanied by the movement of the now-neutral metal to a tetrahedral coordination.[63] The other route taken by the various organic and larger alkali or alkaline earth metals is the ejection of a metal cation species from the host compound. The general approach is to either modify one of the components of the two cation sublattices or to modify the P2S6 backbone by partial substitution of Se.[161] For example, replacing the Cu+ cation with Ag+ results in the formation of AgInP2S6 which shows no ferroelectric phase transition down to 110 K.[162] Partial substitution of Ag for Cu (e.g., Ag0.1Cu0.9InP2S6) reduces the Tc by 30 K,[27] an effect possibly ascribed to the larger size of the Ag 1+ cation where lower temperatures are needed to stop the static hopping of the system. 5. Prospects for the MTP family

The structures of the various MTP compounds are remarkably similar with all materials sharing an essentially identical [P2S(Se)6]4– framework. It is truly astonishing that such a large variety of functionality can be exhibited by simply changing the metal cation species located within the two distorted octahedral cages within this essentially unchanging backbone. The next obvious step in researching this materials class is to engineer properties such as bandgap energy, ferrielectric transition temperature, ferrimagnetic ordering temperature etc. in a rational way that enables functionalization of 2D electronic materials for the next generation of encapsulated electronic

devices. To this end, the following section will briefly summarize some specific properties of MTP materials and how they can relate to current and future research needs. 5.1. Brief Overview of Integration of 2D and Ferroelectric Materials

Integrating graphene with ferroelectric materials was pursued very soon after the early graphene devices were made, with the first successful demonstration in 2009.[211] We would like to refer the interested reader to recent dedicated reviews on the topic, by Hong et al.[212] and by Jie and Hao.[213] Generally, [ferroelectric]/[graphene] or a similar structure couples ferroelectric, piezoelectric, high-k dielectric, and/or pyroelectric functions as well as the non-linear optical response of ferroelectrics with the electronic conductivity of graphene. Graphene devices therefore gain memory and significant electronic response to mechanical deformation or thermal history. As a result, numerous applications, such electronic switches, transistors, infrared sensors, photodetectors and thermal switches have been envisioned. A fundamental interesting aspect of these involves the regime of comparatively high carrier density that can be sustained in device geometry, and does not require the use of ionic liquids. These phenomena generally require carrier densities in excess of 1013 cm–2, that are readily achievable with good ferroelectrics. In addition to band-crossings that were already reported in fewlayer graphene devices[214], increasing carrier doping beyond linear dispersion could be fundamentally very interesting.[215–217] Arguably the most interesting properties of ferroelectric graphene devices are associated with spatial modulation of the carrier density. The p-n junctions in graphene were demonstrated by Williams et al. using a gated geometry.[218] Theoretical proposals for these junctions include Klein tunneling[219] and Veselago lensing.[220]

Oppositely oriented polarization domains in the ferroelectric should fulfill the role of a spatially modulated gate without the need of external gating or patterning. The induced p-n junction would coincide with the ferroelectric domain wall in that case. Crucially, the intrinsic width of domain walls in ferroelectric can be as small as a few nm, as recently analyzed by Morozovska et al.[221] Such a sharp transition is likely not achievable by lithographic methods. It is in this case that the long-sought Veselago lensing effect can become substantial.[222] Spatial modulation can also lead to very attractive opto-electronic effects. Jin et al.[223] predicted that a ferroelectrically modulated graphene sheet can act as a plasmon waveguide, wherein plasmon modes in graphene couple to phonon–polariton modes in the ferroelectric. Notably, this effect does not require a very large magnitude of spontaneous polarization, and is already manifested with a polarization of ≈ 5 µC cm–2.[223] Goldflam et al. have recently made a spectacular demonstration of switchable graphene plasmons using polycrystalline lead zirconate titanate (PZT) as a ferroelectric support,[224] Intense plasmon scattering was observed from the grain-boundaries of the PZT, while the plasmon wavelength was efficiently tunable by applied electric field due to the combination of dielectric tunability of PZT and the modulation of carrier density in graphene. More recently, ferroelectrics have been invoked as tunable materials for 2D semiconductors. Ferroelectric carrier control in MoS2 was demonstrated by Wang et al.[225], Nguyen et al.[226,227], and in black phosphorus.[228] Though theoretically promising, the integration of ferroelectrics and 2D materials has thus far not been straightforward.[212] The choice of ferroelectric largely bifurcates between perovskite oxides (such as BaTiO3 and PbZrxTiyO3) or the ferroelectric polymer PVDF. In either case, the stability of the ferroelectric surface (and therefore its interface) is not warranted, and numerous extrinsic or intrinsic compensating mechanisms in the form of electrochemical reactions, surface reconstructions

or vacancy centers occur to screen spontaneous polarization [See Ref. [212] and the references therein]. In this regard, one of the interesting explanations for the so-called antihysteresis effect in the ferroelectrically modulated conductance of the coupled graphene sheet lies in the partial formation of graphene oxide by the electrochemical reaction at the interface. [229] PVDF is an oxygenfree ferroelectric and is comparatively easy to integrate with almost any matching material. In fact, most of the successful demonstrations of devices up to present involve the use of PVDF. However, as with all polymer films, the questions of uniformity, interface smoothness, and related issues are not trivial to resolve. We believe that metal thiophosphates will play a unique role in this merger. They are oxygen-free ferroelectrics and multiferroics. Unlike perovskite oxides, surface formation does not create dangling bonds, thus producing stable and relatively inert surfaces. Though the defect structure of these materials is still not well understood, it is easy to anticipate a large energy of the anion P2S64– vacancy in contrast to the oxygen vacancy in the perovskite lattice. Yet, these are still solid-state materials with well-defined long-range order that can be difficult to achieve with polymers. Therefore, metal thiophosphates are anticipated to provide clean, controllable, native van der Waals interfaces for 2D materials. Obviously, interesting effects may occur even at the boundary of bulk crystals of layered ferroics with graphene without the necessity to exfoliate the material or grow thin films.

5.4. Optoelectronic Functions Recently, Wu et al.[233] have realized nanocavity lasers with optical pumping thresholds (27 nW at 130 K) using the transition metal disulfide as a gain medium at the surface of a photonic crystal cavity (PCC). The gain medium confines the electrons to the PCC surface, a geometry that allows for the tailoring of the gain properties via gating and current injection.[233] Devices such as these have multiple requirements, including bandgaps in the appropriate frequency range and a desirably high photoluminescence quantum yield. The MTP family offers an intriguing possibility for devices with the same requirements. As previously mentioned, the bandgaps of MTP compounds that have been reported in the literature range from 1.2–3.5 eV, extending the range of bandgaps for layered van der Waals compounds (with TMDs having a low bandgap of < 2 eV). This increased energy range opens up the possibilities of new optoelectronic functions at shorter wavelengths. Interestingly, Gáldamez et al.[234] reported the generation of photoexcited carriers in the selenide compounds CuBiP2Se6, suggesting that the MTP class of materials could be attractive for possible solar energy applications In addition, photoluminescence (PL) has been observed in many MTP compounds (e.g. [235,236] ) where it is seen that not only do MTP compounds have a large PL peak, but that the intensity of the peak can be increased by an order of magnitude by the presence of intercalating compounds.[235] MTP compounds present an interesting opportunity for quantifying PL intensity in terms of MTP

However, recent theoretical work by Li et al.[196] predicts that in Mn2P2S6 and Mn2P2Se6 the domain walls of the antiferromagnetic honeycomb enables valley-protected edge states which in turn support spindependent transport. In addition, Berry curvature-induced topological quantum transport and valley-dependent optical selection rules are predicted in these two MTP compounds through coupling of the valley degree of freedom to the antiferromagnetic order which in turn produces an emergent electronic degree of freedom (resulting from spin and valley indices).[196] The most important conclusion of this theoretical prediction is that the optical and magnetic properties of Mn2P2S6 and Mn2P2Se6 allow for optical polarization in spin-valley space. As the theoretical candidates for study are proposed to have antiferromagnetism in a honeycomb lattice, be a direct bandgap semiconductor, and have interband transitions in near valleys that support circular dichroism, there are many possibilities in the MTP family that will be the subject of much future investigation.

5.5. Predictive Framework Graphene, a structurally simple material with no phase transitions, has been in the center of experimental and theoretical investigations for the past decade, resulting in tens of thousands of publications. More recently, other 2D materials systems have been systematically researched, including BN, black phosphorus, and transition metal dichalcogenides (MX2 where M is a transition metal and X = S, Se, or Te). This latter family, generally referred to as TMDs, is noted for containing a

variety of direct-gap semiconductors whose carrier type (p or n) and bandgap energies can vary with composition.[8] The greater TMD family also contains materials that exhibit other interesting correlated properties such as insulating behavior (e.g., HfS2), low-temperature superconductivity (e.g., NbS2, NbSe2 and TaS2), and charge density waves (NbSe2). In the form of single or few-layered specimens these properties are, for the most part, stable. [8] However, confinement effects may reveal additional behaviors as well (e.g. [237]). This array of materials properties has enabled the development of new devices ranging from energy storage to optoelectronics such as lasers [233] and LEDs.[238] Compared with the wide variety of compounds and materials properties exhibited for the TMD family with just 40 compounds,[8] the greater metal thiophosphate (MTP) family is, in comparison, relatively untapped with over 200 known compounds reported which include most 3d and many 4d and 5d transition metals. As detailed in this review, materials within the MTP family are insulators with bandgaps ranging from 1.3–3.5 eV that also exhibit Mott behavior, antiferromagnetism, and ferrielectricity. In addition, up to the writing of this Review, the optoelectronic functions in this materials class are relatively unexplored. This makes the MTP family a fertile research area. However, given the flexibility toward ionic substitution within the MTP family the total number of possible materials can be much larger than the several scores of known pure-phase compounds, possibly numbering in the thousands or even more. Clearly, an experimental effort to synthesize all these compounds and investigate their properties is vast and unlikely to be achieved, even in a community-wide effort. This diversity of structure, composition, and application present in MTP compounds makes it very easy to advocate the need for material genomics. The closely related Te-containing structural analogues of the MTP family of materials have been partially explored via theory. These materials contain the same van der Waals stacking as the MTP compounds; however, in this case the [P2S(Se)6]4– groups are replaced by [Si2Te6]4–. For example, we have theoretically predicted bandgaps and magnetic ground-states of single layer hexagonal MTP materials belonging to the CrSiTe3 prototype;[70] in addition, we have also predicted newer potential members in this family of materials that can potentially be thermodynamically stable as single layers.[239] While single layers of these materials are easier to deal with from the point of view of materials modeling, advances in accurate description of van der Waals density functional theory for magnetic systems are necessary to properly capture the magnetic ground state and excitations in the bulk. This is because the magnetic coupling between layers, though weak, is important, and is very sensitive to the layer spacing. Very recent advances [240,241] along this direction are promising, and need to be applied more regularly to these materials. The few computational papers that deal with these systems essentially use density functional theory with local or semilocal functionals.[242,243] Since the band edges are predominantly derived from the d-orbitals of the transition metal atoms where effects of electron correlation arising from the localization of the orbitals become important, predictions of bandgaps using these functionals fail. Improvements using DFT+U or hybrid-DFT methods can be achieved,[36,238,244–246] but have not been regularly applied to study the electronic properties of these materials. Because of the large combinatorics involved in the choice of the metal cation atoms for a given thiophosphate host system, with the main change in the ground state being off-lattice distortions or chemical phase separation, MTP is a fascinating ground for performing high-throughput computations to screen potential candidate for photovoltaic applications, i.e., a small direct bandgap of ≈1.2 eV and high electron-hole mobility.

Conclusions Layered metal thio(seleno)phosphate (MTP) compounds are a large materials family of structurally similar van der Waals gapped materials. The structural backbone of this family consists of an hexagonal array of [P2S(Se)6]4– complexes. The metal cations stabilizing this structure can be readily varied, giving rise to correlated behaviors such as magnetism and ferroelectricity. These materials feature comparably strong ionic bonding, which is the root of their rich functionalities as well as the key distinction to transition metal dichalcogenides and single-element layered materials with predominantly covalent bonding. In this Review, we have summarized the progress in the growth, characterization and understanding of the material properties of these compounds over the past two decades. Despite a

number of papers on the subject, it is fair to say that the potential of MTP as a material class has yet to be thoroughly explored, particularly in comparison to other layered materials such as dichalcogenides and graphite. This is especially evident in almost complete lack of papers on thin films and quasi-2D layers of these compounds but is true also of properties more related to the bulk, such as electronic and optoelectronic properties. We envision that MTP will play a unique role in 2D and quasi-2D materials once research activities expand. In fact, we argue that MTP can be thought of as layered analog to complex metal oxides, and therefore all the rich functionalities of the latter materials class can be brought into the domain of 2D materials via native van der Waals interfaces. The unique distinctions of MTP compounds are correlated ferroic properties, such as ferroelectricity and magnetism, which stem in large part from ionic bonding in MTPs. Ionic bonding is also responsible for the comparatively large bandgaps of these materials which fall within a range 1.3–3.5 eV, largely unavailable to present 2D materials and yet enticing for optoelectronic applications. At the same time, MTPs may be a rich source of insulators for encapsulation or gate-media for 2D electronic materials, adding memory and control functionalities to otherwise uniform conducting or semiconducting sheets without doping or broken lattice symmetry.[252–256] We also emphasize that ferroelectric ordering and incipient ionic conductivity themselves make MTP a new model system to answer long-standing problems such as size-effects, superionic conductivity, and polar glassiness. MTPs bring the relative ease of synthesis, clean surface and nanocrystal preparation that have been historically complicated in complex oxides, but are inherent properties of van der Waals crystals. At the same time, the electronic and magnetic states of the bulk crystals with d-metal ions (Fe, Ni, Mn, V, etc.) deserve further attention, as they may exhibit

sufficiently strong electron correlations, crossover of magnetic orders and coupling of ferroic degrees of freedom in the general context of multiferroic behaviors. Finally, MTPs do not have industrial applications at the moment, although catalytic, ionic and intercalation-related bulk properties have been reported. We anticipate that a more expansive investigation of the synthesis, scalability and stability of MTPs will eventually produce practical materials. Most notably, further development of MTPs may accelerate technological adoption of complex material properties, such as ferroic and correlated electron properties, and instabilities proximate to phase transitions that have long been pursued in the realm of complex oxides.

Metal thiophaspahates (MTPs) are a class of cleavable insulating materials with a common P2S6 structural framework. They span a very wide variety of transition metal cations, thus exhibiting ferroelectricity, magnetism and optoelectronic properties and rich solid state chemistry that can combine various properites within a single bulk, quasi-2D or prospectively 2D crystal. In this respect MTP compounds can be viewed as a leyered analog to complex oxides.
