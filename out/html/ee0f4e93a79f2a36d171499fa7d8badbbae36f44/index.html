The large and long-term future deployment of photovoltaics demands stable, abundant and non-toxic materials similar to silicon. Pure sulphide kesterite Cu2ZnSnS4 (CZTS), a quaternary compound semiconductor, has emerged as an excellent candidate for such next generation photovoltaic technology. It has attracted attention due to its non-toxicity, highefficiency and low-cost potential1,2. Kesterite belongs to the diamond-type structural chalcogenide family, and can be regarded as a direct derivative of commercialised CdTe and CuInxGa1-xSe2 (CIGSe) cell technologies. These are both known for their favourable optical and electronic properties and demonstrated >22% efficiency3, suggesting a high efficiency potential for kesterite4. In addition to the use of low-cost abundant materials, most reported efficient CZTS solar cells have been produced using industrially viable sputtering methods developed for CIGSe, which will facilitate upscaling this technology for the future commercialisation. Photovoltaic devices fabricated from the absorber CZTS are among the most efficient of the earth-abundant, non-toxic thin film solar cells under investigation at present. The bandgap of CZTS is 1.5eV, close to the optimal bandgap required for a single junction solar cell. More importantly, the bandgap of CZTS can be easily tuned in a wide range (1.5eV~2.1eV) when alloying with other elements such as Ge or Ag, making it well matched to the high bandgap requirement for the uppermost cells for Si-based tandem cell stacks1,5. In this regard, development of high performance of CZTS cells may be considered a key prerequisite for a successfully developing PV to its full potential, given the stability and toxicity issues with contending tandem cell options. 3 Despite its promise, the highest reported efficiency for CZTS has been stagnant at around 9% for years5,6. This is far lower than its detailed balance limiting efficiency of 33%7 and the efficiency demonstrated by counterpart high bandgap CuInxGa1-xS2 (CIGS) cells8, mainly due to a large VOC deficit (Eg/q-VOC)4. Several factors have been suggested for this VOC deficit, including a large number of charged defects associated with band tailing4,9, an undesirably thick MoS2 layer10,11 at the rear of the CZTS cell, co-existence of secondary phases11,12 and unfavourable band alignment at the CZTS/CdS heterojunction interface13. These problems simultaneously cause non-radiative recombination within CZTS solar cells, limiting VOC and efficiency. To deal with some of these problems, we have developed an approach using an ultra-thin Al2O3 layer at the rear of the CZTS cell to reduce MoS2 thickness and to simultaneously limit the decomposition of the CZTS in this region, which often causes the formation of voids and secondary phases14,15. A combined SnS and S sulfurization atmosphere has been introduced during the annealing of precursor layers to reduce SnS loss16, supress Sn related defects (deep level defects for kesterite17,18) and inhibit defects at the CZTS surface19. These combined efforts enabled the improvement of our baseline CZTS cell efficiency to ~8% (the base-line CZTS cell configuration is with a ~ 60 nm thick CdS buffer layer)14,19- 21. Despite these efforts, the thermal activation energies deduced from the temperaturedependent VOC study for reported high efficiency CZTS cells were still much lower than the estimated cell bandgap22-26. In addition, the ideality factors of CZTS cells with respectable efficiencies (~8%) are generally above 1.5 or even above two in some cases14,19,21,26, much higher than those of other high-efficiency thin film PV technologies (with a typical ideality factor of around 1.3)27,28. These features suggest that non-radiative recombination in the CZTS/CdS heterojunction region, including at the p-n hetero-interface and in the very top part of CZTS within the space charge region (SCR), likely dominates the performance loss of our CZTS cells, leading to a large Voc deficit. Indeed, a centimetre-scale CZTS solar cell exceeding the 10% efficiency barrier sets a record efficiency for any kesterite solar cell of similar size, whether pure sulphide material or by incorporating Se3. The heat treatment of the CZTS/CdS heterojunction greatly reduces the heterojunction non-radiative recombination, confirmed by time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL), temperature-dependent VOC and Suns-VOC measurements. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and Raman measurement reveal the formation of new phases because of the heat-treatment induced elemental inter-diffusion. In addition, secondary ion mass spectrometry (SIMS) confirms the Na accumulation within the heterojunction and atom probe tomography (APT) reveals Na containing nanometre-scale clusters along with Cudeficiency, indicating the likely formation of a Cu2-xNaxZnSnS4 phase, which is thermodynamically possible providing the Na content is low. Consequently, a more favourable band alignment at the p-n interface is established. Device performance The baseline process for fabricating CZTS solar cells is based on two key steps: co-sputter deposition of Cu/SnS/ZnS precursor followed by a high temperature sulfurization annealing. Additional refinements were ultra-thin Al2O3 layers that were deposited prior to the absorber precursor deposition and sulfurization annealing that was conducted in a combined SnS plus S atmosphere, yielding CZTS solar cells of around 8% baseline efficiency14,19. The heat treatment (HT) processes were performed immediately after the chemical bath deposition of CdS on top of the CZTS absorber. We tried a wide range of processing temperatures, from 200°C to 500°C. Treatment at temperatures around 300°C gave the best and most reproducible performance results and was therefore favoured for comprehensive analysis. The traditional CdS heterostructure for CIGS and CZTS was used, with full details presented in the methods section. 8 Recombination analysis We applied a variety of characterisation techniques to understand the underlying mechanisms responsible for the dramatic efficiency improvement from HT. At first, we conducted TRPL measurements on the CZTS devices with and without HT using two excitation wavelengths (470 nm and 640 nm). From our knowledge of the light absorption coefficient of CZTS (Supplementary Figure 6), the excitation by the 470 nm laser generates carriers mostly at the p-n junction interface and in the very top region of the CZTS absorber, whereas 640 nm laser generates carriers more uniformly through the bulk. The TRPL decay curves for the two excitation wavelengths are shown in Figure 2a and 2b respectively; CZTS with HT shows a significantly slower decay compared to that without HT by using excitation of 470 nm laser. By contrast, these decay curves are very similar if 640 nm laser excitation is used. By fitting the TRPL spectra with a bi-exponential function model32, the effective lifetime of CZTS with HT was estimated to be 5.4 ns, which is more than double that achieved without HT when using 470 nm excitation, whereas the extracted lifetime is similar at 3.4-3.6 ns when using the 640 nm excitation. This suggests that the heat-treatment significantly affects the recombination in the heterojunction region rather than deep in the CZTS bulk. Elemental inter-diffusion and aggregation While we have ascertained that the non-radiative recombination within the heterojunction region has been greatly reduced, the next step is to investigate what changes occurred in SCR upon heat-treatment. XPS elemental depth profiles were conducted, as shown in Figure 3a. Clear elemental inter-diffusion occurs in the heterojunction region upon heat-treatment. On the CdS buffer side, Zn was found to have diffused into the CdS buffer with a concentration gradient depth of around 15 nm, which is about a quarter of the CdS thickness (about 60nm). On the CZTS absorber side, after HT process, a significant Cd tail is apparent, having a diffusion depth of around 200 nm; assuming the Ar etching rates for CdS and CZTS are similar. As ZnxCd1-xS can be formed at similar temperature by SILAR20 or at temperature below 100°C by CBD36,37, we speculate that an ultra-thin ZnxCd1-xS layer is formed at the CdS/CZTS interface region where there is much higher Zn content, with this requiring further investigation. On the CZTS side, it is interesting to know how Cd interacts with CZTS. Hence highresolution TEM was performed. If Cd is on the Cu site, a CdCu antisite defect may form in the top part CZTS which would contribute to n-type doping40. If this is the case, the top region of CZTS would exhibit a weak p-type doping, or intrinsic or even n-type doping, which would usually enlarge the depletion region. In addition to the significant elemental inter-diffusion between CdS and CZTS absorber, we also found that Na accumulates near the heterojunction interface region using SIMS. As shown in Figure 4a, much higher Na signals were detected in both the CdS and upper CZTS layers after the HT process. Previous publications reported that, after a post-heat treatment on either CZTS absorber42 or full devices43, similar Na accumulation near the surface of the absorber was seen, leading to a higher doping of the kesterite absorber42. We conducted C-V measurements and found that the depletion width reduces and the doping of CZTS significantly increases after the HT process (Supplementary Figure 17). In order to understand the origin of higher doping, we compared the doping of CZTS, CZTS after HT and CZTS/CdS heterojunction after HT (Supplementary Table 6). Both the accumulation of Na and diffusion of Cd at top part of CZTS should contribute to a higher doping in agreement with previous reports41,44. Moreover, first principle calculation suggests Na is able to saturate dangling bonds at grain boundaries45. Thus, we speculate that excess Na near the hetero-interface can also saturate dangling bonds at the CdS/CZTS interface, thereby passivating this interface. The specific distribution of Na accumulation within these regions were obtained by APT measurements on a sample after HT. Figure 4c shows a 3D atom probe reconstruction showing only Na ions. A high density of Na is observed in the top section of the tip, where the CdS layer is located. Within the absorber, Na can be found at grain boundaries (highlighted with a red arrow in Figure 4c) which is frequently observed and considered beneficial in chalcogenide solar cells45. Unexpectedly, we see some Na-accumulated clusters of about 5-10 nanometers in size visible within the CZTS absorber. A proxigram using the largest Na precipitates within the dataset (see Figure 4d with black arrow) shows there is a negative correlation between the concentration of Na and Cu, while concentrations of other elements such as Zn, Sn remain unchanged. Band alignment study To provide insight into the impact of HT on the band structure due to induced element inter- diffusion, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements including core-level spectroscopy and valence band (VB) spectroscopy were conducted47. Figure 5a shows the XPS VB data of bulk CdS and CZTS, from which the valence band maximum (VBM) positions can be readily estimated13. Without HT, the VBM positions relative to Fermi-energy (EF) for CdS and CZTS resulted to be -1.48 eV and -0.36 eV, respectively, assuming the binding energy below EF is negative. After the HT process, the VBM positions relative to EF for CdS and CZTS changed to -1.40 eV and -0.27 eV, respectively. After HT, the Fermi-level of CZTS moves close to its VBM, suggesting the doping level of the CZTS had increased. This is consistent with the C-V measurements where the doping of CZTS nearly doubles after HT (Supplementary Figure 17). If the bandgaps of bulk CdS and CZTS are assumed unchanged, with the band bending (Vbb) estimated from the core-levels energy of each element at the interface as well as in bulk CdS and CZTS47 (Supplementary Note 4 and Supplementary Figure 18), the CBO without and with HT can be estimated at -0.13 eV and -0.04 eV, respectively (See Figure 5b and c). This implies a more favourable band alignment after HT. However, the HT-induced Zn diffusion into CdS would increase its bandgap by elevating the CBM of the buffer near interface, whilst HT- induced Cd diffusion into CZTS decreases its bandgap by downwardly shifting both the CBM and VBM13. Therefore, upon heat-treatment, the CBO at hetero-junction is expected to take the form of a “cliff”, shallower than -0.04 eV, or even correspond to flat band conditions (see Figure 5c). 18 selenium-containing) of standard centimetre-size to exceed 10% efficiency. The performance improvement of Cu2ZnSnS4 solar cells is achieved by using a simple heterojunction heat treatment process. This approach enables elemental inter-diffusion, leading to a Zn gradient within CdS and a Cd gradient within CZTS, as well as a Na accumulation and local Cudepletion at the heterojunction. The approach also leads to the formation of new phases: Cd diffusion into the lattice of CZTS surface forms a thin layer of Cu2CdxZn1-xSnS4 while Zn diffusion into the CdS likely forms ultrathin ZnxCd1-xS; and a new phase of Cu2-xNaxZnSnS4 (average x~0.07) nanoclusters are revealed. Thanks to this elemental inter-diffusion, a more favourable band alignment is achieved and the recombination at heterojunction region is significantly reduced. The findings in this paper also suggest that all functional layers in the heterojunction devices are inter-related. The effect of any particular process heavily depends on the initial baseline quality and strategies that have been shown effective or non-effective in efficiency improvement need constant re-evaluation. With CZTS being stable, environmentally-friendly and low-cost, our work highlights significant research progress and the potential for further improving the efficiency of CZTS by reducing non-radiative recombination and further extending our understanding of this material. Methods Solar cell fabrication. All devices were deposited on a Mo coated glass, which has been cleaned with ammonia (7%) and DI water in sequence. CZTS precursors were deposited by co-sputtering Cu/ZnS/SnS material (thickness of 500 nm) using a magnetron sputtering system (AJA International, Inc., model). The DC power for Cu target (3 inch) was 23 W, whereas the RF powers for ZnS (3 inch) and SnS (3 inch) were 77 W and 53 W, respectively, in order to confine the Cu/Sn ratio within 1.7~1.8 and Zn/Sn ~1.2. The CZTS films were synthesized by sulfurization of the precursors using Rapid Thermal Processor (AS-One 100) within a combined sulfur and SnS atmosphere at 560 °C for three minutes. The conventional cadmium sulfide buffer layer of thickness 50~60 nm was deposited by the CBD method.
