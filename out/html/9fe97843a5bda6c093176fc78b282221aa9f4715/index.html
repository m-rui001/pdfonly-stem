Ritt! !1, Aleksandr Noy! !3,4 and Menachem Elimelech! !1 ✉ Synthetic membranes with pores at the subnanometre scale are at the core of processes for separating solutes from water, such as water purification and desalination. While these membrane processes have achieved substantial industrial success, the capability of state-of-the-art membranes to selectively separate a single solute from a mixture of solutes is limited. Such high-precision separation would enable fit-for-purpose treatment, improving the sustainability of current water-treatment processes and opening doors for new applications of membrane technologies. Herein, we introduce the challenges of state-of-the-art membranes with subnanometre pores to achieve high selectivity between solutes. We then analyse experimental and theoretical literature to discuss the molecular-level mechanisms that contribute to energy barriers for solute transport through subnanometre pores. We conclude by providing principles and guidelines for designing next-generation single-species selective membranes that are inspired by ion-selective biological channels.

ver the past several decades, reverse osmosis (RO) desalination has become the leading technology for augmenting fresh water supply around the world. The rise of this technology is primarily attributed to the development of a highly permeable thin-film composite (TFC) polyamide membrane with high salt rejection1. Extensive research has sought to further increase the water permeability of desalination membranes2, but it has been shown that greater permeability would only marginally improve the energy efficiency of desalination3. This realization initiated a new research thrust aimed at developing membranes with ultra-high water–solute selectivity4–6. The primary appeal of ultra-high water– solute selectivity, or solute rejection, is that greater retention of all solutes would ensure higher water quality and avoid costly post-treatment steps3. However, to date, these efforts have failed to produce selective layers with higher performance than current TFC polyamide membranes, mainly due to unavoidable defects inherent to the top-down approaches used to fabricate the selective layers2,4,7,8. More recently, demand has grown for selective separation of a single solute from water9–11. This third research avenue aimed at increasing solute–solute selectivity requires high-precision separation with membranes that can discriminate between different solutes. Membranes with high solute–solute selectivity would enable more sustainable water-treatment processes. Such membranes could selectively separate problematic contaminants from water and minimize the disposal of concentrated waste streams to the environment. When the end use is drinking water, such fit-for-purpose treatment can also reduce the need for remineralization of product water, thereby reducing treatment cost and use of chemicals12. Solute–solute selectivity would also greatly expand the applications of membrane technologies beyond water treatment. Valuable elements, such as lithium or important metals, could be extracted from water at lower chemical and energy consumption compared to existing processes (for example, precipitation, solvent extraction and distillation)13,14. Other fields where solute–solute selective membranes could be applied include sensor devices, proton-exchange

membranes for fuel cells, and industrial chemical separation processes involving different solvents. The examples and benefits of high-precision separation discussed above emphasize the paramount importance of a single-species selective membrane. However, conventional membrane fabrication techniques lack the molecular-level design required for achieving solute–solute selectivity. Furthermore, the underlying principles for the design and fabrication of such selective membranes are not well established. Recent advances in ångström-scale fabrication techniques15,16, combined with insights from nanofluidics and ion-selective biological channels, may provide a gateway to novel, ultra-high solute–solute selective membranes. Translating these advances into the design of multipore membrane systems is critical to realizing the full technological potential of precise solute– solute separations. In this Perspective, we synthesize experimental and theoretical evidence to provide a design framework for a new class of synthetic membranes with subnanometre pores that are selective for a single species. We first highlight the limitations of conventional membrane materials in achieving solute–solute selectivity. Then, we introduce the transition-state theory to describe energy barriers for solute transport in membranes and discuss the molecular-level mechanisms that can induce selectivity. We conclude by proposing guidelines and new avenues of research for designing novel synthetic membranes with unprecedented solute–solute selectivity.

Transition-state theory can describe solute transport in membranes

Solute transport through membranes with subnanometre pores can be described as molecular diffusion driven by a chemical potential gradient20. Solute–solute selectivity is induced by differences in solute transport rates across the membrane and is commonly evaluated using solute flux or rejection21–24. However, to explore solute–solute selectivity beyond measured differences in fluxes and to understand the mechanisms underlying such selectivity, a molecular-level approach to model solute transport is needed.

Therefore, molecular diffusion of a solute in a host medium can be considered an elementary reaction and be described using the transition state theory (TST) proposed by Eyring (Box 1)25–28. According to this theory, to translocate from one coordinate to the next, a solute must go through an unstable and high-potential energy transition state before establishing new bonds at the next coordinate. This transition state is associated with an energy barrier (also called activation energy), limiting solute transport only to solutes possessing sufficient energy to overcome the energy barrier29. Solute transport through the membrane pore can be described as molecular diffusion over multiple barriers in series. Ideally, the solute needs to overcome a single barrier at the pore entrance, cross consecutive barriers inside the pore and then partition back into the solution on the other side of the pore. Assuming such a long diffusion path is governed by a rate-limiting step (that is, partitioning from the bulk solution into the pore or diffusion inside the membrane), the overall transport across the membrane pore can be modelled using single-barrier TST28. Molecular-level mechanisms govern energy barriers for solute transport

While the energy barrier for solute transport can be affected by operating conditions (for example, driving force) and solution composition, as well as by interactions with other solutes, solute interactions with the host medium (that is, water and pore walls) provide the most crucial mechanisms to induce solute–solute selectivity. Figure 3a shows examples from selected studies of experimental energy barriers for transport of water and different solutes through membranes and other subnanometre pores. Note that most energy barriers calculated are higher than the activation energy for self-diffusion (or viscosity) of water (~4 kcal mol–1)34, indicating hindered transport. Interpretation of these energy barriers with respect to the different mechanisms that can affect solute–solute selectivity (Fig. 2b) is given below.

Size and steric effects (Fig. 2b(i)) are often used to describe solute transport, as they can pose significant energy barriers. For a solute to enter a rigid confined pore with dimensions smaller than its own bare size, reorganization of intramolecular bonds and atoms, or rearrangement of the membrane material, may be necessary. These rearrangements, if feasible at the tested temperature, may result in relatively high energy barriers35–39(Fig. 3a, ref. 40). Electrostatic interactions between solutes and fixed charged groups at the pore mouth (Fig. 2b(ii)) repel co-ions while attracting counterions, thus hindering or promoting solute partitioning, respectively41,42. In polymeric NF and IEX membranes, where steric exclusion is not as prominent as in RO membranes, electrostatic effects significantly influence solute transport. Experimental (Fig. 3a, ref. 43) and simulation studies44 demonstrate the contribution of electrostatic repulsion to the energy barrier for solute transport through an NF membrane and confined subnanometre pore, respectively. Notably, even like-charge ions can be affected to different extents by the fixed negatively charged carboxyl groups on the polyamide NF membrane pore mouth43. The dissociation of salts into ions in solution establishes an electric field, directing the opposite point charge of water molecules toward the ions to reduce the overall electric field formed. This phenomenon leads to the formation of stabilizing hydration shells around the dissolved ions. The ability of water (or any other medium) to dissolve ionized solutes and stabilize them is governed by its dielectric constant45,46. The water shell significantly increases the ion size, thus hindering its transport through membranes with pores of similar dimensions. Moreover, the ion solvation shell masks the unique properties of the bare ion, hampering its recognition by the pore, which is essential for differentiation between ions and achieving ion–ion selectivity. However, compared to intramolecular bonds governing steric effects, the bonds between the water shell and the ion are less rigid. Therefore, the water shell is often removed or rearranged when the ion enters a pore smaller than the hydrated ion44,47–50. This partial dehydration of ions resulting from dielectric effects (Fig. 2b(iii)) contributes to the energy barrier for ion permeation and can explain, for instance, commonly observed selectivity trends for similar monovalent ions in subnanometre pores. Ions with smaller ionic size, corresponding to higher hydration energy, hold their hydration shell more tightly and therefore experience a higher energy barrier during permeation through polymeric membranes (Fig. 3b,c) and simulated nanopores48,44. Dehydration-based energy barriers will manifest at reduced pore size (Fig. 3d) and denser polymeric membranes (Fig. 3b), where the hydrated size of the ion becomes too large to access the pore (Fig. 3e). After partial dehydration and partitioning from one dielectric (the bulk) into another (the pore), dehydrated ions develop interactions with the pore wall which, to some extent, can energetically compensate for the loss (or deformation) of their hydration shell to reduce the energy barrier for partitioning. This phenomenon is well pronounced, for example, in IEX membranes, where attractive interactions between the ion and oppositely charged groups in the polymer matrix compensate for the loss of the hydration shell of the ion and facilitate its transport23,51. These compensatory interactions of the ion with the pore material are ion-specific and, perhaps, one of the key components to obtaining single-species selectivity, as we discuss in the next section. Solute–medium interactions are not limited to ions; they can also be attributed to relatively weak van der Waals (vdW) forces between uncharged solutes and a polarizable pore material (Fig. 2b(iv))52. Therefore, a more holistic approach with a set of rules and principles should be applied for designing a single-species selective membrane (Fig. 4a). Biological ion channels provide an inspirational glimpse into ideal single-species selectivity. Extensive work by MacKinnon60–62 elucidated the architecture and working mechanism of the K+ channel, which can transport K+ ions 10,000 times faster than Na+ ions through the cell membrane to enable fundamental cellular processes (Fig. 4b). Ultra-selective and fast K+ conduction is possible due to the combination of several features and physical principles that are discussed below. These principles also underlie the selectivity in other ion-selective biological channels63, providing guidelines for future membrane design. Although mimicking the precision of nature poses a daunting challenge64,65, most of the key principles provided below can be applied with existing and emerging fabrication

techniques, and preliminary success has already been demonstrated, as discussed in the next section. Analysis of the K+ channel reveals a selectivity filter as short as 12 Å, approximately a quarter of the entire length of the channel spanning the lipid bilayer61. This ultra-thin layer is optimal to discriminate between solutes by imposing different energy barriers for their transport (Fig. 4c(i)). A longer selectivity filter with high affinity for the target solute (and low affinity for other solutes) could result in higher transport hindrance of the target solute compared to other solutes, thus reducing selectivity. This counteracting effect is well evidenced in micrometre-thick IEX membranes, where the observed selectivity is induced by ion adsorption (or partitioning), but impaired by ion diffusion in the thick membrane32. However, because solute permeability is inversely dependent on both the energy barrier and membrane thickness28, an extremely thin selective layer may result in higher permeation of undesired solutes (Fig. 4c(ii)). Novel fabrication techniques offer pathways for selective membranes

Engineering pores with compensatory interactions that can selectively stabilize a target solute (Fig. 4e(iii)) is highly challenging. One of the major difficulties is the upscaling of pores designed on the molecular level into a defect-free multipore system. In addition, tuning conventional membrane materials (Fig. 1a–d) for such a purpose is impractical due to their inherent chemical heterogeneity as well as surface roughness that exceeds the ångström-scale precision required for high solute–solute selectivity69. Instead, membranes from advanced materials with atomically tunable dimensions as well as homogeneous smooth surfaces (Fig. 1e) create opportunities to mimic molecular phenomena in biological channels. Specifically, such materials can induce solute dehydration by their small pore size and subsequently stabilize a solute by perfect containment and interaction with the pore wall. Extensive research has been devoted to tuning the interlayer spacing in graphene oxide membranes under wetting70–74. Functionalized subnanometre carbon nanotubes can also serve as a platform for selective separation75,76. Here, besides tuning pore diameter, major challenges include functionalization of the pore interior without compromising structural features, and incorporation and alignment of the tubes in an impermeable matrix75,77. While major research efforts with these materials were directed to improving water–solute selectivity and desalination performance, intriguing separations between solutes have also been reported70,76, calling for future research using these tunable materials to study and improve solute–solute selectivity. Atomic layer deposition (ALD) of small precursors, such as SiO2 and TiO2, in cylindrical nanopores is another potential tool to reduce pore size to molecular dimensions and adjust functionality with atomic precision (Fig. 4e(iii), left)78,79. Coordination chemistry, where receptors bind specific species to form a host–guest complex, is another avenue to pursue selective in-pore solute stabilization (Fig. 4e(iii), middle). Such molecular recognition between the host and guest molecules depends on molecular topology, dimensionality and coordination number (that is, number of bonds available between the guest and host molecule)80. While ionophores naturally occur in biological systems to facilitate transport of specific solutes across lipid membranes (Fig. 4e(iii), right)81, a large body of research has focused on developing synthetic macrocyclic and macromolecular receptors82–84. Traditionally, these receptors have been mostly used as mobile carriers in liquid and polymer-inclusion membranes for sensing85 and extracting86 different ions and molecules. More recently, the feasibility of facilitated transport using coordination chemistry was demonstrated for solute–solute separation in subnanometre polymeric membranes87,88, metal–organic framework channels10,11, solid-state nanopores89, and strain-tuned crown ether nanopores in graphene90.

Operating and solution conditions impact solute–solute selectivity

Apart from membrane structure and chemistry, operating conditions (such as driving force) and solution chemistry can also affect the separation efficiency (Fig. 4f(i)). Solution pH, for instance, strongly influences both solute and membrane charge41,43,42, affecting both the electrostatic- (Fig. 2b(ii)) and dielectric-based (Fig. 2b(iii)) energy barriers for solute permeation44. Operating at elevated chemical potential (for example, high solute concentration) can increase the potential energy of undesired solutes in the feed solution, assisting them in permeating the membrane and impairing selectivity91,92. High salt concentration was also shown to

The type of driving force applied can also affect ion–ion selectivity due to charge balance considerations. The K+ channel, similar to other single-species biological channels, transports a single type of ion61. However, for electroneutrality to be conserved, co-transport of an ion with opposite charge or a counter-transport of an ion with the same charge must occur simultaneously through other channels. Electro-driven processes such as ED inherently separate charge by transporting anions and cations thorough anion- and cation-exchange membranes, respectively. This form of operation provides a platform for engineering a set of single-counterion selective IEX membranes (Fig. 4f(ii))32. In contrast, achieving single-ion selectivity in pressure-driven processes such as NF is impossible due to the interlinked transport of anions, cations and water. In this case, single-species selective pores—instead of single-species selective membranes—can theoretically be used in mosaic membrane architectures, which contain more than one type of pore (for example, separate pores for anions and cations)94,95.

Perspectives and outlook

In light of the limited ability of current membranes with subnanometre pores to meet the growing demand for fit-for-purpose applications, it is imperative to develop a new class of membranes that can effectively discriminate between solutes. Such solute–solute selectivity is based on several molecular-scale features that are absent in state-of-the-art membranes such as NF, RO and IEX. Extensive research on ion-selective biological channels, together with more recent molecular simulation studies, have elucidated the general principles for the design of single-species selective membranes. Future efforts should aim at deeper understanding of such principles at the molecular level and incorporating them into multipore membrane systems. The molecular basis for solute–solute selectivity in synthetic membranes is not fully understood, mainly due to lack of experimental techniques with the required spatial and temporal resolution to study the kinetics of solute transport within pores. Molecular simulations are a powerful tool to gain mechanistic understanding of solute transport (for example, partial dehydration at the pore mouth), addressing some of our knowledge gaps of solute–solute selectivity. However, a full and unbiased tracking of molecular transport through a long membrane pore is challenging to achieve due to computational limitations of simulating the large time scales of solute transport96 as well as the chemical heterogeneity of polymeric membranes. Recently developed advanced sampling techniques96,97 provide an opportunity to study the kinetics of solute transport in long pores to better understand the molecular origin of selectivity and establish structure-selectivity relationships that are essential for the development of membranes with single-species selectivity. The conventional solution-diffusion model, frequently used to describe transport in dense polymeric membranes such as RO, is limited in explaining molecular-level phenomena that govern water and solute permeation. Instead, the transition-state theory, which is seldom utilized to describe transport in membranes, can experimentally delineate mechanisms governing solute–solute selectivity by providing molecular interpretation for enthalpy and entropy changes that occur during solute transport. Notably, experimental energy barriers and pre-exponential factors serve as an important bridge for concurrent simulation studies on molecular transport in subnanometre pores. Single channels from novel materials with well-defined geometry and chemistry open opportunities to unravel detailed mechanisms of solute transport. In long and heterogeneous pores of multipore membrane systems, the relative contribution of each hindrance mechanism for solute transport is poorly understood and cannot be properly distinguished.
