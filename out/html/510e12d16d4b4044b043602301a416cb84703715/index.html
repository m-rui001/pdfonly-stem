China) Keywords: single-crystal, ultrathin, oxygen vacancy, defect effect, electrocatalysis Abstract: The role of vacancy defects has been demonstrated to be positive in various energyrelated processes. However, introducing vacancy defects into single-crystalline nanostructures with given facets and studying their defect effect on electrocatalytic properties remains a great challenge. Here we deliberately introduce oxygen defects into single-crystalline ultrathin Co3O4 nanosheets with O-terminated {111} facets by mild solvothermal reduction using ethylene glycol under alkaline condition. As-prepared defect-rich Co3O4 nanosheets show a low overpotential of 220 mV with a small Tafel slope of 49.1 mV/dec for the oxygen evolution reaction (OER), which is among the best Co-based OER catalysts to date and even more active than the state-of-the-art IrO2 catalyst. Such vacancy defects are formed by balancing with reducing environments under solvothermal conditions, but are surprisingly stable even after 1000 cycles of scanning under OER working condition. theory plus U (DFT + U) calculation attributes the enhanced performance to the oxygen vacancies and consequently exposed 2nd layer Co metal sites, which led to the lowered OER activation energy of 2.26 eV and improved electrical conductivity. This mild solvothermal reduction concept opens a new door for the understanding and future designing of advanced defect-based electrocatalysts.

The over-consumed fossil fuels and rising energy demands have attracted much attention to electrocatalyst, which is at the heart of next-generation renewable energy systems, including water splitting, metal-air batteries, and fuel cells.[1-13] To date, noble metal-based materials are the most efficient choices, but their limited reserves and costly price have hindered the practical use.[14-16] Instead, 3d-transition metal oxides have been proposed as the most promising alternatives because of their considerable theoretical activity and relative abundance.[17-19] In particular, cobalt oxide is among the best candidates electrocatalysts for OER, a key half-reaction of water splitting.[20-22] However, tailoring their microstructure to enhance the performance and making them comparable to the noble-metal counterparts still remains a challenge. The most promising strategy to improve the intrinsic activity of cobalt oxide is to construct ultrathin two dimensional (2D) structures with tailored crystalline structure and surface electronic structure.[23-25] On the one hand, ultrathin 2D structures dramatically increase the percentage of surface unsaturated atoms, which benefits the catalysis to the utmost.[26-29] On the other hand, the OER performance of cobalt oxide is primarily attributed to the electronic structure of Co atoms, including their oxidation state, eg filling and O p-band center.[30-33] For instance, Liu and co-works have evidenced the Co(II) as the OER active site by in-operando analysis of Co3O4 with Co(II) and Co(III) substituted with inactive Zn(II) and Al(III), respectively.[34] Cui’s group has also tuned the electronic structure of Co by extracting lithium ions out of LiCoO2, thus improving their OER performance.[35] Until recently, vacancy defect 2

Page 5

has demonstrated its positive role in enhancing electrocatalytic reactions.[36-38] As typical examples, Qiao’s group boosted the OER activity of cobalt (II) oxide material by introducing O-vacancies through a high-temperature cation exchange process.[39] Xie and co-works have also created O-vacancies on the surface of NiCo2O4 nanosheets, leading to a higher content of Co(II) components and improved OER performance.[40] However, introducing vacancy defects commonly involves destructive and corrosive chemical/physical treatments (e.g. using NaBH4 and plasma technique), resulting in limited concentration of created-vacancies or abundant vacancies with destroyed crystalline/mirco structure.[38, 41-43] Therefore, introducing vacancy defects into single-crystalline nanostructures with given facets and getting fundamental insights into the defect effect on their electrocatalytic properties is still a challenging issue for their electrocatalysis optimization and the OER performance especially. Previously, we have in-situ topotactically reduced Ni(OH)2 to single-crystalline metallic Ni nanosheets and extended to alloy synthesis.[44, 45] In this study, we introduce oxygen vacancy defects on the surface of ultrathin Co3O4 nanosheets by slightly weakening the demonstrated reduction condition (i.e. stopping the reduction before metal0 formation). Interestingly, the asreduced Co3O4 nanosheets possessed massive oxygen defects but a single-crystalline nature with {111} facets dominated, thus providing an ideal platform for understanding the vacancy defect effect on their OER performance. High resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) and selected area electron diffraction (SAED) characterizations, together with Xray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) analysis confirmed the single-crystalline structure of Co3O4 nanosheets with a high concentration of O-vacancies. As a result, the as-prepared products showed greatly improved OER activity and highly stable operation, which is among the best Co-based OER electrocatalysts to date. Electrochemical evidence indicated this promoted performance was due to the larger electrochemical surface area (ECSA), more favorable OER kinetics, and better electrical 3

Page 6

conductivity. DFT + U calculation further rationalized such high intrinsic activity by suggesting that the surface O-vacancies would expose 2nd layer Co active sites, thus the OER process was altered and their activation energy exhibited lower values. Projected densities of states (PDOS) also suggested a more conductive structure of O-vacancy introduced Co3O4, which is in excellent agreement with the experimental findings. This work should not only provide a fundamental understanding of the defect effect on OER performance on specific {111} facets of cobalt oxide catalysts, but also be inspiring for designing better defect-based electrocatalysts for next-generation energy devices. The fabrication of single-crystalline ultrathin Co3O4 nanosheets with massive vacancy defects involved a hydrothermal and annealing method to prepare pristine Co3O4 nanosheets, followed by a mild solvothermal reduction process to create oxygen vacancies on the surface of such single-crystalline structures, as schemed in Figure 1. The representative scanning electron microscope (SEM) image clearly showed the morphology of the products. Figure 2a demonstrated that the pristine Co3O4 arrays exhibited dense and compact films consisting of many ultrathin nanosheets with an average thickness of ~ 10.5 nm. Moreover, the reduction treatment did not change their morphology and size, as shown in Figure 2b and S1, these reduced Co3O4 nanosheets were uniform in size and their average thickness was determined to be ~ 9.8 nm by randomly measuring the thickness of at least 100 nanosheets. The ultrathin nature means high surface area and efficient use of the active materials for catalysis.[27, 29] However, the surface of reduced Co3O4 nanosheets was found to be rougher than the pristine Co3O4 structures (Figure 2c and the inset of 2a), implying the occurrence of a surface reaction (e.g. etching or reducing). Interestingly, the crystal structure of the reduced Co3O4 was found to be single-crystalline, as demonstrated by the SAED pattern in the inset of Figure 2c, where the diffraction spots of {220} and {422} facets were clearly indexed, suggesting the {111} facet was mainly exposed.[46, 47] The single-crystalline nature of the 4

Page 7

reduced Co3O4 was further confirmed by HRTEM analysis, as shown in Figure S2, where the two-dimensional lattice fringe of {220} and {422} facets was clearly seen, which agreed well with the SAED results. Further, X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was employed to investigate the crystal structure of these products, as shown in Figure 2d, the diffraction peaks of pristine Co3O4 materials matched well with the pure spinel phased Co3O4 with a lattice constant of a = 0.808 nm (JCPDF: 43-1003). Meanwhile, the reduced Co3O4 exhibited a weaker but the same diffraction pattern as pristine Co3O4, suggesting no phase/structural destruction happened during our mild reduction process. It should be pointed that, if stronger reducing environment is employed, like using NaBH4, the Co3O4 structure would be reconstructed to form CoO phase.[41] This in turn demonstrates the advantage of our mild solvothermal reduction method in introducing vacancy defects to single-crystalline structures. Another notable point is the peaks of the reduced Co3O4 nanosheets turned obviously broad, which suggested the wider distribution of their lattice spacing, implying a disordered Co3O4 structure which might contain defects.[48] In fact, unlike highly thermodynamically stable metal oxides (e.g. TiO2), Co3O4 is a reducible material with relatively weak Co-O bond and low hopping barrier for the formation of oxygen vacancy.[41] To confirm the defect structure of reduced Co3O4 nanosheets, XPS measurements were performed. The XPS O 1s spectrum suggested an enrichment of O-vacancy on the surface of such reduced Co3O4 nanosheets (Figure 2e).[39] The Co 2p spectrum was also examined to get better understanding of their surface properties, as shown in Figure 2f, the two fitted peaks for Co 2p3/2 were found to be Co(III) (779.2 eV) and Co(II) (780.8 eV), respectively. Accordingly, the surface Co(II)/Co(III) ratio of reduced Co3O4 nanosheets was determined to be 1.29, which was higher than that of pristine Co3O4 (1.04), indicating the created surface O vacancies were balanced by the conversion of Co(III) to Co (II).

To reveal the local chemical environment of such reduced Co3O4 nanosheets, X-ray absorption near edge structure (XANES) measurements were performed. In the Co K-edge XANES spectra (Figure 3a), a reduced electronic state of Co component in reduced Co3O4 was observed. This should be attributed to the formation of O vacancy defects, which matched well with our XPS results. To better understand the fine structural environment of the reduced Co3O4, pair distribution function (PDF) and extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) analysis were involved. It was demonstrated from PDF data (Figure 3b) that pristine Co3O4 had well-resolved peaks and strong peak intensity, while the PDF peaks of the reduced nanosheets turned broader and the intensity became lower. As PDF is a weighted histogram showing the distribution of interatomic distances inside a material, such peak broadening and low intensity indicated a large variation of interatomic distances which further suggested the structure was rich in vacancy defects. This finding was also supported by EXAFS analysis and the corresponding Fourier transformed data (Figure 3c and 3d). Particularly, the Fourier transformed data clearly showed that the peak intensities for Co coordination bond were significantly lowered for reduced nanosheets, indicating the Co coordination numbers of reduced nanosheets were much lower than that of pristine Co3O4, which suggested the high concentration of O vacancy defects in reduced Co3O4 nanosheets, agreeing with the O 1s and Co 2p XPS results in Figure 2d and 2e. Additionally, it should be noted that this lowered Co coordination numbers suggested an increased disorder degree, implying the presence of many dangling bonds and surface unsaturation. Such distortion should in turn help to decrease the surface energy, thus endowing them with excellent stability.[49, 50] The presence of massive defect sites usually improved the catalytic activity of these singlecrystalline Co3O4 nanosheets, which was herein carefully investigated as electrocatalysts for OER. Pristine Co3O4 and commercial Ir/C catalysts (Figure S3) were used as references. 6

Page 9

Figure 4a compared the IR-corrected OER polarization curves of O-vacancy rich Co3O4 and pristine Co3O4 catalysts (here denoted as O-Co3O4 and P-Co3O4, respectively) in 1 M KOH solution at a slow scan rate of 1 mV/s to minimize capacitive current after tens of cyclic voltammetric scans to reach a relatively stable state. The O-Co3O4 catalysts displayed a noticeable onset potential (~ 1.45 V versus reversible hydrogen electrode, RHE), which was much lower than that of P-Co3O4 (~ 1.53 V vs. RHE). This O-Co3O4 material is among the best Co-based OER catalysts to our knowledge (Table S1), and even highly comparable to the state-of-the-art Ir/C catalyst (Figure S4). Besides, for the O-Co3O4 electrode, a distinct oxidation peak emerged at ~ 1.3 V, which should be assigned to the Co(II)/Co(III) oxidation. This agreed well with our XPS and XANES analysis that there were relatively more Co(II) sites present in reduced Co3O4 nanosheets. This Co oxidation peak was also found in the CV curves after LSV characterization (Figure S5), suggesting the generated oxygen species during OER cannot oxidize the O-Co3O4 structure back to pristine Co3O4, implying a good structural stability. The Tafel plots (Figure 4b) also suggested the improved OER performance of O-Co3O4, in which a much smaller Tafel slope was revealed (~ 49.1 mV/dec), suggesting better intrinsic activity than that of P-Co3O4 (~ 72.3 mV/dec). Such higher intrinsic activity was also suggested by their turnover frequency (TOF), as shown in Figure S6, when the overpotential of 200 mV was applied, a TOF of 0.39 s-1 was recorded for the O-Co3O4 catalyst, which was more than doubled that of the P-Co3O4 (0.16 s-1), further confirming the outstanding intrinsic activity of such O-Co3O4 catalyst in OER catalysis. To trace the source for such OER activity enhancement, electrochemical double layer capacitance (Cdl) measurement was employed. As shown in Figure 4c, S7 and Table S2, the O-Co3O4 and PCo3O4 electrode possessed a Cdl value of 5982 uF/cm2 and 4716 uF/cm2, respectively. Since Cdl could be charged on almost every electrochemical active site, it fully reflects the ECSA of an electrocatalyst.[5] Therefore, the enlarged ECSA of O-Co3O4 electrode should partly benefit their OER performance. BET analysis was also employed to provide both surface area and 7

Page 10

pore size distribution information, as shown in Figure S8, the surface area of reduced Co3O4 catalysts (29.15 m2/g) was larger than that of pristine Co3O4 (21.37 m2/g), which agreed fairly with our ECSA result. The pore size distribution of reduced Co3O4 was pretty similar to that of pristine Co3O4, implying our mild solvothermal method didn’t change the structure of pristine Co3O4 material. Moreover, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), a powerful tool to study the electrode kinetics in catalytic reactions, was also performed.[51] The Nyquist plots of the O-Co3O4 and P-Co3O4 electrodes under the same potential were shown in Figure 4e. The O-Co3O4 electrode showed a smaller charge transfer resistance (~ 1.9 Ω) than that of P-Co3O4 (~ 2.5 Ω), demonstrating an accelerated OER kinetics for O-Co3O4 electrode. Furthermore, the solution resistance of O-Co3O4 electrode (~ 1.1 Ω) was found to be smaller than that of P-Co3O4 (~ 1.4 Ω), suggesting higher electrical conductivity of O-Co3O4, which should also help reduce the overpotential. This electrochemical analysis confirmed the outstanding OER performance of as-prepared O-Co3O4 electrode and suggested such high intrinsic activity enhancement is attributed to the accelerated OER kinetics and improved electrical conductivity. Besides the outstanding intrinsic activity, this O-Co3O4 material also showed good long-term stability, the polarization curve of O-Co3O4 catalyst after 1,000 cycles almost overlaid the curve of its first cycle and a stable current of ~ 10 mA∙cm-2 was observed for 15 h operating, indicating a good durability of the O-Co3O4 catalyst (Figure 4e). The nearly unchanged morphology and O-vacancies concentration of O-Co3O4 electrode after electrochemical testing further confirmed their stability (Figure 4f and S9). In addition, the fitted O 1s peak at ~ 532.6 eV is associated with the surface-adsorbed OH or water molecule,[39] which was found to be a little bit stronger after OER testing. This also indicated the adsorbed oxygen groups cannot oxidize the O-vacancy structure back to pristine Co3O4, suggesting a highly active and stable electrochemical behavior, thus making these O-Co3O4 material promising for practical use.

It is reasonable that defect-rich structures boost the catalytic activity since the Co chemical and structural environment in O-Co3O4 was finely tuned after the introduction of vacancy defects, which has been probed by our XPS, XAFS, and electrochemical analysis. Since the cubic Co3O4 sheet structure is dominated by {111} crystal orientation (as demonstrated by HRTEM and SAED analysis),[46, 47] we compared the oxidation reaction on the {111} plane of Co3O4 model with/without oxygen vacancy. The computational results were shown in Figure 5 and S10-S12. Noted that the {111} facets of pristine Co3O4 structure is terminated with O atoms on the surface, O was therefore proposed as the active sites, as shown in Figure 5a. After calculating the free energy plots (Figure 5b), we found the complete OER pathways on perfect Co3O4 surface was comprised of five elementary steps with intermediates 1-5, which was quite in accord with Wang’s findings.[52] For the sake of simplicity, only the central catalytic site (where the blue molecular oxygen evolves) was drawn in the reaction circle (Figure 5c). The OER process on such oxo-covered {111} surface included multi-steps like water dissociation, proton jumping and water addition. As a result, it needed a large overpotential (i.e. 2.46 eV, Figure 5b) to overcome the energy barrier and thus not kinetically favorable. However, with a surface oxygen vacancy introduced, a second-layered Co atom became exposed and turned to be an OER active site with the help of neighboring O atoms, as demonstrated in Figure 5a and 5d. We applied bader charge analysis of the Co site on this {111} surface after introducing O defect, as shown in Figure S11, the increased bader charge value suggested a higher electron concentration around the metal Co sites after introducing O vacancy, indicating a lowered valence state, this matched well with our experimental results (XPS and XANES shown in Figure 2e and 3a, respectively). More importantly, owing to the synergies between such 9

Page 12

resultant Co site and the surrounding O atoms, the mechanistic OER pathway on the Ovacancy containing Co3O4 model was fundamentally altered. Based on the investigation of the Gibbs free energy of the evolution (Figure 5b), we found a lower reaction activation energy value (2.24 eV) on the O-vacancy containing {111} surface, suggesting a more favorable kinetics and higher OER intrinsic activity. Besides, the electronic structures of Co3O4 model with/without oxygen vacancy were also calculated to understand this O-vacancy effect on {111} surface in depth. As shown in the PDOSs curves (Figure S13), the bandgap between the valence and conduction bands of pristine Co3O4 structure was calculated to be ~ 1.93 eV, while a narrower bandgap (~ 1.74 eV) was observed after the introduction of surface O vacancies, indicating a more conductive electronic structure, which agreed well with our electrochemical analysis (EIS shown in Figure 4d). Thus, the theory and experiment are in an excellent agreement, suggesting that by creating oxygen vacancy defects on the {111} facets of single-crystalline Co3O4 structure, the OER kinetics on the catalysts’ surface and the electrical conductivity can be facilitated, finally leading to the significantly enhanced intrinsic electrocatalytic activity. In summary, we have demonstrated a solvothermal reduction strategy for defect-rich single-crystalline Co3O4 structures. Ethylene glycol in alkaline condition created a mild reducing environment for the creation of massive oxygen vacancies on the {111} facets of single-crystalline ultrathin Co3O4 nanosheets, which provided an ideal model for understanding the vacancy defect effect on their electrocatalytic properties. DFT + U calculations suggested a more favorable OER kinetics and better electrical conductivity. They are responsible for the significantly improved, almost the best, Co-based intrinsic OER activity. This study demonstrates on defect effect on {111} facets of single-crystalline ultrathin Co3O4 nanosheets, and reveals the important role of 2nd layer metallic ions, indicating the advantages of the vacancy defects on specific crystal facets in tuning the OER 10

kinetics and enhancing the electrocatalytic intrinsic activity, hence shedding light on future design and fabrication of advanced nanocatalysts for fuel cells, metal-air batteries, and other next-generation energy devices.

Experimental Section Preparation of single-crystalline ultrathin Co3O4 nanosheets with O-vacancy defects: The synthesis of single-crystalline ultrathin Co3O4 nanosheets with oxygen vacancies included the preparation of pristine ultrathin Co3O4 nanosheets array and a subsequent solvothermal reduction process to create O-vacancy on the surface. In a typical experiment, a piece of nickel foam (approximately 2 cm × 3 cm) was cleaned by concentrated HCl solution, ethanol and distilled water with assistance of ultrasonication for several minutes. Afterward, the purified Ni foam was put against the wall of a Teflon-lined stainless steel autoclave (40 mL) which contained a homogeneous solution of Co(NO3)2∙6H2O (2 mmol), NH4F (8 mmol), CO(NH2)2 (10 mmol) and 36 ml distilled water. Then the autoclave was sealed and maintained at 100 oC for 6 h to form Co(OH)2 nanosheet arrays. To prepare ultrathin Co(OH)2 sheet arrays, a secondary hydrothermal reaction was adopted. Typically, the obtained Co(OH)2 nanosheet arrays were put into the solution containing 2 mmol Co(NO3)2∙6H2O and 4 mmol hexamethylenetetramine (HMT) and 36 ml distilled water in autoclaves, and maintained at 120 oC for 12 h. After a further calcination at 250 oC for 3 h, ultrathin Co3O4 nanosheets arrays were obtained. The O vacancy-rich Co3O4 nanosheets were then prepared by a solvothermal reduction method. Namely, the obtained ultrathin Co3O4 nanosheet arrays were soaked in a homogeneous solution of NaOH (36 mmol) and 36 ml ethylene glycol. After maintaining at 140 oC for 12 h, the product was taken out, washed with ethanol for several times and dried in a vacuum oven at 80 oC overnight.

Structural characterization: The morphologies of as-synthesized samples were characterized by field-emission scanning electron microscopy (SEM; JEOL JSM6335, 20 kV), transmission electron microscopy (TEM; FEI Tecnai G2 20) and high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM; JEOL, JEM-2100, 200 kV). X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were collected on Shimadzu XRD-6000 with Cu Kα radiation (40 kV, 30 mA, λ = 1.5418 Å), recorded with 2θ ranging from 30° to 80°. XPS measurements were carried out with a PHI Quantera II XPS Scanning Microprobe. N2 adsorption-desorption and pore size distribution of as-prepared samples were characterized by micromeritics ASAP 2460. X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) measurements were performed at 8-ID beamline of the National Synchrotron Light Source II (NSLS II) at Brookhaven National Laboratory in the transmission mode. The X-ray absorption near edge structure and Extended X-ray absorption fine structure spectra were processed using the Athena software package. The AUTOBK code was used to normalize the absorption coefficient, and separate the EXAFS signal, χ(k), from the isolate atom-absorption background. The extracted EXAFS signal, χ(k), was weighted by k3 to emphasize the high-energy oscillations and then Fourier-transformed in a k range from 3.0 to 12.5 Å-1 to analyze the data in R space. Total scattering pair distribution function experiments were carried out at beamline 28-ID-2 at NSLS-II of BNL using an X-ray energy of 66.7 keV (λ = 0.185794 Å) and an amorphous silicon area detector (Perkin-Elmer) to obtain data to large momentum transfer values. Data were integrated using the program Fit2D. PDFgetX3 was used to correct the data for background contributions, Compton scattering and detector effects, and to Fourier transform (Qmax = 23.5 Å) the data to generate G(r), the PDF. Electrochemical measurements: The OER electrocatalytic properties of the as-prepared materials were studied with a three-electrode system in 1 M aqueous KOH solution using an electrochemical workstation (CHI 660D, Chenhua, Shanghai). A saturated calomel electrode (SCE) and a platinum plate were used as the reference and the counter electrode, respectively. 12

Page 15

For work electrode preparation, the as-prepared Ni foam supported electrode should be cut to form a 1 cm × 1 cm catalyst area. For the preparation of Ir/C electrode, 5 mg of commercial 20% Ir/C powder and 50 μL of Nafion solution were dispersed in ethanol (1 mL) with the assistance of ultrasonication for at least 1 h to form a homogeneous catalyst ink. Afterwards, the Ir/C ink was loaded onto Ni foam (1 cm × 1 cm) using a micropipette to achieve a massloading of 3.2 mg/cm2, which corresponds to the loading mass of defect-rich Co3O4 on Ni foam. After twenty cyclic voltammetric scans, the polarization data were collected using linear sweep voltammetry at a scan rate of 1 mV/s to minimize capacitive current. AC impedance measurements were carried out in the same configuration at open circuit voltage from 0.1-105 Hz with an AC voltage of 5 mV. The stability of the electrode was measured by applying a constant potential to achieve an initial current density of 10 mA/cm2 for 15 hours. For comparison, commercial Ir/C catalysts were measured under the same condition. The ECSA was determined by measuring the capacitive current associated with double-layer charging from the scan-rate dependence of CVs. The potential window was 0-0.05 V (vs. SCE, 1M KOH). The scan rates were 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, and 2.5 mV/s. The double-layer capacitance (Cdl) was estimated by the slope obtained from plotting the current density at 0.025 V (vs. SCE, 1M KOH) against the scan rate. The TOF value was calculated by the equation: TOF = j∙S/4F∙n, where j is the current density (mA∙cm-2) at ƞ = 200 mV, S represents the surface area of asprepared electrode, the number 4 means a four-electron oxygen evolution reaction, F is the Faraday’s constant (96485.3 C/mol), and n represents the moles of Co atoms on the electrode which can be calculated by the loading weight and the molecular weight of the coated catalysts. For the sample characterization after OER testing, the as-tested electrode should be taken out of the electrolyte, washed with distilled water quickly and transferred into vacuum chamber before further characterization.

Calculation methods: All DFT calculations were carried out by Vienna ab-Initio Simulation Package (VASP). The projector augmented wave pseudopotentials method was used for describing electron–ion interactions. The Perdew–Burke–Ernzerh (PBE) exchange– correlation functional with the on-site Coulomb repulsion U term was used. In the present work, U = 3.50 for Co transition metal. All the atom positions in the bulk Co3O4 was optimized by the conjugate-gradient optimization procedure. The Brillouin zone integrations were performed using a 5 × 5 × 5 Monkhorst-Pack grid for the bulk. A spin-polarized approach was adopted. The k-point sampling consists of 3 × 3 × 1 Monkhorst-Pack points for all slab models. A vacuum of at least 16 Å were adopted along z-axis. During structure optimization, all energies change criterion was set to 10-4 eV, the maximum force was 0.05 eV/Å, the plane wave cutoff was set to 400 eV. In the simulation, the Co3O4 {111} surface with O termination was taken into consideration. To simulate the O vacancy structure on the Co3O4 {111} surface, an oxygen atom on the top layer was removed. Consequently, a secondlayered Co atom was exposed and became an active site on the surface. During structure optimization, the intermediates involved in OER were O*, OH*, OOH*, and O2*.

A mild solvothermal reduction method is put forward to introduce oxygen vacancy defects on the {111} facets of single-crystalline ultrathin Co3O4 nanosheets. The vacancy defects on the {111} facets lead to the exposure of 2nd layered Co metal sites, which promotes the electrocatalytic activity towards the oxygen evolution reaction.
