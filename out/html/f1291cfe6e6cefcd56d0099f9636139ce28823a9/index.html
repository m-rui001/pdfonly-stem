Introduction

World agricultural and food sector has important challenges to face in the 21st century. Amidst them, food safety and appropriate waste management stand out. Food wastes occupy an ever-increasing space in the waste treatment facilities and in the landfills. Nowadays, many residues derived from the whole Food Supply Chain (FSC) represent the wastage of valuable resources and an environmental problem. On the one hand, in the low-income and developing countries food waste causes severe socioeconomic impacts (Gustavsson et al.,2011). On the other hand, the consumers’ attitudes and the massive consumption of goods and services cause the production of huge amounts of household waste in the medium/high-income countries (Usubiaga et al., 2017). Food wastes can be classified into two main groups according to their origin: animal or vegetable. Within the former, the main sources of wastes are those derived from the dairy, meat, fisheries and seafood processing industries. As for the latter, different types of residues can be found depending on the source: cereals, roots and tubers, oil crops and pulses, and fruits and vegetables (Galanakis, 2012).

Logistic-related problems and their perishable character, among other factors, make these residues 2

Page 3

difficult to valorize. Moreover, a comprehensive characterization is needed to determine their composition and characteristics, owing to their heterogeneity. Nonetheless, all these residues have in common a high content in organic matter that is rich in various compounds, including carbohydrates, proteins, lipids and bioactive compounds (Kosseva, 2009). Food waste reduction and utilization to obtain value-added products have the capacity of increasing the efficiency of the FSC and reducing its associated costs thus improving the accessibility to food, and therefore, food security (Gustavsson et al., 2011). Furthermore, nowadays there is an ever- increasing demand of products with highly appreciated properties (nutritional, health care and cosmetic) ensuing from the present needs of the modern lifestyle. In this regard, fruit and vegetable wastes present the biggest potential due to their nature in addition of being among the categories of residues with largest production. Hence, the aim of this work is to critically review the main ways in which food waste can be valorized, with focus on fruit and vegetable wastes (FVW) as sources of natural products and bio-based chemicals. Large-scale production of value-added bio-based products from these sources could promote the necessary investments to reduce the negative socioeconomic and environmental impacts caused by food waste. This review has been structured as follows: first of all, the introductory section includes a detailed list of food waste definitions which is subjected to a critical discussion, given the controversy found in the literature, which leads to notable discrepancies and uncertainties among the different inventory data. Next, an overview is provided on the present status of food waste generation and impacts. This section is completed by presenting a discussion on food waste composition, with particular emphasis on fruit and vegetable wastes. Further on, there is a description of the different strategies adopted regarding food waste management, including both conventional technologies and a more in-depth discussion on emerging technologies as alternatives for food waste valorization. Focus is put on discussing different bio-based chemicals and bioactive compounds that can be obtained from

Food waste definitions As can be deduced from the different definitions found in the literature, there is not a consensus on the exact meaning of the term “Food Waste” (FW) thus becoming a matter of controversy. This is not a minor issue because the definition of FW is the basis for elaborating waste inventories. In consequence, it has led to significant discrepancies on FW production data depending on the source and at the same time owing to the data themselves being subjected to high uncertainties (Stenmarck et al., 2016). The term “food waste” (FW),refers to “fractions of food and inedible parts of food” removed from the FSC (Tostivint et al., 2016).Indeed, FW includes biomass removed from the FSC to be recovered or disposed of (including composted, crops ploughed in/not harvested, anaerobic digestion, bioenergy production, co-generation, incineration, disposal to sewer, landfill or discarded to sea). However, the term FW does not include those parts, edible or inedible, removed from the FSC but destined to animal feed or to bio-based chemicals production (Stenmarck et al., 2016; Tostivint et al., 2016). For this reason, a more general and useful term is Food Supply Chain Wastes (FSCW), which refers to all wastes generated throughout the whole FSC including the production of raw materials, the food processing industry and the distribution of products (Maina et al., 2017). In addition to these definitions, there exist other concepts that have been used for waste inventory elaboration. Food Loss (FL) is the loss of quantity or quality in food; Food Loss and Waste (FLW) refers to the sum of losses and elimination of edible parts from food bound to human consumption in all stages of the FSC (Gustavsson et al., 2011; Stenmarck et al., 2016); and food byproducts comprises the biomass and residues that can be treated and converted into value-added products (Galanakis,

2012).

Focusing on the fruits and vegetables sector, the most widely used term is “Fruit and Vegetable Waste” (FVW). However, this term is also subjected to certain debate, since different definitions of FVW have been found (Plazzotta et al., 2017). Considering all this, in the present review the term FVW will be used for designating all those parts from fruits and vegetables discarded during the agricultural and industrial processing, distribution and consumption stages of the FSC, which could be either edible or inedible, and that can be further processed and valorized in several ways. 1.2. Food waste generation and impacts The FAOhas estimated that around 1.3 billion tons per year of edible parts from foods destined to human consumption are discarded and wasted worldwide (Gustavsson et al., 2011). Of all the FSC waste generated worldwide per year, more than 88 million tons are generated in the European Union (data for the EU-28 countries in 2012) (Stenmarck et al., 2016), and it is estimated that this value will continue to grow up to 40% in the following years (Plazzotta et al., 2017). In North America (Canada, Mexico and the United States), FLW estimates across the whole FSC are of almost 170 million tons(CEC, 2019).Metrics for FLW worldwide are estimated at roughly one-third of the food produced (Blakeney, 2019). In the United States of America, the estimates on FLW indicate that 30 to 50 % of food produced is lost or wasted, depending on how FLW is defined (Muth et al., 2019). Data provided by the FAO (Figure 1) results in the following decreasing order of FLW generation among the different regions of the world: North America and Oceania > Europe including the Russian Federation > Japan, Republic of Korea and China > Latin America > North Africa, west and central Asia > Sub-Saharan Africa > South and south-eastern Asia (Gustavsson et al., 2011; HLPE, 2014).

This order also applies to FLW generation at the consumer level.

Critical discussion For the purpose of this review, it is important to distinguish between the conventional or traditional FW management practices and the novel or emerging valorization methods (Figure 3). This is because the former ones are based on biological or chemical transformations of the organic constituents of the wastes, normally leading to very simple molecules (CO2, CO, CH4, H2, H2O, NH3 and H2S), a solid that finally adopts a relatively inert form (stabilized sludge, compost, slag and ashes), and, in some instances, wastewater streams (mainly leachates). As a result, despite being capable of providing some useful products (namely biogas, bio-hydrogen, compost, heat and power), these technologies do not allow to recover complex components from FW. At the same time, the environmental risks do not 10

Page 11

disappear due to their impact on atmospheric air pollution, greenhouse gases emissions, and wastewater and solid wastes production. In contrast, emerging valorization methods are proposed for not destroying the nutrients and other valuable compounds. That way they can be isolated and used as such or transformed into other high value-added chemicals. Presumably, this is a more efficient approach, though great attention should be paid to the economic, environmental and natural resources consumption issues associated to these emerging practices to ensure their profitability and sustainability. In consequence, it is important to consider integral valorization approaches in which not only the most valuable components are recovered, but the remaining waste constituents are also conveniently treated. A straightforward option would be combining the emerging and the conventional methods. From the point of view of the waste management hierarchy, reducing and, if possible, avoiding food waste generation are the best managing options and must be promoted (Papargyropoulou et al.,2014). Valorization technologies are also preferable to conventional management methods because they contribute to reduce the amount of waste finally disposed of by recovering valuable components and reincorporating them into the FSC or for other end uses (pharmacy, cosmetics, etc.). In contrast, conventional technologies essentially follow an “end-of-pipe” approach, meaning that their aim is to minimize the impacts and damages caused to the human health and the environment, and finally disposing of the wastes already generated. Given the consumers’ raising awareness on these issues, the food production sector must be particularly sensitive toward any initiative leading to improved sustainability and reduced negative environmental and ecological impacts of their activities. Therefore, ‘green’ or sustainable production of food should be considered of strategic importance for the future evolution of this key socioeconomic sector.

2.1. Conventional food waste management technologies

2.1.1. Animal feeding This option should be considered carefully because not every animal can take every food/residue (Laufenberg et al., 2003). Moreover, there is a risk that residues destined to animal feeding may have toxic compounds that transmit diseases or might be unbalanced from a dietary point of view. Besides, the transportation, and eventually conservation costs associated make this alternative frequently unfeasible (Otles et al., 2015). Rendering FW safe by means of thermal treatments increases the costs as well. Nevertheless, as shown by Salemdeeb et al. (2017) under controlled conditions, animal feeding is clearly advantageous compared to other alternatives such as landfilling or anaerobic digestion. Benefits mainly come from the partial substitution of conventional feed, the production of which has significant environmental and health impacts. In fact, in some countries such as Japan and South Korea the shares of FW recycled as feed are as high as 35.9% and 42.5%, respectively. These practices are strictly regulated in those countries, whilst presently in the EU it is illegal to use most of food waste as animal feed (Salemdeeb et al., 2017). 12

2.1.2. Landfilling It is the most common and simple way to dispose of solid residues despite its environmental impact. In the case of FW this is mainly due to its high biodegradability associated to a high content in nutrients (Ravindran andJaiswal, 2016). Methane from landfills produced by the anaerobic biological degradation of the dumped organic matter is the third largest anthropogenic source of atmospheric methane emissions, accounting for around800 million tons of CO2-equivalent(Breeze, 2018). The produced gas, also known as biogas (a mixture mainly composed of CH4 and CO2), is very often emitted to the atmosphere. However, more and more sites are capturing biogas due to legal requirements and its interest as fuel to produce electric power or cogenerate heat and power in engine- generators. Furthermore, biogas can be further processed to separate CO2 and other minor components to obtain bio-methane (Pearse et al., 2018). In landfills, main macronutrients present in organic matter are hydrolyzed to soluble products and finally to biogas through methanogenesis. However, lignin and lignocellulose are recalcitrant under anaerobic conditions, so in the absence of a suitable pretreatment these fractions may inhibit cellulose bioavailability, thus reducing the biogas yield (Kibler et al.,2018). Another important environmental issue associated to landfilling is leachate formation, mainly originated by the infiltration of rainwater, which percolates through the waste layers and accumulates at the bottom of the landfill, although there is also a significant contribution from the wastes’ moisture. This liquid stream is a complex matrix that incorporates organic matter, inorganic salts, organic trace pollutants, and heavy metals at concentrations that depend on several factors such as weather (ambient temperature and annual rainfall), waste composition, landfill age and depth of the waste in the landfill, and landfill design and operation(Bhatt et al., 2017; Moody andTownsend, 2017). Leachate should be treated in appropriate facilities. Depending on its chemical composition, municipal wastewater treatment plants could treat them. Otherwise, dedicated installations are required.

The European Landfill Framework Directive (1999/31/EC) and the Waste Framework Directive (2008/98/EC) establish that the EU member states must minimize the volume of biodegradable residues disposed of in landfills and there are ongoing amendments to this and other Framework Directives to impose more restrictive limits on food waste landfilling(Council of the EU, 2017; Kosseva, 2013). As a result, the use of landfilling for municipal waste management reduced its share in the EU from 55% in 2000 to 24% in 2016(Eurostat, 2018). Part of this success is also due to the implementation of the Directive (1994/62/EC) on packaging and package waste. 2.1.3. Anaerobic digestion As mentioned in Section 2.1.2, biogas is a product of the anaerobic degradation of organic matter that takes place in landfills (Moral et al., 2018). Anaerobic digestion is a well-known technology for the management of manure and of sludge generated in wastewater treatment plants (Mata-Alvarez et al.,2000; Moral et al., 2018). Its application to FW and FVW valorization has an enormous potential although the implementation level is still low (Braguglia et al., 2018; Gunaseelan, 1997; Ren et al., 2018; Xu et al., 2018). Biological processes involved in the anaerobic digestion of the organic matter that constitutes FW are essentially the same as those taking place in a landfill. The main difference is that anaerobic digestion is carried out in specifically designed bioreactors (digesters) that allow controlling the main operating parameters. Particularly important is the control of temperature because the anaerobic bacteria involved are mesophilic or thermophiles, being active at temperatures within the 30-40 ºC or 50-60 ºC ranges, respectively (Kibler et al., 2018). Other important parameters are the organic acids profile, pH, alkalinity, C/N ratio and the presence of metals (Appels et al., 2008; Zhang et al., 2014). Owing to the control of the fermentation conditions, digesters allow to obtain much higher yields of methane than in landfills. Both CH4 (55-65 vol.%) and CO2 (30-40 vol.%) are the main products contained in biogas, together with H2 and H2S, among other gases, which are present in much lower concentrations. 14

Page 15

Biological degradation is a complex process that involves several steps (Appels et al., 2008): i) Hydrolysis. During this step, macromolecules such as polysaccharides, proteins and nucleic acids, are hydrolyzed by fermentative bacteria producing simple organic substances like amino acids, sugars, and fatty acids; ii) Acidogenesis. During the second step, acidogenic bacteria convert the hydrolysis products into volatile fatty acids (VFA’s), mainly acetic, propionic, butyric and valeric acids, alcohols, NH3, CO2, H2S and other products; iii) Acetogenesis. The next step involves the conversion by syntrophic acetogens of the higher organic acids and alcohols into acetic acid, CO2 and H2; iv) Methanogenesis. During the last step methane is formed by the action of two groups of methanogenic bacteria; one of them splits acetate ions into CH4 and CO2 whereas the other produces CH4 from the conversion of CO2 and H2. The global process is controlled by the rate of the hydrolysis step, which is the slowest one because the hydrolytic enzymes should be previously adsorbed onto the surface of the solid organic substrates. For that reason, it is beneficial to pretreat the substrates, for example by physical means (mechanical grinding, microwave or ultrasounds treatments), with the aim of breaking up aggregated particles and reducing their size. Other types of pretreatments, including chemical, thermal and biological have been developed as well (Appels et al., 2008; Zhang et al., 2014). Anaerobic digestion is most frequently performed in single-stage digesters operated in semi- continuous mode in which all the reactions involved in the several steps that comprise the degradation of organic matter take place simultaneously (Nagao et al., 2012). Performance of these bioreactors is assessed through the following parameters: organic loading rate (OLR), i.e. the mass flow rate of VS fed referred to the reactor volume; solids retention time (SRT); hydraulic retention time (HRT); methane yield as volume of methane produced per unit mass of VS, and the percentage of VS reduction or conversion. As highlighted by Nagao et al. (2012), proper anaerobic digestion of easily biodegradable substrates (such as most FW and FVW) relies on a balance between the rates of the hydrolysis and methanogenesis reactions. This is because methanogenic bacteria are more sensitive than the hydrolytic and acidogenic ones to the pH decrease occurring at high VFA’s concentration. 15

Page 16

Therefore, an excessively high OLR may provoke that the rates of hydrolysis and acidogenesis become higher than that of methanogenesis, leading to an irreversible acidification of the digester. These authors carried out the anaerobic digestion of household FW with high contents of vegetables (53.6%) and fruits (24.8%) at mesophilic conditions for 225 days. At OLR of 9.2 kg-VS/(m3·day) high VS reduction (91.8%) and methane yield (455 mL CH4/g-VS) were achieved. Working at wet (TS below 5%) to semi-dry (5-10% TS) conditions guaranteed high mass transfer rate of FVAs through the liquid phase and sufficiently high methanogenesis rate to avoid acidification despite the high OLRs employed. Under these conditions, a maximum OLR of ca. 10.5 kg-VS/(m3·day) was estimated for a single-stage digester fed with FW (Nagao et al., 2012). The solid fraction, or digested sludge, removed from the anaerobic digesters requires stabilization because still maintains biological reactivity that comes mainly from the unconverted VS fraction. An interesting valorization option for this stream is composting to obtain a product that can be used as soil amendment and source of nutrients in agriculture, as mentioned in Section 2.1.3 (Wang et al., 2017). The most valuable product of the anaerobic digestion is the methane contained in biogas, though the digestate obtained as byproduct of the digestion process may also be valorized, either straightforward as livestock bedding and as substrate in soil amendments, or by subsequent composting or vermicomposting treatments to obtain fertilizers. In addition to the process conditions and digester technology, methane production from FW is highly dependent on the substrate composition. As a result, in the case of the organic fraction of municipal wastes, methane yields vary amply between ca. 300 and 580 NL/kg-VS due to the high variability in composition (Campuzano and González- Martínez, 2016; Fisgativa et al., 2016). Gunaseelan (1997, 2004, 2007) has investigated extensively the potential of FVW for methane production through anaerobic digestion. The variability within this category is also very high: values between ca. 100 and 500 NL CH4/kg-VS have been reported. In general, methane yields are higher for fruits than for vegetables. For example, mango peels yield between 370 and 520 NL CH4/kg-VS depending on the fruit variety. In contrast, three varieties of 16

banana peels give similar yields around 320 NL CH4/kg-VS. Mandarin peels, pomegranate pomace and pineapple peels produce 486, 420, 357 NL CH4/kg-VS, respectively. On the other hand, cabbage and carrot peels give 240-310 NL CH4/kg-VS. Correlations have been found between methane yields and the carbohydrate, protein, lignin and cellulose contents of the substrates (Gunaseelan, 2007). 2.1.4. Composting and vermicomposting It is a technology that allows for the management of the wastes’ organic matter, and therefore it is well suited for FW and particularly for FVW. Environmental benefits arise from lower greenhouse gas emissions and leachate generation compared to landfilling. However, several requisites are needed: sorting of the wastes to separate the organic fraction, energy for mixing and aeration, water addition and a careful control of the operating conditions. In contrast to the processes taking place in landfills, composting involves the aerobic biological conversion of organic matter, thus producing CO2, N2, NH3 and a solid recalcitrant material often referred to as compost that contains humic substances. Compost is used as a kind of soil amendment that facilitates water retention thus reducing irrigation needs, though this use requires that heavy metals, pathogens and micropollutants are not present (Kibler et al., 2018). Critical composting process variables are pH, C/N ratio, moisture content, aeration rate, particle size, and porosity (degree of compaction of the organic matter). Under inappropriate conditions, odor emissions can represent a significant environmental impact and, at the same time, a low-quality compost is obtained(Cerda et al., 2018). More recently, vermicomposting of food wastes for the production of bio-fertilizers has been developed(Rorat & Vandenbulcke, 2019), which consists in the stabilization of waste organic matter through the joint action of earthworms and microorganisms. The final product obtained (vermicompost) presents better properties than conventional compost as concerns nutrient availability in soil (Suthar, 2009). Some recent studies have investigated the earth worm activity in vermicompost of food waste. It could be concluded that vermicomposting can effectively destroy bacterial pathogenic load, thus being more suitable than composting for the development of soil fertilizers (Soobhany, 2018). 17

In the EU, the environmental legislation has promoted composting over landfilling. As a result, the share of composting for municipal waste management has almost doubled from 9% in 2000 to 16% in 2016(Eurostat, 2018). In the US, that share has also risen in the last years, though at a lower rate than in the EU, reaching ca. 9% in 2014. In Japan, despite the huge amount of household FW generated (above 10 million tons per year), less than 6% is currently being composted, mainly due to scarce implantation of separation of the organic fraction at source. In the case of China, the rapid economic development is causing municipal wastes generation to increase up to impressive figures: 172 million tons in 2013, 60% of which are landfilled whereas less than 2% are composted. This is due to the poor quality of the compost produced, which has high contents of heavy metals as a consequence of deficient separation practices (Wei et al., 2017). 2.1.4. Thermal treatments Incineration, or combustion, is a practice that can be regarded advantageous from the point of view of the significant reduction in the waste volume finally disposed of, thus contributing to extend landfills’ lifetime. The incineration plant should be carefully designed and operated to prevent serious atmospheric air pollution issues, though modern incineration plants integrate suitable air pollution control technologies resulting in extremely low emissions of contaminants (Kajiwara et al., 2017). However, its implementation requires of higher investments and operating costs because of the great amount of energy needed. This is due to the low calorific content and high moisture contents of FW, and particularly FVW, which leads to the need for co-firing FW with other wastes with high-energy contents. If plastics are used, in addition to the risk of generating very harmful air pollutants (dioxins among others), net CO2 emissions to the atmosphere increase significantly (Kibler et al., 2018). If necessary, primary energy (e.g. natural gas) can be used to sustain suitably high operating temperatures. Combustion heat can be partially recovered and used in the incineration process itself (for instance, to preheat air) or destined to power generation according to the needs and interests.

As in the case of composting, the share of incineration among the technologies used for municipal waste management in the EU has almost doubled, increasing from 15% in 2000 to 27% in 2016(Eurostat, 2018). By way of comparison, this share in China is only slightly higher (ca. 30%)(Wei et al., 2017), whereas in Japan incineration is the main technology for solid waste management (share of ca. 80% in 2012) (Yolin, 2015), due to the pressure posed by the scarce availability of land, which leads to policies for minimizing landfilling. Gasification and pyrolysis constitute other types of thermal treatments in which organic matter is converted into a variety of products (De Corato et al., 2018; Otles et al., 2015; Pham et al., 2015). In both treatments, the gaseous stream obtained contains hydrogen and CO, among other components, which can be further processed to obtain synthesis gas, also known as syngas, (a mixture of H2 and CO in different proportions) that can be used as feedstock for chemicals synthesis, or it can be straightforward used as fuel for power and/or heat generation(Girotto et al., 2015; Kibler et al., 2018). In the case of pyrolysis, the thermal decomposition of the wastes is conducted in a non-oxidizing atmosphere and in the absence of any other reactant, resulting in the production of three fractions (gas, liquid and solid), the proportion of which depends on the operating conditions, mainly process temperature, heating rate and residence time of the vapors (García et al., 2015). Food waste valorization Wastes produced from the agri-food industry constitute a loss of valuable biomass and nutrients, since these wastes have the potential to become useful products or even become raw materials for other industries. Laufenberg et al. (2003)proposed a clean production strategy, which they called the "holistic concept of food production". This approach consists of integrating environmental aspects into the product development and food production, always pursuing the highest product quality and safety, and the highest efficiency. In order to develop clean production processes these authors proposed waste reduction and recycling of valuable byproducts as a short-term goal, the development of new and efficient production processes to add value to residues in the medium term and the development of innovative products such as functional foods in the long term. In this context, there is an ever- increasing interest in obtaining high value-added bioactive compounds through FW valorization. These bio-compounds could be used as functional food ingredients, as nutraceuticals or for obtaining other valuable bio-products, such as antibiotics, enzymes, biopolymers and biofuels (Dahiya et al.,

2018; Kosseva, 2009).

However, most of the recently developed valorization techniques having high potential for residual biomass exploitation are not yet implemented on a large scale in the routine management of agri-food residues. The low degree of implementation can be explained as a consequence of various factors. From an economic point of view, it is very costly to pick up the residues in the origin and transport them to the processing plant. Moreover, from their generation through their final processing these residues decompose and suffer degradation. This makes it necessary to adopt conservation methods (refrigeration, addition of preservatives), which also hinder the process and raises the overall costs. Therefore, it would be advisable to locate the valorization centers in areas having a high density of agri-food industries. Another issue that restrains the valorization of these residues is the difficulty in scaling-up the technologies to a full industrial scale, while making them economically viable (Galanakis et al., 2015). Processing costs and their impact on the final price of the products obtained will exert a definitive role in making viable a given conversion/valorization process (Ravindran andJaiswal, 2016). The development of the above-mentioned holistic concept of food production is very complex as it requires of a strong degree of involvement by all the key actors including consumers and policymakers(Laufenberg et al., 2003). It is necessary to adopt multidisciplinary approaches that allow acquiring the necessary degree of innovation and achieve the ambitious targets of a low-waste economy and a sustainable societal development. On the economical side, biofuel applications ($ 200– 400/ton biomass) create more value compared to generating electricity ($ 60–150/ton biomass) and cattle feed ($ 70–200/ton biomass), but the turnover that could be generated by high-value bio- chemicals is much higher and estimated to be around $ 1000/ton biomass(Tuck et al., 2012). Enzymes Enzymes are proteins that act as active and exceptionally selective catalysts of given chemical reactions under mild conditions. Commercial enzymes are generally expensive, foremost due to the high manufacturing and raw materials costs. Plastic materials, or simply plastics, constitute the most important category of polymers. Their production amounted up to 299 million tons in 2013 (Rivero et al., 2017).Typical examples are (low- and high-density) poly(ethylene) (PE), poly(propylene), and poly(ethylene terephthalate) (PET). Plastics are commonly used in many applications by virtue of their properties, such as mechanical resistance, chemical inertia, lightness, easy processability, versatility, and low cost. However, because of their very low degradation rate under ambient conditions, plastics are among the most abundant and persistent environmental pollutants(Shah et al., 2008). For these reasons, there is an enormous interest in developing biodegradable plastics from renewable resources, especially for short-life applications (Gonçalves de Moura et al., 2017). Biopolymers can consist on naturally occurring macromolecules (e.g. cellulose, lignin, starch, proteins, natural rubber, etc.) or synthetic products. PLA is a linear aliphatic polyester produced through the polymerization of lactic acid (2-hydroxy propionic acid, LA). Current global PLA production capacity is ca. 600,000 tons per year. Production is led by Cargill Dow Polymer LLC, with a share of about 75% of the total capacity (Rivero et al., 2017). PLA is fully biodegradable and biocompatible (bioabsorbable), and has been classified as GRAS by the US Food and Drug Administration(Farah et al., 2016). PLA exhibits optical, mechanical, thermal, and barrier properties comparable to those of commodity polymers such as poly(propylene), PET, and poly(styrene). These properties, together with its GRAS classification, allow using PLA as substitute for the commodity polymers in all food-packaging applications (Gonçalves de Moura et al., 2017). Moreover, PLA is also highly appreciated because of its important biomedical applications in fields such as suture, bone fixation material, drug delivery, and tissue engineering. PLA is degraded by simple hydrolysis of the ester bonds, which implies that the presence of enzymes is not strictly 35

necessary(Iqbal et al., 2008; Lasprilla et al.,2012). LA is a chiral molecule that exists as two enantiomers, L-LA and D-LA, or (S,S) and (R,R), respectively. As a result, PLA presents stereoisomers: poly(L-LA) (PLLA, Fig. 3A) and poly(D-LA) (PDLA), which are semi-crystalline, and poly(DL-LA) (PDLLA) that is amorphous. Polymer conformation according to the stereochemical makeup of the monomers and the molecular weight are the main characteristics controlling the physical and mechanical properties. In this sense, PLA containing more than 93 % L-LA is semi-crystalline whereas PLA with 50-93 % L-LA is amorphous. On the other hand, PLLA with molecular weight below 300,000 g/mol develops 30-50 % crystallinity. For those reasons, L-LA is the most interesting enantiomer from the practical point of view. Auras et al.(2004) and Farah et al.(2016) have extensively reviewed the relationships between the composition and structure of polylactide polymers and their properties. Brittleness can be highlighted as one of the main shortcomings of PLA. However, some applications require specific end-use properties. For those reasons, much effort is being directed toward improving some properties by means of PLA blending with other polymers, and obtaining composites by addition of reinforcing fibers, micro- and nanofillers, and additives within PLA matrix (Murariu and Dubois, 2016). Moro et al. (2017) have reported the use of dried passion fruit peel, which is very rich in fiber (ca. 60 %), as reinforcement of PLA. PLA can be produced from polycondensation of LA, and ring-opening polymerization (ROP) of a cyclic LA dimer named lactide. Even though polycondensation is the least expensive route, it is difficult by this way to control and obtain high molecular weights, and thus synthesize copolymers. In consequence, ROP of lactides is the most widely used method for synthesizing PLA (Kricheldorf et al.,1996). The two main methods to produce LA are bacterial fermentation of carbohydrates and chemical synthesis (lactonitrile hydrolysis). On the one hand, fermentation is the preferred way within the context of sustainable PLA production because it allows valorizing substrates such as FVW containing polysaccharides. On the other hand, chemical synthesis presents serious drawbacks such as limited production capacity, inability to produce selectively the desired L-LA enantiomer, and high 36

production costs. Approximately 90 % of the LA produced worldwide is obtained through bacterial fermentation(Castro-Aguirre et al., 2016; Lasprilla et al., 2012). In practice, a homofermentative method with Lactobacillus genus species is the most frequently employed due to the higher LA yields obtained. Bacteria that predominantly yield L-LA are L.amylophilus, L.bavaricus, L.casei, L.maltaromicus, and L.salivarius. Conversely, strains such as L.delbrueckii, L.jensenii, or L.acidophilus yield D-LA or mixtures of both enantiomers (Madhavan Nampoothiri et al., 2010). If LA is used for PLA synthesis destined to medical and food-packaging applications, it must be isolated and purified after fermentation. Bacteria metabolize the simple sugars obtained from the hydrolysis of polysaccharides present in the feed. FVW that can be used as raw materials are sugarcane and cassava bagasses, starchy materials from potato, tapioca, corn stover, wheat straw, wheat bran, lignocellulose/hemicellulose hydrolysates, Jerusalem artichokes, beet molasses, rye flour, sweet sorghum, carrot processing waste and molasses spent wash(Castro-Aguirre et al., 2016; Lasprilla et al., 2012; Madhavan Nampoothiri et al., 2010; Moro et al., 2017). Global PHAs production capacitycan be estimated at ca. 80,000 tons/year, being Metabolix® the world leading company with a share of ca. 60%. However, as highlighted byJiang et al. (2016) PHAs are currently being produced mainly from food crops (sugar cane, beet, corn and vegetable oils). This fact also has a very negative impact on PHAs cost, which is ca. 15 times higher than that of conventional petroleum-derived plastics. In addition to the raw materials cost, with a share of about 50%, there is also a great contribution of the isolation and purification costs (ca.30%) (Możejko-Ciesielska and Kiewisz, 2016). Therefore, it is essential to develop processes that do not compete with the food sector. In this regard, there is an opportunity for employing FW and FVW as indicated later. PHAs are polyesters produced from the polymerization of a variety of R-hydroxyalkanoic acids by heterotrophic microorganisms, which accumulate the biopolymers in the cytoplasm as carbon and 37

energy storage materials. Therefore, the cell density and the capacity of cells for accumulating PHAs are the limiting factors for the amount of PHAs that can be produced by fermentation employing microorganisms (Steinbüchel, 2001). About 150 different monomers (including straight, branched, saturated, unsaturated and aromatic structures), and over 90 genera of microbial species have been reported as involved in the synthesis of PHAs(Anjum et al., 2016; Możejko-Ciesielska andKiewisz, 2016). PHAs can be classified into two groups based on the number of carbon atoms in the R radical group of the R-hydroxyalkanoic acid monomers: short-chain length (3-5 C atoms) or medium-chain length (6-14 or more than 14 C atoms) PHAs. Examples of the former group are poly(3-hydroxybutyrate) P(3HB), poly(4-hydroxybutyrate) P(4HB), poly(3-hydroxyvalerate) P(3HV) and the copolymer P(3HB-co-3HV). Examples of the medium-chain length PHAs are poly(3-hydroxyhexanoate) P(3HHx) and poly(3-hydroxyoctanoate)(Anjum et al., 2016). P(3HB) is the simplest and first PHA known (Fig. 3B). It can be synthesized with molecular weights ranging from 50,000 to more than 1,000,000g/mol, which places poly(3-hydroxybutyrate) among the commercial polymers. It is biocompatible and biodegradable through the action of enzymes from filamentous fungi and bacteria. However, P (3HB) is also degraded by hydrolysis, among other processes (e.g. thermal). Precisely the fact that P (3HB) degrades by hydrolysis allows its use in medical applications. Physical properties of P (3HB) are similar to those of poly(propylene), though poly(3-hydroxybutyrate) is stiffer. Because of its water insolubility and low permeability to O2 and CO2, P (3HB) has good properties for food package applications. It is also piezoelectric (which is infrequent in plastics), thus finding application in bone prostheses because piezoelectricity stimulates bone growth and aids in wound healing. Additional medical uses are heart valves and other cardiovascular applications as well as matrices for controlled drug delivery(Rai et al., 2011; Rivero et al., 2017). Production of PHAs from inexpensive substrates as wastes is widely accepted as a viable strategy, although the scale up to the industrial application is still challenging. Biofuels Biofuels, that is, fuels derived from biomass sources with low carbon footprint that do not compete with the food sector, are recognized as a key element of an awaited sustainable energy system. This field is in continuous evolution, and nowadays, despite significant progresses, biofuels production still achieves a very low share of the global demand for transportation fuel (3.5% in 2013). The ways in which lignocellulosic biomass can be converted into target molecules that constitute advanced biofuels through the so-called platform chemicals have been recently reviewed by Leitner et al. (2017). These authors have followed an original approach based on developing criteria for biofuel design, taking into account the performance and pollutant emissions characteristics of the modern thermal engines used in the transportation sector. These interesting ideas could be adapted as well to the lignocellulosic fraction of FVW. By contrast, this review is focused on more conventional biofuels such as biogas, bio-hydrogen, bioethanol and biobutanol whose production from the wastes considered in this work has been reported in the literature (Beyene et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2016).

Biogas valorization as a biofuel offers different possibilities. It can be directly used as heating/cooking fuel or in onsite electric power generation in gas (micro)turbines and engines, as well as in co- generation installations (Budzianowski, 2016; Sun et al., 2015). However, biogas upgrading to biomethaneis one of the most widely implemented technologies for biogas exploitation nowadays, especially in Europe. By way of example, in the period comprised between 2001 and 2011 the total installed capacity for biogas upgrading grew in Europe form less than 10,000 N m3/h (raw gas) in 2001 to over 160,000 N m3/h (raw gas) in 2011. Such approach results in the possibility of obtaining a valuable biofuel that can be injected into the natural gas grid or be directly used as vehicle fuel in compressed natural gas (CNG) engines, though the methane content in the upgraded biogas must be typically over 95 % and sometimes up to 98+ %. In consequence, the upgrading stepinvolves the removal of gaseous products present in the raw biogas, mainly carbon dioxide but also other minor impurities such as hydrogen sulfide or siloxanes. Typical biogas compositions can have up to 35-45 vol. % of CO2. Therefore, several biogas upgrading technologies may be used to remove CO2 and other impurities from the biogas. The most widely implemented are pressure swing adsorption (PSA) and water or amine scrubbing (absorption processes), though in recent years, membrane separation technologies are gaining increasing interest. Furthermore, the production of syngas (a gas mainly composed of H2 and CO) from biogas reforming has also attracted the attention of several researchers, since this alternative would benefit from the flexibility of valorizing raw biogas as a valuable intermediategaseous product. Syngas can be used as raw material in the production of chemicals, synthetic fuels or be further used as biofuel, either straightforward or via upgrading to biohydrogen (Navarro-Puyuelo et al., 2019). As for bio-hydrogen production, the so-called dark fermentation (DF) method is among the most investigated ones(Das andVeziroglu, 2008). Microorganisms producing hydrogen upon fermentation are known as phototrophic (photo fermentation) or non-phototrophic (DF) depending on whether light is used or not as energy source. DF takes place through the action of anaerobic bacteria, which can be 40

described as strictly anaerobic (e.g. Clostridium) or facultative (e.g. Enterobacteraerogenes, Escherichia coli), the latter providing lower hydrogen yields. As discussed by Martínez-Merino et al. The preferred substrates are monosaccharides obtained from the hydrolysis of polysaccharides present in FW and FVW, as already indicated for biogas production. This two-stage concept for the simultaneous production of hydrogen and methane is being currently developed. Nevertheless, its application to FW is considered very challenging because the composition of wastes is complex and little is still known on the effects of many components other than polysaccharides on the global process (Algapani et al., 2018).
