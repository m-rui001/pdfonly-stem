Abstract Garnet-type solid-state electrolytes have attracted extensive attention due to their high ionic conductivity, approaching 1 mS/cm, excellent environmental stability, and wide electrochemical stability window, from lithium metal to ~6 V. However to date, there is little success on the development of high-performance solid-state batteries using these exceptional materials, the major challenge being the high solid-solid interfacial impedance between the garnet electrolyte and electrode materials. In this work, we effectively address the large interfacial impedance between lithium metal anode and the garnet electrolyte using ultrathin aluminum oxide (Al2O3) by atomic layer deposition (ALD). Li7La2.75Ca0.25Zr1.75Nb0.25O12 (LLCZN) is the garnet composition of choice in this work due to its reduced sintering temperature and increased lithium ion conductivity. A significant decrease of interfacial impedance, from 1710 Ω∙cm2 to 1 Ω∙cm2, was observed at room temperature, effectively negating the lithium-metal/garnet interfacial impedance. Experimental and computational results reveal that the oxide coating enables wetting of metallic lithium in contact with the garnet electrolyte surface and the lithiated-alumina interface allows effective lithium ion transport between the lithium metal anode and garnet electrolyte. We also demonstrate a working cell with lithium metal anode, garnet electrolyte and a high voltage cathode by applying the newly developed interface chemistry.

Keywords: garnet electrolyte, Li metal anode, interface impedance, atomic layer deposition, Al2O3, all solid-state batteries

Solid-state lithium batteries (SSLiBs) provide potential solutions to the primary problems encountered in traditional lithium (Li)-ion batteries with liquid electrolytes, such as poor safety, limited voltage, unstable solid-electrolyte interphase (SEI) formation, and poor cycling performance. Solid-state electrolytes (SSE) are the enabling material for the successful development of SSLiBs. A range of SSEs have been investigated, such as LiSICON,1 thioLiSICON,2 NASICON-type Li-conductors,3,4 perovskites,5 antiperovskites,6 LiBH4,7 sulfidebased glass/ceramic,8-9 garnet10-14 etc. Steady progress on the improvement of Li-ion conductivity in SSEs has resulted in record high conductivity of 10-2 S/cm in sulfide-base electrolyte,15 which rivals the conductivity of organic liquid electrolytes. However, for full cell development, a stable interface with small interfacial impedance between SSE and electrodes is critical. Quite a few successful approaches have been applied to reduce interfacial impedance between SSE and cathodes, including surface coating,16-18 interface softening,19,20 and buffer layer, e.g. LiNbO2,21 Nb,22 BaTiO3,16 and additive in cathode composite, e.g. LBO.23 The interface at the anode side, however, is rarely explored,24 especially when metal Li is used. This is likely due to the fact that many solid electrolytes including perovskite-type (Li, La)TiO3, NASICON (especially Ti-based materials), and sulfide-based glass electrolytes are not stable against metallic Li. Given the fact that Li metal has the highest capacity (3860 mAh/g) and the lowest potential (-3.040 V vs. standard hydrogen electrode) for use as an anode, solving the interfacial impedance between the SSE and Li metal is extremely critical for high energy density SSLiB development. Garnet electrolytes are very attractive for SSLiBs because they (1) have a wide electrochemical window and the most stable interface against Li metal among SSEs;25 (2) are environmentally 3

Page 4

stable with much processing flexibility; and (3) have high ionic conductivity, close to 1 mS/cm at room temperature.22,23,26-28 Since their discovery more than ten years ago, fundamental research has led to both increased understanding of the underlying mechanisms and improved Li-ion conductivity.29-32 However, there is still little progress on the successful demonstration of high performance SSLiBs with garnet SSE. One of the major challenges is the large interfacial resistance between garnet electrolyte and electrode materials due to its rigid ceramic nature. Heating or even melting Li metal for its integration with garnet electrolytes has been reported.28 However, the resistance reduction at the interface was limited, likely due to the poor wetting properties of garnet SSE and molten Li metal which causes microscopic gaps at the interface. Doeff and colleagues33 confirmed that Li2CO3 naturally formed on the garnet surface as a source of high interfacial resistance for LLZO with Li metal. After removing surface impurities by polishing, they successfully achieved a much lower interfacial area specific resistance (ASR), ~109 Ω∙cm2. Nevertheless, the achieved interfacial impedance is still too high for SSLiBs, and their polishing approach is only applicable to flat garnet electrolytes, significantly limiting cell geometries and manufacturing scalability. In this work, we demonstrate that the introduction of an ultrathin Al 2O3 coating on garnet-like Li7La2.75Ca0.25Zr1.75Nb0.25O12 (LLCZN) via atomic layer deposition (ALD) dramatically improves the wetting and stability of garnet SSE, and leads to a ~300 fold decrease in interfacial to only 5 Ω∙cm2. Experimental and computation studies are employed to investigate the possible mechanism for the excellent effect of the ALD-Al2O3 coating on the garnet/Li interface improvement. Based

on this newly developed interface engineering method, a quasi-SSLiB is successfully designed and operated using Li metal anode, garnet electrolyte and a high-voltage cathode.

Characterization of garnet LLCZN solid electrolyte Garnet-structured Li7La2.75Ca0.25Zr1.75Nb0.25O12 (LLCZN) was synthesized, sintered and polished into thin solid electrolyte pellets. The common garnet composition is Li 7La3Zr2O12 (LLZO), and the simultaneous substitution of La3+ site with Ca2+ and Zr4+ site with Nb5+ brings merits of reduced sintering temperature, stabilized cubic garnet-phase and increased Li-ion conductivity.34 Figure 1a displays a yellowish LLCZN pellet polished to about 150 μm thick. The thin pellet is translucent and also strong and dense enough for operation. The powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern of pulverized LLCZN pellet shown in Figure 1b matches that of cubic garnet-phase Li5La3Nb2O12 (PDF 80-0457), indicating that the LLCZN is pure cubic phase which has a higher Li-ion conductivity compared to the tetragonal garnet-phase. The cross-section scanning electronic microscopy (SEM) image of the LLCZN pellet (Figure 1c) reveals a dense morphology. Using Archimedes method with ethanol, the density was determined to be 97% of the theoretical. The different morphology of top-view presented in Figure 1d is likely caused by impurities inevitably formed in air, such as Li2CO3.35 For later tests, all the LLCZN pellets are polished and stored in an argon-filled glovebox. Li-ion conductivity of the LLCZN was evaluated by electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) with gold electrodes at temperatures from 22-50oC. All the resulting Nyquist plots summarized in Figure 1e show semi-circles at medium frequencies associated with the total 5

resistance from bulk and grain boundary response, and a connected vertical low-frequency tail corresponding to the capacitive behavior of Li-ion-blocking gold electrodes. The LLCZN resistance decreases with increasing temperature, and the Arrhenius behavior for total Li-ion conductivity is plotted in Figure 1f. The observed activation energy of 0.35 eV and Li-ion conductivity of 2.2×10-4 S/cm at 22oC are in line with literature values for garnet electrolytes.

Stability of LLCZN contacting Li metal anode The stability of LLCZN with Li metal anode is important for high energy density batteries. The interfacial stability experiments were carried out in argon-filled glovebox. LLCZN powder was in contact with Li metal at room temperature (22-25oC) for at least half a year without any visible change (Figure S1 in Supporting Information). To evaluate the Li/garnet stability at a high temperature, LLCZN powder was thoroughly mixed with molten Li metal in a 1:1 weight ratio at 300oC for 12 hours. Li metal was then removed by washing with methanol several times, and the remaining solid was dried and evaluated using XRD. The featured XRD peaks of the cubic LLCZN are maintained, indicating LLCZN’s chemical stability towards Li metal (Figure S2 in Supporting Information), but slightly shifts to lower angles compared with the pristine LLCZN, which is in agreement with the literature.35 This shifting indicates an increased lattice parameter, implying the potential for garnet expansion with Li insertion upon heating at 300oC. The electrochemical stability window of LLCZN was also evaluated using cyclic voltammetry (CV). The results are shown in Figure S3 in Supporting Information, where an LLCZN pellet was sandwiched between platinum sheet as the working electrode, and Li foil as the reference and counter electrode with slow scan rates (1 mV/s and 0.05 mV/s) in a Swagelok cell. The representative redox peaks at about -0.19 V and 0.09 V suggest Li plating/stripping processes with a polarized voltage of ~0.1 V. No other sensible redox current is observed in the entire test voltage

Page 9

range, suggesting LLCZN’s electrochemical stability over a wide voltage window up to 6 V versus metallic Li. Conformal interface between Li metal and ALD-Al2O3 coated LLCZN We designed a two-step process to achieve a conformal interface on thin garnet pellet LLCZN. First, a 40 ALD cycle Al2O3 coating was applied to the garnet surface. Then a piece of Li metal foil was pressed on the garnet pellets using a hydraulic press at 50 psi, and then the stacked garnet/Li pellet was heated at 250oC for 1 hour under a small pressure (see the details in Supporting Information). A control sample was made using bare LLCZN pellets in the same way. It was reported that Li metal cannot wet garnet LLZO until heating for a long time (24-168 hours) at temperature 300-350oC,35 much higher than Li metal melting point 180.5oC. Our two-step process to integrate Li metal with garnet at a relative low temperature causes neither color centers nor cracks.35 Figure 2a presents a schematic of the interface between garnet and Li metal. Without surface modification, garnet has insufficient physical contact with Li metal. With the ALD coating, the ultrathin Al2O3 layer helps the molten Li metal to conformally coat the garnet surface with no interfacial void space. The SEM images in Figure 2b clearly demonstrate the enhancement of interfacial contact by applying ALD-Al2O3 ultrathin layer on garnet surface. Insets are digital photos to further demonstrate improvement in wetting of ALD-treated garnet. Li metal-LLCZN interface resistance To quantify the effect of ALD on the improvement of garnet/Li interface, symmetric Li/garnet/Li cells were prepared and evaluated by EIS to examine the interfacial resistance. The cells were assembled with otherwise identical procedures and used garnet from the same batch of sintered 9

Page 10

pellets in order to maximize consistency and isolate the effect of ALD. The LLZCN pellets used were 200 μm thick with electrode surface areas of 0.49 cm2. Samples were measured with EIS at 22°C to identify individual contributions to the impedance. As shown in Figure 2c, two distinct arcs were seen in each sample. The bulk area specific resistances (ASRs), determined from the high frequency x-intercepts, are 26 and 28 Ω∙cm2 for cells with and without ALD coating, respectively. The first arc represents the overlap of grain boundary and interface impedances, with capacitances of 3.1x10-9 F and 2.1x10-9 F for cells with and without ALD, respectively. The grain boundary/interface ASRs were found to be 150 Ω∙cm2 and 3500 Ω∙cm2 for cells with and without ALD coating, respectively. Since the garnet pellets used in the two cases share identical origin, the dramatically higher ASR in the non-ALD sample is probably due to poor interfacial contact (Figure 2b) and an overlap in the grain boundary and Li-metal/garnet interfacial impedance frequency dispersion. Interfacial ASR was determined for the interface resistance on either side of the symmetric cells by subtracting the electrolyte resistance from the total cell resistance, dividing by two, and then normalizing to the electrode surface area. The total (bulk and grain boundary) impedance for the LLCZN samples are ~108 Ω∙cm2, which is calculated from the sample thickness and total conductivity obtained by EIS measurements of the Au/garnet/Au blocking electrode symmetric cells (Figure 1f). The total impedance for the symmetric Li/garnet/Li cells with and without ALD coating are 176 and 3528 Ω∙cm2, respectively. Therefore, the ALD treatment decreased the interfacial ASR by EIS from 1710 Ω∙cm2 to 34 Ω∙cm2. The reduction can be possibly attributed to the following reasons: (1) conformal contact of Li metal on SSE increases the effective ionic transfer area; (2) the thermal lithiated Al2O3, formed by contact with Li metal upon 10

Page 11

heating, is Li-ion conductive and effectively transports ions between garnet and Li metal; 36 and (3) the ALD coating layer inhibits impurity, e.g. Li2CO3 formation on the garnet surface during processing. We further carried out DC Li plating and stripping experiments to evaluate the interfacial impedance and Li-ion transport capability across the garnet and Li metal interface. At a current density of 0.1 mA/cm2, the Li/LLCZN/Li cell with an ALD coating stabilized at ~13 mV (Figure 2d). On the other hand, the control cell without ALD coating displayed a noisy potential with large voltage polarization indicating uneven ion transport through the interface. Figure 2e shows 90 hours of cycling at a current density of 0.2 mA/cm2 in both directions for 30 minutes and a stable voltage response at 22 mV. The excellent cycling with small polarization confirms that both low interfacial impedance and a stable interface during Li cycling were obtained with the ALD oxide interfacial layer. The stable interface is possibly due to the high ductility of the lithiated Al 2O3, which agrees with literature on the mechanical properties of lithiated and sodiated Al 2O3.37-42 The ASR calculated from the stripping/plating test, based on Ohm’s law, is 110 Ω∙cm2 close to the total garnet ASR measured by EIS (~108 Ω∙cm2), which indicates the effective removal of the Li/garnet interfacial impedance. The obtained interfacial ASR is therefore only ~1 Ω∙cm2 (calculated by subtracting the overall garnet ASR of ~108 Ω∙cm2 and dividing by two) thus demonstrating that the ALD-Al2O3 coating essentially negated the primary challenge of large interfacial impedance for the practical application of garnet electrolytes with Li metal anodes. Characterization of ALD coating layer To understand the ALD-Al2O3 layer at garnet SSE surface, a series of characterizations were carried out. Figure 3 shows the results of transmission electron microscopy (TEM), electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS), and selected area electron diffraction (SAED) characterizations for the ALD-Al2O3 treated garnet pellets. The TEM sample was prepared by mechanical polishing and then ion milling with a focused ion beam (FIB) at liquid nitrogen temperature. To prevent the damage of the ALD-Al2O3 coating layer, 10 nm Ti and 2 μm carbon were deposited on the garnet surface before sampling (Illustrated in Figure S5 in Supporting Information). As shown in Figure 3c and e, the elemental mappings of Al and O overlap, especially at the garnet border (Figure 3f). When further comparing the relative position of elemental Al, Li, and Ti (Figure 3c, d, and g), Al is found to exist between the Ti layer and Li layer, i.e. garnet. These results confirm the ALDAl2O3 coating layer on garnet. It is also observed that Al diffused deeper into the garnet surface (Figure 3c-f), possibly due to some surface porosity in the garnet (not 100% dense), and some pores were found in the garnet during FIB sampling (Figure S6 in Supporting Information), thus

Page 15

enabling the Al precursor (gas state) to infiltrate into the garnet pores during ALD process. The EELS data confirms the existence of Al2O3 coating layer on the garnet surface. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed for Al2O3 detection before and after ALD treatment on garnet. As shown in Figure S7 in Supporting Information, the emergence of Al 2p peak after ALD treatment further confirms the Al2O3 on the garnet surface is produced by ALD. After location of Al2O3 coating layer by EELS mapping, the thickness of the Al2O3 layer was measured to average 5~6 nm in the typical TEM image (Figure 3a). This is almost identical with the estimation (5.2 nm) based on the Si wafer control (See the details in Supporting Information). Also, it is worth noting that between the garnet and the ALD-Al2O3 coating layer, there is a Li-AlO layer with a typical thickness of 2~3 nm as shown in Figure 3a. This Li-Al-O layer is a transition layer formed between the garnet surface and the Al2O3 layer. The transition Li-Al-O layer was confirmed by EELS results obtained from the Li-Al-O layers at different samples. The EELS data shown in Figure 3h suggests the presence of Zr, O, and La (from garnet) and no Li2CO3 due to the lack of carbon energy loss at ~ 284 eV. Meanwhile, the energy loss at 58 eV and 76 eV shown in Figure 3i can be assigned to Li K-edge and Al L-edge, respectively, with the Al coming from the ALD deposition. Neutron depth profiling (NDP) shows a surface Li-ion enrichment layer and adjacent Li depletion zone in garnet due to strong Li segregation in the solid electrolyte (Figure S8 in Supporting Information). So it is inferred that surface excess Li ions on bare garnet may react with the sensitive trimethyl alumina (TMA) ALD precursor and water at the early ALD nucleation stage, forming Li and Al oxide layer (denoted as Li-Al-O). With the rapid surface reaction during ALD, Li ions may 15

Page 16

diffuse out of the garnet surface forming Li-Al-O, followed by regular ALD Al2O3 deposition on top. Therefore, the ALD treatment on garnet produce a bi-layer structure: Li-Al-O close to the garnet surface, and Al2O3 on top. The layered structure can be observed in Figure 3a with what appears to be a sub-nanometer transition region between the two layers. SAED was applied to confirm the Li-Al-O layer composition. As shown in Figure 3j, few rings are present in the electron diffraction pattern, indicating high crystal symmetry of the Li-Al-O structure. Further analysis from the EELS and SAED data suggests that the composition is close to Li2Al4O7 (see the details in Figure S9 in Supporting Information); however, longer e-beam irradiation leads to damage of the Li2Al4O7 layer. This layer should be Li ion conductive. In Figure 3d and f, it is clearly observed that no Li diffuses into or goes through the pure Al2O3 layer. This result is consistent with the mechanism that Li ion from the garnet surface is consumed to form a Li2Al4O7 layer. In turn, the 40 cycles of ALD-Al2O3 can restrain Li ion segregation. Note that the samples characterized in Figure 3 were not heated after ALD treatment. Upon heating at 250oC for 60 min, elemental Li was clearly observed to diffuse into and even go through the pure Al2O3 coating layer (Figure S10 in Supporting Information), which is consistent with the observation in NDP characterization (Figure S11 in Supporting Information).

First principles computation of Li metal and garnet interface To study the interaction between Li metal and garnet after ultrathin ALD coating, first principles computation was employed. Firstly, the binding energy of Li metal on lithiated pure ALD-Al2O3 layer with a variety of Li stoichiometry LixAl2O3+x/2 (x=0.4 to 1.4, See Supporting Information for 17

Page 18

details) was calculated. The results prove a strong chemical binding between Li metal on LixAl2O3+x/2 (x=0.4 to 1.4) with high binding energies ranging from 6.0 to 11.4 eV/nm 2. In turn, this strong interface binding improves the wetting of the Li metal on the Al2O3 coated solid electrolyte. The ultrathin alumina after lithiation is also a good Li-ion conductor and provides effective Li-ion transport paths37 and low interfacial resistance between Li metal and the garnet. Since garnet electrolytes are typically fabricated in air (which contains CO2), a thin Li2CO3 layer covering the garnet surface is expected (Figure S12-13 in Supporting Information). The interface binding energy between Li metal and the Li2CO3 layer is calculated as 1.6 eV/nm2, which is significantly lower than that between Li and the lithiated alumina (Figure 4a-b). The weak binding energy leads to low contact area, thus forming gaps and intervals. The existence of gaps and Li2CO3 in the garnet/Li interface would cause large interfacial resistance. This computation result agrees with notable decrease of interfacial impedance after ultrathin ALD-Al2O3 coating. Secondly, chemical interfacial stability was also calculated for garnet/Li metal from first principles. Based on the Li grand potential phase diagram of the LLZO system (Figure 4c), a small decomposition energy of -27 kJ/mol is calculated for the bare cubic LLZO in contact with Li. This decomposition corresponds to a composition of Li2O, Zr, and La2O3, indicating a potential tendency for the Li reduction of garnet. Compared to the undoped LLZO, the doped garnet LLCZN used in this study has similar phase stability against Li metal and phase equilibria with additional decomposed compositions, CaO and Nb. The relatively small thermodynamic driving force for the Li reduction of garnet may explain its relative stability against Li metal. 46 At high temperatures (> 250oC), the decomposition of garnet contacting Li metal may proceed and lead to a significant 18

Page 19

volume expansion up to ~50% on the basis of the first principles calculations. This is supported by the experimental evidence of low-angle shifting XRD peaks of LLCZN subjected heating with Li metal (Figure S2 in Supporting Information). This large volume expansion localized at the interface may crack the garnet electrolyte and induce a mechanical failure as shown in a previous experimental study.35 In addition, the decomposition of the garnet electrolyte forms an additional interphase layer similar to solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) but with poor Li-ion conductivity. The decomposed materials may contain Li2O/Li2O2, La2O3, Zr/ZrxO, CaO, Nb/NbOx, and also possible air contaminations such as Li2CO3,33 detrimental for good interfacial ionic conduction. In contrast, the Li grand canonical phase diagram (Figure 4d-e) indicates that garnet LLZO, similar for LLZCN, is stabilized with lithiated alumina in an equilibrium Li chemical potential (μLi) window of -0.06 eV to -1.23 eV. Therefore, the introduction of ALD-Al2O3 coating protects garnet from its decomposition via reacting with metallic Li, and maintains stable, high-conductive interface for garnet electrolyte with Li metal anode. The experimental results on galvanostatic cycling for long time without an increase of voltage agree with the computation on the stable interface between Li metal and ALD-coated garnet.

Working Cells with Li metal anode and ALD coated LLCZN The effective interface between Li metal anode and the garnet electrolyte developed in this work can potentially enable a range of high-energy density Li-ion batteries. As shown in figure 5a, full cells were prepared based on the Li/ALD-treated garnet by using cathode with liquid electrolyte. Since garnet electrolyte is stable up to 6 V, the high-voltage chemistry of Li2FeMn3O8 (LFMO) was selected for a cathode to demonstrate a high-voltage cell. The cathode, comprising LFMO, carbon black (CB), and polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF, binder), was made by conventional slurry coating on Al foil. A small amount of high-voltage liquid organic electrolyte was added in between cathode and garnet SSE to reduce the interfacial resistance. The high-voltage organic electrolyte, prepared by dissolving 1.0 M LiPF6 in highly fluorinated solvents, which has a high voltage stability over 4.8 V.47 Figure 5b shows the photographs of working cells, which can power a light emitting diode (LED). The battery was assembled in argon-filled glovebox and the working cells were packaged into 2032 coin cell cases sealed by epoxy resin. A schematic of the cell assembly is shown in Figure S14 in Supporting Information. Figure 5c shows the voltage profile of the first cycle at 0.1 C (1 C=150 mA/g). Clearly, two groups of well-defined plateaus are observed at 4.0 V and 4.9 V for discharge and 4.1 V and 5.0 V for charge, validating the working full cell, and the Li metal anode with a small over-potential (0.1 V). The cell delivered a capacity of 103 mAh/g (70% theoretical specific capacity of LFMO) with coulombic efficiency of 83% in the first cycle. 21

In summary, we report an innovative strategy to solve the interfacial issue between Li metal anode and garnet-type LLCZN solid electrolyte toward all solid state Li batteries. Ultrathin ALD coating of Al2O3 effectively decreased the interfacial ASR from 1696 Ω∙cm2 of the pristine Li/garnet to 1 Ω∙cm2 of the stabilized Li/ALD coated garnet. A working full cell was demonstrated using highvoltage cathode LFMO, garnet LLCZN, and Li metal anode. The possible mechanism for the interface improvement is proposed based on experimental and computational evidence: (1) the ALD-Al2O3 coating on garnet enables conformal interface of garnet/Li; (2) the higher binding energy of Li with lithiated alumina further enhances the conformal interface; (3) ultrathin lithiated alumina provides high Li-ion transport paths through the interface; and (4) the Al2O3 coating could possibly prevent garnet decomposition in contact with Li metal, and maintains garnet/Li interface stability and excellent electrical properties. This work has addressed the primary challenge of high interfacial resistance in garnet SSEs with Li metal anodes, and is thus a major breakthrough towards the development of high energy density and safe all-solid-state batteries. Based on the Li/ALD-treated garnet SSE, we used high-voltage LFMO as cathode to successfully demonstrate full cells, paving the way to achieve all-solid-state lithium batteries in the future.

Methods Materials synthesis. The LLCZN garnet electrolyte was synthesized via a sol-gel method. The starting materials were La(NO3)3 (99.9%, Alfa Aesar), ZrO(NO3)2 (99.9%, Alfa Asear), LiNO3(99%, Alfa Aesar), NbCl5 (99.99%, Alfa Aesar) and Ca(NO3)2 (99.9%, Sigma Aldrich). Stoichiometric amounts of chemicals were dissolved in de-ionized water and 10% excess LiNO3 was added to compensate for lithium volatilization during high temperature synthesis. Citric acid and ethylene glycol in the mole ratio of 1:1 were consequently added into the solution. The solution was slowly evaporated on hotplate to produce the precursor gel with stirring, which was then heated to 400℃ for 10 hours to burn out the organics. After this, the obtained powder was ball milled and pressed into pellets for calcination at 800oC for 10 hours. And then, the synthesized powders were then uniaxially pressed into pellets, which were sintered at 1050℃ for 12 hours in alumina boat covered with the same powder. Characterization. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was performed with a Hitachi SU-70 analytical scanning electron microscope. The phase analysis was performed with powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) on D8 Advanced with LynxEye and SolX (Bruker AXS, WI, USA) using a Cu Kα radiation source operated at 40 kV and 40 mA. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was performed on the JEOL 2100f field emission transmission electron microscope equipped with an electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS) system (TRIDIEM). To prepare the TEM sample, TESCAN GAIA focus ion beam (FIB) and Fischione 1010 ion milling were used to cut out garnet samples. Before cutting, 10 nm Ti via e-beam evaporation was deposited onto samples to protect the ALD layer from being damaged during the cutting process. XPS was applied to study the 24

Page 25

surface chemistry of garnet surface with and without ALD Al2O3 coating. Data collected with a monochromatic Al X-ray source in a Kratos Ultra DLD tool. Survey spectra were collected using a 12 kV monochromatic Al Kα X-ray source in hybrid lens mode with a step size of 1 eV and pass energy of 160 eV. High-resolution spectra were collected using a 12 kV monochromatic Al Kα Xray source in hybrid lens mode with a step size of 0.1 eV and pass energy of 20 eV. All peak locations in this data are reference to the hydrocarbon peak in the C1s spectra, which is calibrated to be at 284.8 eV. High resolution data was collected with a pass energy of 20, and peak fits performed using CasaXPS software. The near surface Li depth profiles of garnet from various preparation histories were examined using neutron depth profiling (NDP). NDP spectra were acquired at the NG1 Cold Neutron Depth Profiling station at the NIST Center for Neutron Research (NCNR). The entire specimen was irradiated at a constant fluence rate of cold neutrons, with wavelengths in the range of 0.4 nm-2 nm. The Li composition was calculated using the stopping power of the stoichiometric composition of metal oxide and a standard specimen with known Li abundance. Atomic layer deposition for LLCZN electrolyte pellets. The atomic layer deposition was performed with Beneq TFS 500 for Al2O3 deposition. High-purity nitrogen at 150oC was used as carrier gas for the whole process. Typically, for 5-6 nm Al2O3 coating, 40 ALD cycles were performed. Each cycle included alternating flows of trimethylaluminum (TMA, 4 sec, Al precursor) and water (4 sec, oxidant) separated by flows of pure nitrogen gas (4 and 10 sec, respectively, carrier and cleaning gas). For estimation, we carried out the control test for the ALD system on clean Si wafer, and got the control thickness is average 0.1297 nm/cycle after 500 and 25

Page 26

1000 ALD cycles respectively. So 40 cycles is estimated as 5.2 nm (40 × 0.1297 nm ≈ 5.2 nm), agreeable with the measurement from TEM image (5~6 nm in Figure 3a). Assembly of Li metal coated LLCZN. Thin Li foil disk (0.8 cm in diameter and 0.2 mm thick) was put on ALD-Al2O3 treated LLCZN pellets in glovebox filled with ultrahigh pure Ar, then the stacked Li/LLCZN pellet was heated at 250oC for 60 min with pressure of 0.26 psi by 6 pieces of stainless steel disks (1.5 g each). The small pressure was used to help the initial contact of melted lithium on the garnet surface. For control, freshly polished LLCZN pellets were assembled with lithium metal via the same procedure. After cooling down to room temperature, the thin lithium disk was stuck on the pellets. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was performed for the samples’ cross-section obtained by breaking with tweezers. Synthesis of Li2FeMn3O8 (LFMO). LFMO was synthesized according to the literature48 with modification. Briefly, glycine-nitrate mixture solution was firstly prepared by dissolving lithium nitrate, iron nitrate, manganese nitrate (2:1:3, mole ratio) and glycine in de-ionized water. The mole ratio of nitrate to glycine is 2:1. Then, the LFMO powder was obtained by combustion reaction of the glycine-nitrate solution at 300oC, followed with a 2-hour annealing at 700oC. First principles calculations. First principles calculations were performed using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) within the projector augmented-wave approach with the Perdew−Burke−Ernzerhof generalized-gradient approximation (GGA) to density functional theory (DFT). The materials entries for the grand potential phase diagram were obtained from the Materials Project database.49 The interface models comprising a slab of amorphous Li metal and a slab of the Li2CO3 or the lithiated alumina were equilibrated using ab initio molecular dynamics 26

Page 27

simulations at 513K for 30 ps. The binding energy was calculated as the energy of the interfaces minus the energy of the separated surface slabs. Battery assembly and electrochemical tests. To measure the ionic conductivity of the garnet solid-state electrolyte, Au paste was coated on both sides of the ceramic disk and acted as a blocking electrode. The gold electrodes were sintered at 700oC to form good contact with the ceramic pellet. The EIS was performed over a frequency range of 1 MHz to 100 mHz with a 50 mV perturbation amplitude. Owing to the sensitivity of Li metal to oxygen and moisture, all the cells including symmetric cells and full cells were assembled and tested in ultrahigh pure argon (99.999%) filled glovebox with moisture and oxygen levels below 0.1 and 0.01 ppm, respectively. In a typical LLCZN/Li assembly, Li foil was pressed to about 0.2 mm, and then punched into thin disks with a diameter 0.79 cm. The thin Li foil disk was spread and gently pressed on ALD-Al2O3 coated LLCZN pellets at one or both sides with a pair of plastic tweezes. 6 pieces of stainless steel (9 g in total) was put on the Li foil. Then the stacked Li/ALD-Al2O3 coated LLCZN/Li cell was put in an oven in the glovebox, which was then heated at 250oC for 1 hour, followed by natural cooling down to room temperature. The LLCZN/Li was then ready to use. The stainless steel was used as the current collector. The cathode disks were made outside the glovebox. The assynthesized LFMO was thoroughly mixed with carbon black and PVDF at mass ratio of 80:10:10 in NMP (solvent, N-Methyl-2-pyrrolidone). The mixture was then coated on a clean and flat Al foil. After drying in air, the Al foil with cathode slurry coated was put in vacuum oven and further dried overnight at 110oC. It was then punched into cathode disks (diameter of 0.79 cm). The mass measurement was done with a microbalance (Sartorius). The full cells were assembled by putting 27

Page 28

the cathode disk on the bare side of Li/ALD-Al2O3-LLCZN pellet with a tiny amount of highvoltage organic liquid electrolyte added between cathode and LLCZN pellet. The cells were packaged in 2032 coin cell cases and the edge of the cells sealed by epoxy resin. They were kept in a glovebox during all tests. The tests were carried out with a BioLogic battery tester at room temperature via a feedthrough in the glovebox. The cut voltages were 3.5 V and 5.3 V with a current density of 0.1 C (1C=150 mA/g).
