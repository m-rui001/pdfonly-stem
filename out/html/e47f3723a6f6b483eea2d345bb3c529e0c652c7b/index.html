Alex Greaneyb*, and Xiulei Jia* a Department of Chemistry, Oregon State University, Corvallis, OR, 97331, United States b Materials Science and Engineering, University of California, Riverside, 900 University Ave. Riverside, CA, 92521, United States c Chemical and Engineering Materials Division, Oak Ridge National Laboratory, Oak Ridge, TN, 37831, United States d Hewlett-Packard Co., 1000 NE Circle Blvd., Corvallis, OR 97330, United States Abstract: Hard carbon is the candidate anode material for the commercialization of Na-ion batteries — the batteries that by virtue of being constructed from inexpensive and abundant components open the door for massive scale up of battery-based storage of electrical energy. Holding back the development of these batteries is that a complete understanding of the mechanism of Na-ion storage in hard carbon has remained elusive. Although as an amorphous carbon, hard carbon possesses a subtle and complex structure composed of domains of layered rumpled sheets that have local order resembling graphene within each layer but complete disorder along the c-axis between layers. This article presents two key discoveries: first that characteristics of hard carbon’s structure can be modified systematically by heteroatom doping, and second, that these changes greatly affect Na-ion storage properties, which reveal the mechanisms for Na storage in hard carbon. Specifically, P, S and B doping was used to engineer the density of local defects in graphenic layers, and to modify the spacing between the layers. Opening the interlayer spacing through P or S doping extends the low-voltage capacity plateau, while increasing the defect concentration with P or B doping leads to high first sodiation capacity. We further observe that the highly defective B-doped hard carbon suffers a tremendous irreversible capacity in the first desodiation cycle. Our combined first principles calculations and experimental studies revealed a new trapping mechanism, showing that the high binding energies between B-doping induced defects and Na-ions are responsible for the irreversible capacity. The understanding generated in this work provides a totally new set of guiding principles for materials engineers working to optimize hard carbon for Na-ion battery applications. Na-ion batteries (NIBs) represent one of the most promising solutions for grid-scale storage of electrical energy produced by intermittent renewable energy sources, such as wind and solar power.

Their potential for massive scale-up is possible because they are

electrochemically similar to Li-ion batteries (LIBs), but unlike LIBs can be composed entirely of inexpensive and earth abundant elements. Cathode materials, including layered oxides and polyanion compounds have exhibited very promising performance for NIBs 1-5. Unfortunately the principal anode materials for LIBs, graphite, only stores sodium up to a stoichiometry of NaC64, one tenth of its capacity for lithium storage unless graphite is expanded or solvent co-intercalation occurs6, 7, and so alternative anode materials are being sought. These candidate materials, in addition to having high sodium capacity, must also meet the requirements for scale-up by being inexpensive to produce in large quantities with minimal environmental impact.

Currently the most promising candidate is an allotrope of carbon called hard carbon. This is an amorphous form of turbostratic carbon comprising nanoscale domains of stacked rumpled graphenic sheets8. Within each plane, the sheets have graphene-like order, but the sheets lack long-range ordering and are therefore disordered along the c-axis. The titular adjective hard refers to the material’s reluctance to transform to graphite during annealing, and as such it is also known as non-graphitizable carbon. The ability of hard carbon to accommodate a large quantity of Na-ions electrochemically was first demonstrated by Stevens and Dahn in 20009. Since then a worldwide research effort has enhanced this storage capacity, and advanced numerous processes for synthesizing hard carbon from abundant feed stocks, such as 2

Page 3

sucrose10, peat moss11, cotton12, wood13 and even banana skins14. Most of this advancement has been Edisonian in nature, because efforts to attribute features of the sodiation profile to hard carbon’s structure have not fully appreciated the subtlety of its structure. That hard carbon does not readily transform to graphite indicates that, unlike other amorphous (nongraphitic) carbons, its graphenic sheets possess a topology that cannot be unfolded and flattened — topology that is also beneficial for Na-storage.

Early work proposed a Na-ion storage mechanism that followed that for Li, where storage during the sloping region in the sodiation profile is attributed to intercalation of Na-ions into turbostratic nanodomains, and the capacity from the low-potential plateau is attributed to nano-plating inside the enclosed micropores between the domains. Our recent studies lead us to question this model. We annealed hard carbon at high temperatures, with the aim that the resulting increased size of turbostratic nanodomains would increase the capacity under the sloping region of the discharge profile15. Instead we found the reverse, that the capacity of the sloping region was reduced. This contradiction suggests an alternative correlation where the removal of defects by annealing results in the decrease of sloping capacity, and thus we tentatively attribute the sloping capacity to favorable binding of Na at local defects rather than indiscriminate Na-ion intercalation. Other experiments have also led us to question which Na storage sites are filled during the plateau region of the discharge profile. X-ray diffraction measurements of the (002) d-spacing (the average inter-sheet spacing) show marked dilation between sheets during the low-potential plateau sodiation, which indicates that the plateau capacity might originate from inter-sheet intercalation of Na-ions instead of nanoplating in micropores11, 16. One can test this hypothesis by determining whether a higher capacity is obtained by a hard carbon with a larger initial (002) d-spacing between the graphene sheets inside turbostratic nanodomains. It is reasonable to hypothesize that a larger

Page 4

initial sheet spacing incurs a lower strain energy penalty for Na-ion intercalation. Recently, Huang et al. and Jiang et al. investigated heteroatom doping into carbon structures, where these authors report that doping facilitates a larger d-spacing, which is responsible for the higher capacity17, 18. It is important to note that these reported carbons are not conventional hard carbon known by the field as they were pyrolyzed at relatively low temperatures, and therefore do not exhibit a low-potential plateau behavior. So, unfortunately, these doped structures cannot provide mechanistic insights on the Na-ion storage in conventional hard carbon. The lack of mechanistic understanding of Na-ion storage in hard carbon has, indeed, slowed the progress toward tailor-designed hard carbon structures for high capacity values, and the reported reversible desodiation capacity for typical hard carbon has rarely been above 300 mAh/g.

A straightforward empirical approach to reveal storage mechanisms is to enhance the postulated desirable local structures of hard carbon and monitor the corresponding capacity variation. The most promising method for engineering the structure of hard carbon is through heteroatom doping, and to date, many dopants have been investigated for various hard carbon materials17, 19-26. However, thus far there has been no systematic knowledge of how doping affects the local structure of hard carbon, and the carbon materials reported in the literature vary hugely in their specific surface areas.

This makes it difficult to compare carbon

structures because in materials with large surface area the formation of solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) can mask the reversible capacity from the internal local structures27.

In this article we elucidate the structure-capacity correlation for hard carbon with a systematic set of experiments that address the challenges above using controlled syntheses combined with detailed structural and electrochemical characterization.

presented in two parts. The first demonstrates that we have used boron, phosphorous, and sulfur doping to tune two different structural characteristics of turbostratic nanodomains: the interlayer spacing, and the defect concentration. The second part of the article presents detailed electrochemical characterization of these materials and computational analysis of probable Na storage sites.

We have prepared “conventional” hard carbons by pyrolysis of sucrose at 1100 ˚C, and all hard carbon samples in this study are of consistently low specific surface areas, i.e., <10 m 2/g enabled by our well-established method10, 28. Recently, we reported that P-doped hard carbon exhibits a record-high reversible desodiation capacity of 359 mAh/g at 20 mA/g; though hypothesis of favorable structural sites is raised, the capacity-structure correlation is not solidified10. B-doping was used to increase the number of local defects. As boron can only form three bonds in the absence of strong Lewis bases, thus B-doping more likely takes place within the graphenic planes. Furthermore, computational study have reported that borondoped graphene can improve the performance of graphene as anode in NIBs29. We selected doping phosphorus and sulfur to increase the interlayer spacing between graphene sheets by steric occupancy. We use phosphoric acid and sulfuric acid as precursors, because the respective oxo-acids are difficult to fully reduced to valence zero, thus often forming more than three bonds around P or S due to their hypervalance.

Synthesis of these materials was followed by detailed structural and chemical characterization using x-ray diffraction (XRD), Raman spectroscopy, neutron total scattering, and transition electron microscopy (TEM) equipped with energy-dispersive x-ray mapping (EDX). Together these provided a picture of the doped hard carbons’ mesoscale structure, chemical structure, and short- and intermediate-range atomic order. It was revealed that both

Page 6

P- and B-doping produce a more defect laden hard carbon with defects breaking up their graphene-like intermediate-range atomic order, and significantly enlarge Na storage capacity in the first sodiation sloping region of the potential profiles. Both S- and P-doping increase the interlayer spacing between graphenic sheets and have increased Na storage capacity in low-potential plateau region. Our results provide solid evidence that supports our hypothesis of structure-capacity correlation, where the low-potential plateau is attributed to the intercalation of Na-ions in turbostratic nanodomains with less defects and the sloping region corresponds to the binding between defective sites of hard carbon and Na-ions. We also find that the large first sodiaiton capacity of B-doped hard carbon is highly irreversible in the following desodiation process. We conducted combined experimental and first principles studies, which reveal that it is the high binding energy between B-doping induced defects and Na-ions that cause the irreversibility, thus raising a new trapping mechanism to explain the low first cycle coulombic efficiency of B-doped hard carbon.

Results and discussion Structure characterization of synthesized materials. All the dopant precursors are soluble in the sucrose aqueous solution, this helps achieve the molecular-level dispersion of dopants in the final products, as demonstrated in our previous work10. We aim at 5 wt.% of doping by assuming the dopants are not volatile and the carbonization yield of sucrose is 25 wt%. We determined the doping levels by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) to be 3.0 wt.%, 3.0 wt.% and 0.1 wt.% for P-doped (P-HC), B-doped (B-HC) and S-doped (S-HC) hard carbon, respectively. Note that the S-doping level is significantly lower than the target amount of doping because sulfuric acid is most likely reduced into SO2 gas during pyrolysis. Near atomic level distribution of dopant was achieved, as proved in P-HC in our previous work10. The densities for all carbons were measured based on Archimedes’

Page 7

principle, which are 1.60, 1.42, 1.50, and 1.49 g/cm3 for non-doped hard carbon (HC), P-HC, B-HC, and S-HC, respectively. The much lowered densities of all these carbons compare to graphite (2.11 g/cm3) indicate larger d-spacing between graphenic layers as well as enclosed voids in the structures30. All as-obtained HC, P-HC, B-HC and S-HC exhibit very low Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) surface area of 8.5 m2/g, 7.3 m2/g, 8.0 m2/g and 5.2 m2/g, respectively. All these carbons consist of bulk particles, tens of microns large, as shown by the scanning electron microscope (SEM) images (Supplementary Fig. 1). The consistently low surface area and similar morphology of these carbons will facilitate a faithful comparison between their local structures and corresponding electrochemical performance.

We first investigated the chemical states of the dopants by x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (Supplementary Fig. 2). None of dopants became fully reduced to its elemental valance from their oxo-acids. Phosphorus in P-HC occurs in P(V), P(II), and P-C bonds with its 2p signal deconvoluted into components at 134.5, 133.1 and 131.0 eV, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 2a)31, 32. As for S-HC, the sulfur 2p signal is deconvoluted into peaks at 168.3, 164.9 and 163.9 eV, attributed to C-SOx, S 2p1/2 and S 2p3/2 of C-S-C, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 2b)17, 33. For B-HC, boron may occur in boron oxide, BCO2, BC2O and B3C, as suggested by deconvoluted 1s peaks at 193.3, 192.5, 191.6 and 190.8 eV, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 2c)34.

The x-ray diffraction (XRD) results reveal the amorphous structure of carbons and confirm our hypothesis that phosphorus and sulfur doping increase the interlayer d-spacing and the interlayer distance of B-HC stays nearly the same. The positions of (002) peaks determined by PDXL software are at 23.6°, 22.5°, 23.2°, and 23.5°, indicating d-spacing values of 3.77 Å, 3.95 Å, 3.83 Å and 3.78 Å for HC, P-HC, S-HC, and B-HC, respectively (Fig. 1). In PDF plots, the peak amplitude of S-HC and HC is similar. Both B-HC and P-HC exhibit lower peak amplitude, where P-HC shows the lowest amplitude.

The local defect

concentration follows the sequence of P-HC > B-HC > HC ≈ S-HC. Both P and B exhibit a mass ratio of 3.0%, neither of P and B species are completely reduced to valence zero. Instead, they occur with the presence of POx and BOx in the structure, as shown in XPS

Page 10

results (Supplementary Fig. 2). Considering the atomic mass of phosphorus (30.97) and boron (10.81), the atomic doping of boron is nearly three times of phosphorus. However, the defects induced by P-doping overpass that of B-doping, which may have to do with the disparity of the bonding configurations between doped BOx and POx with nearby carbon atoms. With the absence of strong Lewis acids, BOx with boron only forming three bonds should constitute a planar structure, isostructural to sp2 carbon atoms. However, POx will most likely not be coplanar with graphenic layers, thus inducing more curvatures. This agrees with the Raman results where B-HC shows a larger La value than P-HC. Furthermore, during pyrolysis, both boric acid and phosphoric acid are reduced to a certain degree by carbothermal reactions; carbon gasification may take place near the dopant, creating vacancies.

To test our hypothesized Na-ion storage mechanism, we have obtained the desirable structural properties of hard carbon by heteroatom doping. First, through P- and S-doping, we have enlarged the inter-layer spacing inside the turbostratic nanodomains, which should enhance the plateau capacity. Second, P-doping and B-doping have significantly increased the structural defects inside hard carbon, where the sloping capacity should be enlarged. Importantly, these doped carbons meet the two prerequisites: (1) the specific surface area of all carbons are below 10 m2/g, where the structure-property correlation would not be masked by the SEI formation; (2) by pyrolysis treatment at 1100 ˚C, these carbon samples belong to the category of hard carbon.

Electrochemical behaviors of all carbons. Our electrochemical results via galvanostatic sodiation/desodiation support the structure-capacity mechanism that Na-binding with carbon defects results in the sodiation slope capacity, and Na-intercalation into turbostratic

Page 11

nanodomains causes the plateau capacity. The conclusion is drawn on the basis of the data that more defective P- and B-doped HC provide higher slope capacity in the first sodiation, and the more dilated P- and S-doped HC exhibit higher capacity on the plateau (Fig. 5a). Table 1 summarizes the detailed slope/plateau capacity values, where we use the inflection point of the potential profiles to define the sloping and plateau regions.

During the first sodiation, B-HC and P-HC exhibit slope capacity values of 304 mAh/g, and 245 mAh/g, respectively, compared to 178 mAh/g of the un-doped HC. Such high slope capacity values confirm the causality between Na-defect binding and the slope behavior during the first sodiation. The desodiation of P-HC and B-HC generates reversible slope capacity of 157 and 70 mAh/g vs 134 mAh/g of HC. Both P-HC and B-HC have less coulombic efficiency for the slope region: 64% and 23% compared to 75% of HC. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) results also reveal the degrees of irreversible sodiation in electrodes (Supplementary Fig. 3), where the CV curves of B-HC look completely different from the other doped and un-doped hard carbon. The lower first-cycle coulombic efficiency of P-HC has been discussed in the reference 10, where the irreversible reduction of POx is responsible. It is stunning that the sodiation of B-HC is so irreversible, as shown by both the galvanostatic sodiation/desodiation and CV results.

In the first-cycle CV curves, its lack of sharp

reversible desodiation peak very much resembles those high-surface area carbon anodes in NIBs27. Here, we should emphasize that the very low first-cycle coulombic efficiency of BHC should have little to do with SEI formation on the electrode surface because B-HC’s specific surface area of 8.0 m2/g is simply too little to be impactful. We hypothesize that Naions are trapped by doped B and/or B-doping-induced carbon defects due to very strong binding energies. We will further discuss this critical point with computational studies later on.

When we look at the plateau capacity, both P-HC and S-HC are superior to up-doped HC by exhibiting 240 and 213 mAh/g compared to 175 mAh/g of HC, and the plateau capacity for all carbon samples is relatively reversible. The capacity values confirm that a larger dspacing due to S- and P-doping does increase the plateau capacity due to the energetically more favorable Na-ion insertion in between more dilated graphene sheets.

An issue of heteroatom doping is its impact on the electronic conductivity and the rate capability of the doped hard carbon anodes. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 4, all carbons exhibit similar rate performance, where their capacities at various rates are proportional to the capacity obtained at a low rate. Furthermore, all doped carbons show quite stable cycling performance at 20 mA/g, where P-HC and S-HC exhibit a high capacity of 327 and 323 mAh/g even after 200 cycles.

Computational studies of the Na binding.

To further understand the influence of

heteroatom doping on sodium storage processes in hard carbon, we used first principles density functional theory (DFT) calculations to determine the electronic structure of sodium binding at dopants sites. Being amorphous and having a rumpled sheet topology the structure of every individual Na storage site will be different. Rather than attempt to model this we instead examine the electronic structure of Na binding at idealized dopants in a single graphene sheet. Sodium binding on bare graphene is unfavorable with respect to sodium metal, and so this may not seem like a meaningful calculation, but here we focus on a comparative study between binding on pristine vs defected graphene with the expectation that the same trends in electronic structure and energetics will carry over to the more complex misaligned, multilayered, turbostratic structures of hard carbon. From the charge distribution plots (Fig. 6), that the boron atom draws a significant amount of negative charge, most likely due to the electron deficiency of a boron atom. This allows it to form a strong ionic interaction with the Na atom as opposed to the pristine graphene sheet. The P-doped graphene also shows a significant amount of negative charge near the P-atom, but this is expected, as it contains an extra electron. Furthermore, the size of the P-atom causes the graphene sheet to buckle, which enhances the interactions. Correlating the simulation data to the first-cycle sodiation profiles of the B-HC and P-HC, we see a lot of similarities: the B-HC profile has a much more pronounced slope, and on average has a much higher voltage than the P-HC profile. Considering that Na atoms bind more strongly, and thus at higher voltages, to Boron heteroatom such profiles match up well with the DFT results, and help to explain why the B-HC has a much longer slope than the P-HC.

Though helpful, these initial results are to be taken with caution, as they make the assumption that the heteroatoms are embedded in the graphene sheets with no corresponding oxygen/hydrogen atoms. While exploring all the possible combinations of the heteroatoms with oxygen cluster would be ideal, such a comprehensive effort is beyond the scope of this paper. However, there is another structural feature of the carbon material whose effect on Na-ion storage we can also evaluate: the presence of defects. From the PDF results, we know that P-HC and B-HC have a higher defect concentration than HC. When simulating a monovacancy, divacancy and a large-vacancy defect, where we define them all as in-plane defects, we see that the binding energy is very favorable, especially in the base of the large-

Page 15

vacancy defect (Supplementary Table. 2). From the charge density plots (Fig. 7), we see that negative charges gather at defects sp2 sites, thereby acting as favorable binding locations for Na atoms. This is especially true in the case of the monovacancy defect and the largevacancy defect, which show strong regions of charge at sp bonded carbons, which are highly favorable for binding with Na atoms.

Since the B-HC provides the highest first sodiation sloping capacity, which sodiates at higher average potentials, it is either the B-doped sites or B-doping induced vacancies that play a critical role in the first sodiation process. Additionally, the strong binding at defect sites can also be used to explain the low reversible capacity obtained with B-HC. When desodiating the B-HC anode, the reversible capacity is much smaller than the initial sodiation capacity. Given that the overall surface area of B-HC is similar to the other doped carbons, it is difficult to make a case for a greater onset of SEI formation, as SEI formation on the first cycle is closely correlated to surface area/porosity. However, when the half-cell battery is held at a constant voltage of 3.0 V for 30 h, an additional 40 mAh/g of capacity is recovered (Fig. 5b). This goes to suggest that the B-HC anode can reversibly store Na atoms, but it does so very strongly. Thus, simply desodiating the anode up to a potential of 2.0 V is not enough to fully remove all of the Na atoms. This behavior is consistent with the high binding energies arising from the defected carbon structure, along with the increased binding energy stemming from the in-plane boron.

Conclusions In conclusion, via well-controlled syntheses, comprehensive structural characterizations and first principles DFT studies, we, for the first time, provide in-depth insights on the correlation between the local structure of hard carbon and the corresponding capacity performance. We

Page 16

confirm the linkage between defects and sloping region of potential profiles, where too strong binding energy of defects causes large irreversible capacity in the first cycle. We also confirm the favorable impacts of larger interlayer spacing between graphenic sheet on extending the sodium storage capacity during the low voltage plateau. Overall, we elucidate the Na-ion storage mechanism for hard carbon as NIB anode, and we identify that the ideal hard carbon anode should be a low surface area ensemble of dilated turbostratic nanodomains that contains a high density of point and local structural defects with mild binding energies. We believe that these fundamental insights into Na-ion storage mechanisms provide the community guiding principles to steer the development and optimization of hard carbon anodes and as such will hasten commercialization of high capacity Na-ion batteries.

Experimental Section Materials Synthesis: Modified Hummer’s method was applied to prepare aqueous GO suspension. In a typical experiment, 2 g sucrose was added to GO suspension (1.8 mg/mL) with a mass ratio of 80:1. Then, an oxo-acid precursor, H3PO4, H2SO4 or H3BO3 was added to the suspension with a designed mass ratio of the corresponding dopant of 5% for P-HC, SHC and B-HC, respectively. Un-doped hard carbon suspension was prepared with same method without adding a dopant acid precursor. The obtained suspension was dried at 80 ˚C for 48 h, dehydrated at 180 ˚C for 24 h, and then pyrolyzed at 1100 ˚C for 5 h under Ar flow.

Materials Characterization: The doping levels of all doped carbon were measured by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES). The density of all carbons were measured by density measurement kit using cyclohexane as the solvent. N2 sorption measurements were performed on a Micromeritics TriStar II 3020 analyzer for Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) surface area.

The electrodes were composed of active materials, polyvinylidene fluoride (PVdF) and carbon black with a mass ratio of 80:10:10. All these components were mixed together in a mortar and grounded in N-Methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP), and the obtained slurry is coated onto Al foil by doctor blade and dried at 100 ˚C for 12 h under vacuum. The active mass loading for all electrodes are between 1.5 to 2 mg/cm2. The counter/reference electrode was sodium metal and the electrolyte was 1.0 mol/L NaPF6 solution in ethylene carbonate (EC)/diethyl carbonate (DEC) (1:1 in volume).
