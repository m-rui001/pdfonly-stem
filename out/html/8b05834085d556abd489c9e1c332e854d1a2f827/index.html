Three of the fundamental catalytic limitations that have plagued the electrochemical production of hydrogen for decades still remain: low efficiency, short lifetime of catalysts and a lack of low-cost materials. Here, we address these three challenges by establishing and exploring an intimate functional link between the reactivity and stability of crystalline (CoS 2 and MoS 2 ) and amorphous (CoS x and MoS x ) hydrogen evolution catalysts. We propose that Co2+ and Mo4+ centers promote the initial discharge of water (alkaline solutions) or hydronium ions (acid solutions). We establish that although CoS x materials are more active than MoS x they are also less stable, suggesting that the active sites are defects formed after dissolution of Co and Mo cations. By combining the higher activity of CoS x building blocks with the higher stability of MoS x units into a compact and robust CoMoS x structure, we are able to design a low-cost alternative to noble metal catalysts for efficient electrocatalytic production of hydrogen in both alkaline and acidic environments.

Improvements in the fundamental understanding of electrochemical interfaces 1 have started to revolutionize the development of hydrogen-based technologies 2–6 as key alternatives to coal, oil and natural gas 7. Today, the major commercial processes for producing hydrogen are catalytic steam reforming, partial oxidation, and coal gasification - all of which significantly contribute to global CO 2 emissions. Hydrogen is also commercially produced in electrocatalytic processes either in chlor-alkali electrolyzers as a side product, or in water electrolyzers as the 1

Page 2

main product 8,9. Unfortunately, the electrochemical production of hydrogen is hampered by three fundamental limitations: (i) the thermal efficiency is considerably below the thermodynamic limits for the water splitting reaction (1.223 V); (ii) the lifetime of electrode materials is too short; and (iii) there is a lack of a cost-effective replacement for noble metals. Currently, various combinations of metals, metal alloys, metals modified with 3d transition metal hydroxides (3d-TM(OH) 2 ), 3d-TM phosphides 9–17 and transition metal sulfides (TMS x = MoS 2 , CoS 2 , FeS 2 , Mo 3 S 13 ) are used to catalyze the conversion of H 3 O+ (acid) and H 2 O (alkaline) to H 2 18–24. Traditionally, interest in TMS x materials has stemmed from environmentally and technologically important hydrodesulfurization (HDS) processes, where nanocrystalline TMS x (chalcogenide) catalysts are employed to remove sulfur from natural gas and from refined petroleum products 25–30. It is generally assumed that the active sites for the HDS processes on crystalline MoS 2 are the coordinately unsaturated TM centers 31,32. It is also well-established that Co added to MoS 2 increases the reactivity of the so called “Co-Mo-S x ” materials 25–27. Closely following the HDS processes on TMS x , it has also been suggested that the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) on crystalline MoS 2 in acid media correlates with the number of exposed edge sites that have a local stoichiometry and electronic structure that differs from the catalytically inert basal planes 31,32. However, the role of Mo and sulfur ligands is still unresolved, e.g., density functional theory (DFT) suggests that the active sites are either sulfur atoms 31 or the Mo metal centers 33. Regardless, there are still many experimental issues that need to be resolved in order to begin to understand the electrochemical conversion of H 2 O/H 3 O+ on TMS x , including: (i) identification of the role of TM cations (TMn+) in controlling the catalytic behavior; (ii) exploring relationships between the activity and stability of TMS x materials; and (iii) understanding the role of pH on the kinetics of the HER. Ultimately, this knowledge will allow the development of TMS x catalysts for economical production of hydrogen in acid and alkalinebased electrolyzers. Herein, we have used a rational design approach to synthesize highly active and stable CoMoS x that is demonstrated as a pH-universal and cost-effective catalyst for the HER. We arrived at this material by first studying the HER in alkaline solutions containing K+/Ba2+ cations (Cn+), and we found that irreversibly adsorbed sulfur (S ad δ-) on Au(111) and Pt(111) acts as a 2

Page 3

promoter, affecting the HER indirectly via the formation of a S ad δ---Cn+-H 2 O network in which hydrated cations can enhance the dissociation of water. We propose that the S ad δ---Cn+-H 2 O clusters mimic the catalytic behavior of the Sδ--TMn+ building blocks of CoS x , MoS x and CoMoS x chalcogels. We further suggest that the relationship between the activity and stability of TMS x materials is governed by a synergy between electronic effects (substrate-adsorbate binding energies) and morphological effects (number of defects), which are optimized for the CoMoS x catalyst. Role of pH in the HER on Au(111)-S ad We begin by exploring the HER on Au(111) and Au(111) modified by irreversibly adsorbed sulfur atoms (hereafter Auδ+-S ad δ-), with surface coverages (ϴ Sad ) ranging from 0.05 < ϴ Sad < 0.55 monolayers (for details see Methods and ref. 34). We chose to study these systems for two reasons: (i) to build an electrochemically-relevant model interface comprised of a sulfur-cation unit cell (e.g., S ad δ----K+-/Ba2+-H 2 O in alkaline solutions); and (ii) to rationalize the role of this unit cell in guiding the electrochemical transformation of H 2 O (pH = 13) or H 3 O+ (pH = 1) to H 2 . The HER in alkaline solutions is usually assumed to proceed by an initial discharge of water and the formation of hydrogen intermediates (H ad ) in the so-called Volmer step (H 2 O + e- + M M-H ad + OH-); followed by either the electrochemical Heyrovsky step (H 2 O + M-H ad + e-  M + H 2 + OH-) or the chemical Tafel recombination step (2H ad  H 2 ). The reaction pathway in acidic solutions is similar except that H ad is formed by discharge of the hydronium ion (H 3 O+ + M + e-  H ad + H 2 O). In agreement with a previous work 35–37, Figure 1a shows that the activity of the HER on Au(111) is an order of magnitude lower at pH = 13 than at pH = 1. Significantly, in 0.1M KOH the HER on Auδ+-S ad δ- (ϴ Sad ≈ 0.4) is 3-fold more active than on bare Au(111) (Figure 1a), signaling that S ad δ- could be the active site for the reaction. As illustrated in Figure 1b, at higher coverages by S ad (ϴ Sad > 0.45) a steep deactivation is observed, suggesting that S ad δ- is not a catalyst in its own right. In acid solutions, however, the HER on Au(111) is always more active than on the Au(111)-S ad δ- electrode, supporting previous observations that S ad is a poison (spectator) for electrocatalysis on metal surfaces in acidic electrolytes 38. The striking disparity in the pH-dependent role of S ad can be rationalized by considering missing relationships between a nature of electrode material and hydrated cations present in the double layer, as we shall discuss below. 3

In the following, we offer an explanation which is conceptually identical to the promoting role of hydrated cations (Cn+---(H 2 O) x ) in the electrochemical conversion of H 2 O to H 2 on metal surfaces modified by 3d-TM(OH) 2 9,10. Here we explore the effect of Ba2+---(H 2 O) x on activity of Au-/Pt-S ad surfaces in alkaline electrolytes relative to electrolytes containing K+, and observe that the HER on both Au(111)-S ad δ- (Figure 1a) and Pt(111)- S ad δ- (Supplementary Figure S1) is more active in the presence of Ba2+ than K+. This implies that the HER is controlled by the nature of cations in addition to the activity of metal sites. A plausible synergetic role of S ad and K+/Ba2+ is schematically depicted in Figure 1c. In short, while S ad δ- serves as an anchor for keeping beneficial Cn+---(H 2 O) x cations close to the gold/platinum electrode surface via van der Waals forces (for Pt see Supplementary Figure S1 and Supplementary Table S1), C+---(H 2 O) x cations serve to activate a near-surface water through additional noncovalent type of interactions 10,35,39. This activated water molecule can then serve as a reactant in the kinetically hindered Volmer step. As expected, the cation effect is much higher on Au than on Pt due to the intrinsically higher activity of Pt (see SI Section 1). Overall, then, we propose that the HER on Au-/Pt-S ad in alkaline solutions involves a Volmer-Tafel pathway in which the S ad δ---Cn+--H 2 O network serves to promote the water dissociation step followed by the adsorption of H ad intermediates on neighboring metal atoms that, finally, recombine to form H 2 . In acid solutions, however, due to the low activation barrier for the discharge of H 3 O+ on metal sites, S ad behaves as a spectator and poisons the active surface sites for the formation and recombination of H ad . To a first approximation, one might expect that the S ad δ---Cn+-H 2 O cluster would mimic, at least in part, the catalytic behavior of the TMn+-S x building blocks, as we discuss further below. Structure-activity-stability relationships and proposed active sites for the HER on TMS x Two types of TMS x with strikingly different morphologies are studied: commercially available crystalline MoS 2 and CoS 2 with regular, ordered arrays of sulfur and TMn+ centers held together by uniform intermolecular forces, and amorphous MoS x , CoS x and CoMoS x (x = 4-6) chalcogels with more irregular distances/numbers between metal centers and sulfide ligands 40–43. Insight into the structure and morphology of TMS x was obtained by utilizing several characterization tools described in the Methods Section (for characterization details see SI, Section 2). The TEM (transmission electron microscopy) micrograph in Figure 2a and SEM (scanning electron microscopy) images in Supplementary Figure S3 show that the chalcogels 4

Page 5

consist of random porous networks in the range of 50-100 nm that are composed of aggregated nanoparticles with rather high surface area (~200 m2/g). The appearance of both diffuse rings in the SAED (selected area electron diffraction) as well as the broad features centered around 25 nm-1 in the P-XRD (powder x-ray diffraction) patterns in Supplementary Figure S2 confirm the amorphous nature of the gels. Analysis of PDF data depicted in Figure 2b suggests that both MoS x and CoMoS x consist of [Mo 3 S 13 ] building blocks with triangular Mo metal clusters surrounded by sulfur atoms (characteristic bond distances are summarized in Supplementary Table S2). Further structural information was obtained from analyses of temperature-dependent magnetic susceptibility (TDMS) measurements in Figure 2c and Raman spectra (see Supplementary Figure S4). From the TDMS measurements we found that, as in the case of CoS x (see Supplementary Figure S5), CoMoS x obeys the Curie-Weiss law with an effective magnetic moment (0.6-1.2 μ Β ) that is too low to account for either low or high spin tetrahedral geometry of Co atoms; thus suggesting that the majority of Co atoms in CoS x and CoMoS x are octahedrally coordinated with the sulfur ligands. EXAFS analysis of CoS x confirms the presence of Co-Co interactions and the formation of characteristic Co-dimers in addition to the CoS 6 octahedra (see Supplementary Table S2), which agrees with the PDF data. Unlike CoS x , EXAFS analysis of CoMoS x reveals that the concentration of Co-Co interactions is extremely low when compared with the Co-Co interactions in CoS x (see Supplementary Figure S6). Admittedly, due to the amorphous nature of the chalcogels, the assessment of the coordination numbers for the average TMn+-TMn+ and TMn+-S pairs is ambiguous with large error bars, providing only a rough estimate of the most likely conformation of the chalcogel structures (for details see Supplementary Table S2). As summarized in Figure 2d and Supplementary Table S2, XPS (x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy) and XANES (X-ray absorption near edge structure) results show that Mo is in the 4+ valence state and Co is in the 2+ valence state, with sulfur showing the presence of polysulfide fragments (see Supplementary Figure S7). The chemical nature of the as-synthesized chalcogels was further characterized in situ by XAS (x-ray absorption spectroscopy) in the potential region where the HER takes place (see Supplementary Table S1). XAS results for CoMoS x in alkaline solutions are shown in Figure 3a, and are representative of all of the chalcogels discussed in this work (XAS results for other chalcogels are summarized in Supplementary Figure S8). Given that no potential-dependent variations in the oxidation state of 5

Page 6

Co and Mo are observed in the XAS spectra, it is reasonable to anticipate that between 0.05 and 0.5 V, the valence state of Mo and Co is the same as for the as-synthesized materials (e.g., Mo4+ and Co2+). Notice also that the TMn+-TMn+ and TMn+-S bond distances calculated by in situ EXAFS are similar to those determined from ex situ PDF data. Altogether, several important morphological and structural features are notable: (i) all chalcogels studied in this work have an amorphous structure with random porous networks; (ii) MoS x consists of robust Mo 3 S 13 clusters interconnected with MoS 4 tetrahedra (Figure 2e); (iii) CoS x has a more open structure with a random distribution of CoS 6 octahedra and a fraction of Co 2 S 6 blocks that contain Co-Co dimers (Figure 2e); and (iv) CoMoS x consists of Mo 3 S 13 clusters interconnected by CoS 8 octahedra that are capped by polysulfide oligomers in a compact and robust structure (Figure 2e). The proposed models in Figure 2e, however, do not exclude the existence of a significant number of defects in any of these amorphous TMS x materials. In fact, due to the amorphous nature of the TMS x chalcogels and the infinite arrangement of their building blocks (interconnected through loose S-S bonds), it is expected that the density of defects should be rather high, at least compared to crystalline MoS 2 and CoS 2 . Large differences in the arrangements of TMn+ and sulfur ligands in the unit building blocks and the presence of defects would suggest that the stability/activity of materials under the same electrochemical conditions would vary from one chalcogel to the other, as we demonstrate for the HER in alkaline and acid media. We first note that a fully rigorous kinetic analysis and reaction pathway of the HER lies beyond the scope of the present discussion. This is because for high surface area TMS x materials it would be very difficult, if not impossible, to establish unambiguously the turnover frequency, the exchange current density, and the Tafel slope; three “kinetic parameters” that historically have been used as key descriptors for determining the reaction pathway of the HER 44,45. In order to circumvent some of these problems, one alternative is to establish activity trends that can be expressed as reaction overpotential (η) at constant current density (in our case at 5mA/cm2, see Methods). Characteristic polarization curves are summarized in Figure 3b and Supplementary Figure S9, with the corresponding initial activity trends (after the first two cycles) shown in Figure 3c. Three key points are noteworthy: (i) in alkaline solutions there are large differences in activity (e.g., MoS x << CoMoS x << CoS x ), whereas in acid solutions the differences are 6

Page 7

significantly smaller (e.g., MoS x <CoS x ≤ CoMoS x ); (ii) the activity of CoMoS x is almost pHindependent; and (iii) for the crystalline chalcogenides, CoS 2 is always more active than MoS 2 , while both materials are more active in acidic than in alkaline solutions. As expected, the commercially-available, crystalline CoS 2 and MoS 2 used in this work are not as active as the highly optimized chalcogenides investigated in previous studies 22–24. In order to establish trends in the stability of the corresponding TMS x in the potential range where the HER takes place, the potential sweep-dependent deactivation of the catalyst was monitored (Figure 4a) and correlated with ex situ with quantitative measurements of the dissolution of Co and Mo via inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). For the purposes of this study, concentrations of cations dissolved during the HER (shown in Figure 4b and listed in Supplementary Table S3) are used as a reasonable indicator of the number of defects on a particular TMS x . Therefore, the dissolution-induced formation of defects serves as a useful indicator for establishing the important role defects play in electrocatalysis of the HER. Admittedly, neither the exact “mechanism” of dissolution nor a true stoichiometry of defect sites is known at the present; thus, schematics depicted in Figure 4c serve only as a crude representation of a possible active site motif comprised of single TMn+ cations and sulfur ligands. Close inspection of Figure 3 and Figure 4 reveals several interesting features. First, less active crystalline materials are more stable than their amorphous counterparts by approximately a factor of 10, suggesting that the density of defects plays an important role in activity-stability relationships. Second, since MoS x chalcogels are less active but ~35-40 times more stable than CoS x analogues, it is reasonable to suggest that the stability-activity relationship strongly depends on the structure/nature of the unit building blocks; e.g., the robust Mo 3 S 13 clusters interconnected with MoS 4 tetrahedra are more stable. Third, while Co2+ is ~5-times more stable in CoMoS x than in CoS x , Mo4+ is almost equally stable in CoMoS x and MoS x , indicating that the Co-based building blocks can be stabilized in the CoMoS x structure. Even more importantly, by interconnecting the active CoS x building blocks with the more stable MoS x units into the CoMoS x structure, it was possible to design a chalcogel material with unique combination of stability and activity in both alkaline and acid environments. Taken together, it is obvious that less stable TMS x materials are always more active, suggesting that this relationship is governed 7

Page 8

by the number of defect sites (e.g., morphological effects), which, to a first approximation, is proportional to the rate of dissolution of Co and Mo cations (Supplementary Table S2). Previously, similar activity-stability trends were observed for the HER on metal surfaces 46, CO oxidation reaction 47, and the oxygen evolution reaction on noble metal oxides 48 as well as on more complex perovskites 49, indicating that the observed activity-stability trends are more of a rule than an exception. Thus, together with the results from Au/Pt-S ad -Cn+-H 2 O, we suggest that TMn+ play the key role in accelerating the sluggish water dissociation step. For CoMoS x materials, the HER takes place simultaneously on the Co2+ and Mo4+ active centers; however, due to the higher instability of the Co2+ cations, the density of Co2+ active sites is higher so that they contribute more to the measured current density. In addition to pure morphological effects, electronic effects may also contribute to higher activity of Co-based materials. This is consistent with both the S ad δ----K+-/Ba2+-induced activation of water discussed above as well as with the previously-observed role of 3d-TM cations in catalyzing the HER on metal surfaces decorated by 3dTM(OH) 2. In particular, since activity decreases in the order Mn > Fe >> Co > Ni, it was concluded that the observed trend is governed by a delicate balance between the transition state energy for water dissociation (decreasing from Ni to Mn) and the final state energies of interaction between 3d-TMn+ and OHproduced in the water discharge step (increasing from Ni to Mn) 10. By implication, then, as shown in the schematics of Figure 4b, we propose that while water dissociation is kinetically facile on Mo, the turnover frequency for the Volmer step substantially decreases on these sites because of the stronger Mo-OH- interaction (OH- being the water dissociation product in the schematics of Figure 4c). The result is that the Mo4+ sites are less active than the Co2+ sites in the critical Volmer step. Although the activation barrier for deprotonation of H 3 O+ is very low, we cannot completely rule out that TMn+ also promote the formation of hydrogen intermediates from hydronium ions. In contrast to Au(111)-S ad , the nature of active centers for the adsorption of H ad and the concomitant H 2 production cannot be verified, at least not experimentally, for the TMS x systems. Further theoretical/computational insights are required to fully unravel the reaction mechanism on amorphous TMS x . In summary, we propose that: (i) TMn+ cations play decisive role in accelerating the slow rate of the Volmer step; (ii) the established activity-stability trends are of paramount importance for elucidating the role of TMn+ cations in the HER on TMS x ; and (iii) by blending the higher activity of CoS x building blocks with the higher stability of MoS x 8

units into a compact and robust CoMoS x structure, we were able to design a low-cost alternative to noble metal catalysts for efficient electrocatalytic production of hydrogen in both in alkaline and acid electrolyzers.

Methods Synthesis of TMS x chalcogels. CoMoS x was prepared by mixing the tetrathiomolybdate with the cobalt metal linker. To obtain a gel, an amount of 0.2 mmol of CoCl 2 .6H 2 O and 0.2 mmol of ammonium tetrathiomolybdate were dissolved in 2 mL of formamide (FM) in separate vials. Then the metal precursor solution was added slowly to the (NH 4 ) 2 MoS 4 solution and the resultant black product was kept standing for 2 weeks. The CoS x was prepared by treating the cobalt metal liker with polysulfide ions S 5 2-. In a typical preparation, 0.2 mmol of CoCl 2 .6H 2 O dissolved in a 2 ml of FM was added to 0.2 mmol of K 2 S 5 dissolved in 2 mL of FM. The black product was kept undisturbed for 7-10 days to result into a monolithic gel. MoS x gel was obtained by reacting iodine (I 2 ) with the ammonium tetrathiomolybdate. To form the gel, 1.2 mmol of iodine dissolved in 2 ml of DMF was added to 1.2 mmol of ammonium tetrathiomolybdate solution in 4 ml formamide. The dark red solution of (NH 4 ) 2 MoS 4 turned black to give a black pasty product on the addition of a violet solution of iodine. The resultant product was aged for 3 weeks. After gelation, the remaining formamide was decanted and solvent exchanged with ethanol/water mixture (1:1 by volume) then washed with ethanol for four days each. During the solvent exchange process, fresh supplies of solvents were provided twice a day. The washed gels in ethanol (alcogel) were dried under vacuum or ambient conditions to produce chalcogels. All the chalcogels are powders and black in color and their average composition was estimated by energy dispersive spectroscopy. Physical characterization of TMS x chalcogels. CTR and EXAFS/XANES. Pair Distribution Function (PDF) analysis. The pair distribution function method (PDF) is a powerful technique for local structure studies. PDF describes the distribution of interatomic distances in a material. The power and the difference of PDF from other crystallographic techniques like Rietveld is that PDF is a total scattering technique which means both the Bragg and diffuse scattering are treated on an equal basis. PDF studies both the long-range atomic structure (Bragg reflections) and the local structure imperfections (diffuse component of the diffraction pattern). The data analysis does not presume any periodicity therefore the PDF technique is very useful for examining amorphous and crystalline samples. Diffraction patterns were collected at room temperature on powder samples of each chalcogel that was packed in a Kapton capillary. The rapid acquisition pair distribution function (RA-PDF) technique was used to collect diffraction data at room temperature using a Perkin-Elmer image plate detector and Xrays with an energy of around 90 keV (λ = 0.143 Å) at the 11-ID-B beamline at the Advanced Photon Source (APS) at Argonne National Laboratory. Counting statistics were improved by the collection of 200 frames per sample with an exposure time of 2 seconds per frame. The data were integrated using the program Fit2d and corrections (subtraction of background and container, Compton and fluorescence scattering, geometric and absorption corrections, etc) were performed using the program PDFgetX3. The normalized data were truncated at 24 Å−1 before PDF calculation. PDFgui was used to model the data. Further information and references can be found in Supporting Information. The pair distribution function method (PDF) is a powerful technique for local structure studies. PDF describes the distribution of interatomic distances in a material. The power and the difference of PDF from other crystallographic techniques like Rietveld is that PDF is a total scattering technique which means both the Bragg and diffuse scattering are treated on an equal basis. PDF studies both the longrange atomic structure (Bragg reflections) and the local structure imperfections (diffuse component of the diffraction pattern). The data analysis does not presume any periodicity therefore the PDF technique is very useful for examining amorphous and crystalline samples. The rapid acquisition pair distribution function (RA-PDF) technique [S6] was used to collect diffraction patterns at room temperature on powder samples of each chalcogel. The data were integrated using the program Fit2D [S7] and corrections (subtraction of background and container, Compton and fluorescence scattering, geometric and absorption corrections, etc.) [S8] were performed using the program PDFgetX3 [S9]. The normalized data were truncated at 24 Å−1 before PDF calculation. PDFgui was used to model the data [S10]. The experiments are straightforward X-ray or neutron powder diffraction measurements. Stability of chalcogel materials To establish not only the electrochemical performance changes during cycling, monitoring materials dissolution is relevant not only to account for the performance loss but, to a first approximation, correlate to the number of active sites. We use ICP-MS analysis to determine the amount of Co and Mo lost during potential cycling. The results are shown only for pH 1 as in alkaline media precipitation of TMn+ hinders their detection in solution, even by ICP-MS. The ICP-MS results shown in Supplementary Table S1 indicate three main trends for the chalcogels materials. First, the chalcogels are most stable as crystalline form than as amorphous. Second, chalcogels containing cobalt shows grater dissolved metal contents in solution than the one containing only Mo. These results are close related to the activity trend observed, where amorphous materials are much more active than crystalline and within amorphous, CoSx are more active than MoSx. For last, the combination of Co and Mo centers in CoMoSx suggests a remarkable stability synergy, observing a reduced cobalt dissolution rate when compared to pure CoSx at the same time that the Mo becomes slightly less stable in CoMoSx than in MoSx. Additional to elucidating the stability of the TM centers by following their dissolution, their correlation with the activity observed for the HER indicates the active role the TM centers play in the HER mechanism.

Effect of pH on the HER for Chalcogel materials To understand the reaction mechanism for the hydrogen evolution reaction, pH can be used as powerful probe as the change in the reactant nature for hydrogen formation can tell us what the limiting step in the reaction pathway is. Supplementary Figure S9 show the polarization curves in both acid and alkaline for all materials used here. The general trend observed for all materials is an enhancement of the reaction rate at lower pH values, as the reactant nature is the hydronium ion, much easier to brake to form H atoms than water, as in the case of pH 13. CoMoSx on the other hand is the only material not showing this pronounced tendency, which leads us to point in a rate determining step change from the other materials. While braking hydronium or water is the usual limiting step for the chalcogel, the combination of CoSx and MoSx units in CoMoSx enables the reaction to break the initial reactants at much faster rate, turning the HER limited only by the recombination step of Hads. This indicates that this material can provide even further activity enhancement if the appropriate modification to enhance the Tafel step is selected.
