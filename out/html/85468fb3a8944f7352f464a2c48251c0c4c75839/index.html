Mitzi[a], * Abstract: The double perovskite family, A2MIMIIIX6, represents a promising route to overcome the lead toxicity issue confronting current photovoltaic (PV) standout, CH3NH3PbI3. Given the generally large indirect bandgap within most known double perovskites, bandgap engineering provides an important approach for targeting outstanding PV performance within this family. Using Cs2AgBiBr6 as host, we demonstrate bandgap engineering through alloying of InIII/SbIII. Cs2Ag(Bi1-xMx)Br6 (M = In, Sb) accommodates up to 75% InIII with increased bandgap, and up to 37.5% SbIII with reduced bandgap—i.e., enabling ~0.41 eV bandgap modulation through introduction of the two metals, with smallest value of 1.86 eV for Cs2Ag(Bi0.625Sb0.375)Br6. Band structure calculations indicate that opposite bandgap shift directions associated with Sb/In substitution arise from different atomic configurations for these atoms. Associated photoluminescence and environmental stability of the three-metal systems are also assessed.

Photovoltaic research on the hybrid organic-inorganic perovskite (HOIP) APbX3 (A = small organic cation, e.g., methylammonium; X = Cl, Br and I), a system first reported on in 1978,[1] has increased explosively since the first solar cell report involving CH3NH3Pb(I,Br)3 in 2009.[2] Along with the remarkable development of energy conversion efficiency, the limitations of this particular HOIP (e.g. long-term instability and Pb toxicity) have also become increasingly apparent.[3] One straightforward idea to solve the Pb toxicity issue involves replacing the PbII with SnII or GeII, from the same group 14 of the periodic table. However, SnII or GeII readily oxidize, due to the high energy 5s and 4s orbitals, and the corresponding perovskites exhibit extreme sensitivity to the ambient atmosphere.[4] Using BiIII or SbIII to replace PbII provides a more stable solution. However, the trivalent vs divalent oxidation state for the metal implies a need for vacancies on the metal site and therefore a lower electronic and structural dimensionality and larger bandgap for the resulting A3MIII2VX9 (A = small cations including Cs and methylammonium; V = vacancy; MIII = Bi and Sb; X = Cl, Br and I).[5] In this context, the three-dimensional double perovskite, A2MIMIIIX6 (A = small cations, e.g., Cs, methylammonium; BI = monovalent metal ions, including Ag, Na, and K; MIII = trivalent metal ions, e.g., Bi, and Sb; X = Br, Cl, F and CN), has become a promising new candidate for optoelectronic application.[6] In

However, the bandgaps of the current double perovskites are generally either large, indirect or direct forbidden—i.e., not a suitable solution for highefficiency photovoltaic application.[6-7] Though some computational studies indicate promise when both metals involve lone pair states (e.g. Cs2InSbCl6 and Cs2InBiCl6),[8] our experimental and calculation results confirm the instability of these compounds.[9] A recent experimental report considers Tlsubstituted Cs2AgBiBr6, which exhibits comparable bandgap energy and carrier lifetime to those of (CH3NH3)PbI3,[10] with however the use of highly toxic Tl. Given these observations, further efforts focused on bandgap engineering are required if double perovskites are to play an important role in future optoelectronics. Atomic substitution is a primary method for semiconductor bandgap engineering,[11] with all lattice sites of the double perovskite (A2MIMIIIX6) available for substitution. Considering the four different lattice sites, MIII provides the most degrees of freedom, with over 34 choices of trivalent metals available for selection.[6g] In this work, we use Cs2AgBiBr6 as a host material, which exhibits a long carrier recombination lifetime, for bandgap modification with SbIII and InIII substitution on the BiIII lattice site. The lattice constants of the alloyed compounds, Cs2Ag(Bi1xMx)Br6 (M = In, Sb), as measured and refined by powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) (Table S1), decrease linearly with increasing x. InIII can replace as much as 75% of the Bi III before secondary phases appear in the PXRD data. SbIII has a smaller homogeneity range, with up to 37.5% substitution demonstrated. Notably, the bandgap measured by diffuse reflection increases in energy with InIII incorporation and decreases for SbIII substitution. Relatively broad photoluminescence (PL) peaks, with peak position ranging from 550 to 900 nm, depend on alloying type/concentration. As previously reported, Cs2AgBiBr6 crystallizes in the cubic 𝐹𝑚3̅𝑚 space group with unit-cell parameter a = 11.27 Å and a three-dimensional (3-D) perovskite structure (Figure 1a).[6b] When suitably small levels of InIII or SbIII substitute into the BiIII position of Cs2AgBiBr6, the products retain the original Cs2AgBiBr6 structure, as demonstrated with the PXRD data of Figure 1b. However, increasing the level of substitution reduces the lattice parameter linearly following Vegard’s law[12] for both InIII and SbIII incorporation (Figure 1c), in accordance with the smaller radii of InIII (94 pm) and SbIII (90 pm) relative to BiIII (117 pm).[13] The systems Cs2AgBi1-xInxBr6 (x > 0.75) and Cs2AgBi1xSbxBr6 (x > 0.375) are unstable as shown in Figure S1.

UV-vis diffuse reflection data have been collected for the single phase substituted double perovskites and converted into F(R) (corresponding to the absorption coefficient, ; See ESI) using the Kubelka-Munk function.[14] From Tauc plots for bangap fitting,[15] we deduce direct bandgaps of from 2.15 eV to 2.41 eV and indirect bandgaps of from 1.86 eV to 2.27 eV in the Cs2Ag(Bi1-xMx)Br6 (M = In, Sb) alloys (Figure 2). Given the band structure results described below, we use indirect bandgap values for further discussion. The bandgap of Cs2AgBi1-xInxBr6 increases from 2.12 eV to 2.27 eV as x increases from 0 to 0.75. In contrast, the bandgap of Cs2AgBi1-xSbxBr6 decreases from 2.12 eV to 1.86 eV as x increases from 0 to 0.375 (Figure 2d). The bandgap shift agrees with the sample color change (Figure 2e). where E(x), E(CAIB) and E(CABB) are the bandgaps of Cs2AgBi1-xInxBr6, Cs2AgInBr6 (hypothetical) and Cs2AgBiBr6, respectively, and b is the bowing parameter. From this analysis, we get the formulas E(Cs2AgBi1-xInxBr6) = 2.12 + 0.265x – 0.092x2 and analogously E(Cs2AgBi1-xSbxBr6) = 2.12 –1.067x + 1.027x2. The bowing parameter (b = 0.092) of E(Cs2AgBi1xInxBr6), which may reflect the miscibility of the guest and host in the alloy,[17] is much smaller than the analogous value (b = 1.027) for E(Cs2AgBi1-xSbxBr6), suggesting that Cs2AgBiBr6 better matches with Cs2AgInBr6 (compared with Cs2AgSbBr6) in terms of crystal/band structures and chemical electronegativity.[18] The smaller bowing parameter for the InIII-incorporated series may also correlate with the increased homogeneity range for the Cs2AgBi1-xInxBr6 series relative to Cs2AgBi1-xSbxBr6.

All powder samples have relatively broad PL emission, with unsubstituted Cs2AgBiBr6 exhibiting a similar spectrum to previous literature reports.[6b] For the In alloyed samples, 75.0% substitution yields the smallest PL intensity, whereas 25.0% substitution offers the largest intensity (Figure 3a). The Sbsubstituted-sample PL is weaker and obviously shifted to longer wavelength compared to the analogous spectrum for Cs2AgBiBr6 (Figure 3b). All PL spectra fit well to two Gaussian peaks (Figures 3c and S2). The two fitted PL peaks in the Insubstituted samples appear at ~610-560 nm and ~680 nm, respectively. Comparing with the corresponding bandgap, the ~610-560 nm emission shifts with substitution level and correlates well with band-edge emission, while the 680 nm emission (1.8 eV, i.e., smaller than the bandgap) may reflect a defect state in the band structure. The gradually reduced PL intensity for In alloying above the 25% level may reflect the fact that the defect state becomes deeper with higher In alloying level and/or it may signify that the transitions from valence to conduction band become symmetry forbidden as In 5s states begin to dominate the conduction band.[7] The two fitted PL peaks for Sb-substituted samples appear fixed at ~775 nm (1.6 eV) and ~905 nm (1.4 eV), respectively. Both of these values fall significantly lower than the corresponding bandgap (> ~0.25 eV), suggesting the presence of substantial relatively-deep defect states, which may account for the rapidly suppressed emission intensity.

To further understand optical absorption/emission in our samples, we calculated the band structures of Cs 2AgBiBr6 alloys with Bi substituted by differing amounts of Sb/In (Figure 4), using density-functional theory (DFT) with hybrid functional[19] plus spin-orbital coupling (HSE+SOC).[4b] For easy comparison, the

band structures are aligned with respect to the Cs 5s states (the lowest flat orange lines in Figure 4). For Cs2AgBiBr6, the conduction band minimum (CBM) derives from Bi 6p orbitals and the valence band maximum (VBM) mainly derives from Bi 6s orbitals (Figure 4a), yielding an indirect calculated fundamental bandgap of 2.0 eV, which agrees with the experimental 2.12 eV value. Substitution of Sb for Bi in Cs2AgBi0.75Sb0.25Br6 (Figure 4b) introduces a new Sb 5s orbital-derived valence band positioned above the Bi 6s band, and new Sb 5p orbital-derived conduction bands with similar energy ranges to the Bi 6p-derived conduction bands. As a result, the CBM shifts down by 0.15 eV and the VBM shifts up by 0.27 eV. The bandgap remains indirect, but the calculated fundamental bandgap is reduced to 1.58 eV in Cs2AgBi0.75Sb0.25Br6. The bandgap reduction trend induced by Sb substitution agrees with the experimental results. When more Bi atoms are substituted by Sb atoms, the contribution of Bi 6p orbitals to the CBM becomes less and the Sb 5s-derived valence band slightly broadens. For the hypothetical Cs2AgSbBr6 double perovskite, the CBM is derived by the Sb 5p orbitals and VBM is derived mainly from the Sb 5s orbital (Figure 4c). The CBM is 0.04 eV lower, but the VBM is 0.29 eV higher for Cs2AgSbBr6 than that for Cs2AgBiBr6. The calculated HSE+SOC fundamental bandgap is 1.67 eV. Substitution using In leads to different band structure changes. For Cs2AgBi0.75In0.25Br6 (Figure 4d), In substitution introduces In 4s states in the lower conduction band (LCB), with this band falling 0.06 eV lower in energy than the Bi 6s-derived CBM for Cs2AgBiBr6 and therefore forming the new CBM. The VBM still derives from Bi 6s orbitals and remains at the same energy position; however, its VBM density of states (DOS) decreases due to the reduced Bi atom density. The calculated fundamental bandgap is still indirect but decreases by 0.06 eV, in contradiction with the experimental trend—i.e., the apparent experimental bandgap increases as In concentration increases. This discrepancy may arise from two factors: (1) The lower VBM DOS leads to lower absorption for photons with energies close to the bandgap value and therefore a weak absorption spectrum tail, which may drive an underestimated experimental bandgap; (2) The supercell used in our calculations incorporates chemical ordering—i.e., some [BiBr6] octahedra have the same arrangement as that in Cs2AgBiBr6. The coupling between these [BiBr6] octahedra may lead to significant underestimation of the bandwidth narrowing for the Bi 6s band and therefore an overestimation of the VBM position. The chemical ordering effect could be explored with a larger supercell; however, this is not feasible for the computerresource-intensive HSE+SOC approach. To qualitatively verify this speculation, we calculated the band structure of Cs2AgBi0.75In0.25Br6 using a double-sized supercell and generalized gradient approximation (GGA) of the Perdew-BurkeErnzerh (PBE) functional[20] for exchange-correlation without SOC. As shown in Figure S3, the VBM of Cs2AgBi0.75In0.25Br6 (panel b) down-shifts by 0.03 eV compared to that of analogous Cs2AgBiBr6 (panel a), with larger downshifts expected as supercell size increases. When the In concentration increases, the chemical ordering of the [BiBr6] octahedra decreases, leading to a much-reduced bandwidth for the valence band (shown in panels c-f of Fig. S3). Due to the high In concentration, chemical ordering among [InBr6] octahedra leads to some broadening of the In 5sdominated LCB and the new CBM down shifts by 0.4 eV. Therefore, the HSE+SOC calculated bandgap for Cs2AgBi0.25In0.75Br6 is 2.28 eV, which is 0.28 larger than that of Cs2AgBiBr6, consistent with the experimentally observed trend. These results reveal competing effects upon In incorporation, between the down-shift of the CBM caused by the In 5s states and the down-shift of the VBM caused by the reduced connection among Bi atoms. The bandgap increases if the second effect outweighs the first, as is most likely the case with our experimental bandgap results for Cs2AgBi1-xInxBr6 (x  0.75). However, when the In concentration is high enough, such that the [InBr6] octahedra exhibit strong coupling, the In 5s-derived conduction band may have large band broadening [see Figure 4f], leading to reduced bandgap. Regarding the environmental stability of the substituted double perovskites, the samples for testing were maintained in the ambient atmosphere (30~50% humidity) under fluorescent lighting, with the PXRD monitored as a function of time (Figure 5). CsAgBr2 was confirmed in the decomposition product for each sample (Figure 5d). However, the emerging peaks represent a minority of the sample relative to the main peaks. After 120 days’ exposure, additional Cs3Bi2Br9 could be identified in Cs2AgBiBr6 (Figure S4). So the typical decomposition pathway may be Cs2AgBiBr6 → 1/2CsAgBr2 + 1/2AgBr + 1/2Cs3Bi2Br9.

In conclusion, we have successfully synthesized the substituted double perovskites, Cs2AgBi1-xInxBr6 (x = 0, 0.25, 0.5 and 0.75) and Cs2AgBi1-xSbxBr6 (x = 0, 0.125 and 0.375), with the double perovskite phase being unstable substantially outside the listed range of x. The cubic lattice constant progressively shrinks for all substituted systems, in comparison with the original Cs2AgBiBr6. Notably, increasing In content enlarges the bandgap, whereas increasing Sb content reduces the bandgap. Broad PL spectra have also been observed and discussed in the context of the shifting bandgap and possible presence of defects. Band structure calculations suggest the different atomic configurations of Sb and In induce the different bandgap changes for the corresponding alloys. These results highlight the complex nature and impact of atomic substitution on optoelectronic character within the double perovskite family.
