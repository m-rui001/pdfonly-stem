BACKGROUND: Electromagnetic (EM) waves underpin modern society in profound ways: they are used to carry information, enabling broadcast radio and television, mobile telecommunications, ubiquitous access to data networks via WiFi, and forming the backbone of our modern broadband internet via optical fibers. In fundamental physics, EM waves serve as an invaluable tool to probe objects from cosmic to atomic scales. For example, the Laser Interferometer Gravitational-Wave Observatory (LIGO) and atomic clocks, which are some of the most precise human-made instruments in the world, rely on EM waves to reach unprecedented accuracies. This has motivated decades of research to develop coherent EM sources over broad spectral ranges with impressive results: Frequencies in the range of tens of GHz (radio and microwave regimes) can readily be generated by electronic oscillators. Resonant tunneling diodes enable the generation of mm and THz waves, which span from tens of GHz to a few THz. At even higher frequencies, up to the PHz level, which we usually define as optical frequencies, coherent waves can be generated by solid-state and gas lasers. However, those approaches often suffer from narrow spectral bandwidths, since they usually rely on well-defined energy states of specific materials, resulting in a rather limited spectral coverage. To overcome this limitation, nonlinear frequency-mixing strategies have been developed. These approaches shift the complexity from the EM source to non-resonant-based material effects. Particularly, in the optical regime, a wealth of materials exist that support effects suitable for frequency mixing. Over the last two decades, the idea of manipulating these materials to form guiding structures (waveguides) has provided improvements in efficiency, miniaturization, production scale and cost, and has been widely implemented for diverse applications. ADVANCES: Lithium niobate, which was first grown in 1949, is a particularly attractive photonic material for frequency mixing thanks to its favorable material properties. Abstract: Lithium niobate (LN), first synthesized 70 years ago, has been widely used in diverse applications ranging from communications to quantum optics. These high-volume commercial applications provided the economic means to establish a mature manufacturing and processing industry for high-quality LN crystals and wafers. Breakthrough science demonstrations to commercial products have been achieved due to its ability to generate and manipulate EM waves across a broad spectrum, from microwave to UV frequencies. In this contribution, we provide a high-level review of the history of LN as an optical material, its different photonic platforms, engineering concepts, spectral coverage, and some applications before providing an outlook for LN’s future.

The technological development of our modern society is closely linked to our ability to make use of electromagnetic (EM) waves. The wide EM spectrum, spanning from radio- and microwaves through infrared radiation, visible light, ultraviolet radiation, up to high-energy X- and 𝛾𝛾-rays, has transformed the way we image, carry information, and transmit energy. Driven by the sophistication over the control of EM waves, the past few decades have witnessed remarkable breakthroughs in a wide range of areas such as high-speed communication (1, 2), ultra-precision time-frequency metrology (3–5), bio-imaging (6–8) and quantum-information science (9, 10). The generation and manipulation of EM waves lies at the heart of all scientific and technological explorations. Depending on the frequencies, there are several main strategies for generation and processing: Radio frequency (<~100 GHz) signals can readily be produced by microwave oscillators and then manipulated through conventional CMOS electronics; signals between 0.3 to 10 THz, which are usually regarded as mm or THz waves, can be generated, for example, by resonant tunneling diodes and processed by high-speed electronics utilizing silicon or III-V semiconductors. For higher frequencies (10 THz to 1 PHz) in the optical regime, the most common methods for EM wave generation use solid-state, fiber, gas, and semiconductor lasers. Although these strategies have successfully enabled many applications, each platform individually can only offer a limited spectral coverage due to their dependence on well-defined energy bands or levels of solid-state materials, atoms, and molecules. To overcome this limitation, another important strategy, parametric nonlinear frequency mixing, was introduced. Starting from optical frequencies (hundreds of THz), this process leverages broadband parametric nonlinear effects for multi-wave mixing to unlock previously inaccessible EM frequencies on demand. This approach shifts the complexity from a custom EM wave source to non-resonant material effects, which can be engineered with additional degrees of freedom, allowing access to a much broader part of the EM spectrum (THz to PHz) with unprecedented control and performance. In addition to nonlinear frequency mixing, the GHz to THz part of the EM spectrum can be bridged via microwave-to-optical conversion, enabling the efficient processing of EM waves through well-established CMOS electronics. These prospects spurred the development of a wide range of nonlinear and electro-optic material platforms over the past few decades. Among these platforms, Lithium Niobate (LiNbO3, LN), which has been described as the ‘The Silicon of Photonics’ (11), turns out to be particularly suitable for the generation and manipulation of EM frequencies, as it offers a rare combination of advantageous properties: (i) large electro-optic, piezoelectric, and nonlinear-optic material coefficients (ii) engineerability of velocity matching via quasi-phasematching and waveguide dispersion, (iii) broad transparency (400 nm to 5 µm), (iv) long-term stability and (v) widespread commercial availability of large, low-cost, optical-quality wafers. This makes LN one of the key photonic materials with the potential to expand access to an ultrawide part of the EM spectrum and support the next generation of breakthrough scientific demonstrations and commercial products.

Lithium Niobate Platforms LN is a ferroelectric crystal, first synthesized in 1949 (12) in its polycrystalline form. From this discovery it took 15 years until further studies identified the material’s characteristic electrooptical (13) and second-order nonlinear-optical (14) properties. The growth of single-crystalline LN using the Czochralski technique (15, 16) represented a breakthrough; this technique is still in use today and able to produce optical-quality wafers up to a diameter of 150 mm (6 inches) with several crystal compositions such as congruent, near-stoichiometric, or doped with alkaline or transition metals being commercially available.

Such crystals are typically millimeter to centimeter-scale blocks of LN with optical-grade polished facets (see inset Fig. 1). Early demonstrations in bulk LN crystals include electro-optic modulation (13) and secondharmonic generation (14). A breakthrough discovery occurred, when it was observed that the 5

spontaneous polarization of LN crystals could be inverted locally by applying a high electric field at room temperature (17). This process, referred to as ‘Electric-Field Poling’, opened a reliable path for engineering the phase-velocity matching (i.e. momentum conservation) between different waves and made previously explored domain inversion methods that relied on high temperature ionic diffusion processes (18) obsolete. Photorefraction was first discovered when investigating LN for nonlinear devices (19), providing later the means for high density data storage in LN (20). Bulk crystals have also been formed in the shape of disks and ring cavities, by careful polishing their facets (21–23). Such discs can form so-called whispering gallery resonators (24), with quality factors reaching hundreds of millions (25), making them attractive for highly coherent optical/microwave sources and nonlinear-optical applications. Recently, it was shown that ferroelectric domain engineering can also be achieved in three dimensions by using femtosecond laser pulses that are focused into the crystal (26). This demonstration opens opportunities for a new class of wave-mixing devices that were previously not feasible, such as 3D nonlinear photonic crystals. Bulk crystals are particularly attractive in optical cavity configurations such as parametric oscillators (27, 28) (see Figs. 2 and 4) to enhance the nonlinear interaction and for high-power applications, inside laser cavities (Q-switch, intra-cavity second-harmonic generation), or when using ultra-short, high-peak-power laser pulses. Weakly confining LN waveguides maintain the interacting fields over centimeter-length distances in small mode volumes at high intensities (Fig. 1), thereby increasing nonlinear mixing efficiencies by two to three orders of magnitude when compared to bulk crystals. This relaxes the optical power requirements and enables efficient EM wave generation at moderate optical powers in the tens of mW (continuous-wave) or few nJ (pulsed) range. Weakly confining LN waveguides can be formed by slightly altering the material composition or structure to locally increase the refractive index to form the guiding core. The first weakly confining waveguides were demonstrated by lithium out-diffusion (29), shortly followed by titanium in-diffusion (30), and later proton exchange (31) and femtosecond laser writing (32). Titanium in-diffusion and proton exchange remain common fabrication methods and require elevated temperatures to drive the diffusion processes (~1100˚C for titanium in-diffusion and ~200˚C for proton exchange), which increase the extraordinary refractive index of LN by a few times 10-3 (33) and ~0.1 (34), respectively. To form low-loss, nonlinear-active waveguides via proton exchange, an annealing step at ~300˚C is used, reducing the index contrast to ~0.02. Such waveguides have been used for a number of frequency-mixing demonstrations such as the first implementation of quasi-phasematching (see Figure 3) for second-harmonic generation in LN (35, 36) and integrated erbium lasers (37). Importantly, used as low-loss, high-speed electro-optic modulators, such waveguides were a key component for long-haul communication systems (38). This platform has been explored for low-loss quantum-optical applications, for example upconversion to near-visible wavelengths for single-photon detection (39) and on-chip entangled photon-pair generation via spontaneous parametric down conversion and control of the generated photons (40). A large body of work in this field is reviewed in (41). Weakly confining waveguides are attractive as their mode volume is naturally close to that of standard optical fibers, enabling low interface losses (<0.5dB at near infrared wavelengths) between waveguide and fiber. Thus, a wide range of optical equipment that has been developed for high-speed optical communication can readily be connected to these waveguides, providing a large range of linear and nonlinear optical signal processing functionalities (42). can be inexpensively produced on a wafer scale and only require standard lithographic tools with readily available micrometer resolution. Tightly confining LN waveguides are a relatively new class of LN structures with even smaller mode volumes reaching sub-wavelength mode diameters, resulting in nearly two orders of magnitude higher frequency mixing efficiencies compared to weakly confining waveguides in combination with offering broad integration and dispersion-engineering opportunities. Such waveguides (43) use a thin film of LN on a lower-index cladding layer, akin to the siliconon-insulator (SOI) platform, which can be manufactured at scale and with good film uniformity and quality. Optical waveguides are typically made by dry etching (44) the LN film to form ridge waveguides or by strip-loading with a material that has a refractive index higher than the top cladding (45), however also methods such as laser ablation and diamond blade scoring have been explored. The optical modes in such waveguides are tightly confined due to the high refractive index contrast between guiding core and cladding and the often sub-wavelength waveguide dimensions, enabling dense integration using low-loss small-radius bends (46). This high index contrast has enabled highly efficient and compact frequency mixing/generating components such as frequency doublers (45, 47, 48), electro-optic modulators (49, 50), optical frequency comb generators (51–53) as well as lasers/amplifiers based on Er doping (54, 55). The thin-film LN (TFLN) platform enabled large-scale photonic integration, approaching those available on semiconductor platforms (56). This is a highly attractive proposition as LN’s material properties allow for the homogeneous integration of important active and passive photonic functions, often eliminating the need for additional materials. To incorporate lasers and detectors on TFLN, blocks based on III-V semiconductor materials still require heterogeneous integration (57), which may be accomplished using techniques developed for other popular integratedphotonic platforms such as silicon and silicon nitride (58). Since TFLN combines high confinement and high nonlinearity, it is particularly suitable for lowpower continuous-wave, and low-energy pulsed applications (59). For high-power or high-energy pulsed excitation, confinement or nonlinearity are less critical, many other bulk materials such KTP and BBO are frequently used. However, one advantage of thin-film platforms, such as TFLN, over bulk solutions is the flexibility of engineering the waveguide geometry, and hence dispersion, resulting in greater flexibility of phase/group velocity matching, leading to novel operating regimes. Material Properties and Engineering Concepts for Bridging the Electromagnetic Spectrum LN is one of several materials with attractive properties for generating and manipulating EM waves, including large nonlinear-optic, electro-optic, and piezo-electric coefficients: Nonlinear-optical effects can be used to generate new EM waves via second-order (𝜒𝜒(2)) and thirdorder (𝜒𝜒(3)) nonlinear-optical processes. Below LN’s Curie temperature (1150˚C), its crystal structure is non-centrosymmetric, giving rise to a large second-order nonlinearity, with d33 = -27 pm/V at 1064 nm (all waves polarized parallel to the optical axis) being the largest component. Common second-order nonlinear processes include second-harmonic generation (SHG), sumfrequency generation (SFG) and difference-frequency generation (DFG) (Fig. 2A). Third-order nonlinear optical processes in LN utilize its nonlinear refractive index (n2) 1.8×10-19 m2/W (60), 7

which is of similar in strength to Si3N4 (2.5×10-19 m2/W at 1.55 µm), enabling efficient four-wave mixing processes suitable for applications such as Kerr microcomb generation (Fig. 2B). The linear electro-optic effect (or Pockels effect) changes the refractive index of LN proportional to an applied electric field, which can be used to modulate EM waves passing through the crystal, generating new frequencies around the injected wave spaced by a single or multiple of the electrical modulation frequency (Fig. 2C). LN’s largest electro-optic coefficient (r33 = 30.8 pm/V at 0.633 µm), typically requires interaction lengths of several millimeters up to a few centimeters to achieve the desired phase shifts with reasonably low voltages. The effect has been used to modulate and manipulate EM waves with electrical signals from DC to hundreds of GHz. Important to note is that this effect can be used to phase modulate light with minimal intensity modulation, which is advantageous over modulators in silicon that typically modulate the phase with considerable intensity modulations. The photo-elastic effect changes the refractive index of LN as a function of strain, which can be used for modulating and frequency shifting EM waves by interacting with periodically sheared or compressed LN caused by the excitation of acoustic waves via the piezoelectric effect (Fig. 2D). Typically, operation frequencies of acousto-optical devices in LN are in the MHz to single-digit GHz range, mainly due to practical considerations such as the size of the crystal and manufacturability of the electrodes. A more detailed description of these effects and their use in LN can be found in several other review papers (60–65). Important to note is that LN does not necessarily exhibit the strongest material effects compared to other materials. Indeed, there are many other materials such as KTP, BBO, GaAs and InP that have attractive material properties. However, LN distinguishes itself with its maturity, stability, commercial availability, wide transparency range and engineerability with respect to the coupling between a wide spectral range of EM frequencies, making their generation more efficient and tailorable.

Velocity matching is used for one of the most common optical components in LN, namely broadband electro-optic traveling-wave modulators (Fig. 3A). For efficient electro-optic interaction of the RF wave with the optical wave, the phase velocity of the single frequency radiofrequency wave and the group velocity of the optical wave should ideally be identical. This can be achieved by using waveguide and electrode dimensions as well as material dispersion. In the weakly confined LN waveguide platform, the relatively high dielectric constant (𝜀𝜀11,22 = 44, 𝜀𝜀33 = 27.9) of LN at microwave frequencies can result in a velocity mismatch, which can be addressed by using thick electrodes (a few µm) to increase the phase velocity of the radio-frequency wave. The lower dielectric constant of SiO2, which is a common buffer layer for tightly confining waveguides, increases the phase velocity of the radio frequency wave, overcoming the need for thick electrodes for velocity matching. Dispersion engineering uses the material stack and waveguide dimensions as degrees of freedom to engineer the modal dispersion, which depends on the wavelength-dependent field distribution in the core and cladding of the waveguide (Fig. 3B). This waveguide (or geometric) dispersion can become a significant factor in the overall dispersion, otherwise solely determined by the material properties, providing control over the group-velocity mismatch and group-velocity dispersion (59, 9

Quasi-phasematching (QPM) is a concept to compensate for the phase-velocity (i.e. momentum) mismatch of different waves. This can be achieved by either periodically inverting the spontaneous polarization of the crystal when the phase mismatch reaches 180˚ (after a distance called the coherence length LC (17) (Fig. 3C), or, in guided-wave platforms, by periodically perturbing the magnitude of the nonlinear coupling through modifications of the waveguide dimensions (67). In the small signal conversion regime, this results in a unidirectional energy flow over the entire propagation length. The periodic reversal of the spontaneous polarization can be achieved using well-developed optical, thermal and electrical domain-engineering methods (68), among which the electric-field poling method is the most widely adopted one and has been applied to all three LN platforms to generate periodically and aperiodically poled LN crystals with periods reaching sub-micron dimensions (69). Due to the Fourier-transform relationship between the QPM grating and the device’s transfer function (70–72), a non-trivial frequency response can also be readily engineered (73). It is important to note that the electric-field poling and the QPM engineerability is not available in most other optical material platforms and is one of the main reasons for LN’s wide adoption for nonlinear optical applications.

Visible and ultraviolet light (400 to 900 THz) experiences very low material losses in LN due to the 3.93-eV-wide band gap (~950 THz) (84) of LN. Light in this spectral range is required for visual applications such as virtual reality (85), probing atomic transitions for optical clocks or magnetic field sensors (86) as well as molecules and cells for bioimaging (6). Visible frequencies can be generated in LN by making use of the material's second and third-order optical nonlinearities in combination with well-developed near-infrared light sources. For example, the second-order optical nonlinearity has enabled the generation of blue (17, 81, 87, 88), green (80, 87), yellow (87), orange (87, 89), and red (87) frequencies either by using second-harmonic generation (SHG), which increases the EM frequency by one octave, by sum-frequency generation (SFG), or a combination thereof. The highest EM frequencies that can be generated by such nonlinear optical processes are only limited by the UV absorption edge of LN (~950 THz, or 315 nm), reaching frequencies of 800 to 900 THz (78, 87) in the near UV. Supercontinuum generation (SCG) provides another means to generate visible light in LN by generating EM frequencies over a very broad spectral bandwidth possibly covering the entire visible spectrum and can reach all the way to 850 THz (59, 77, 90). SCG typically utilizes the third-order nonlinearity in dispersion-engineered waveguides. However in LN, the second-order optical nonlinearity can further help to push the generated spectrum towards shorter wavelengths via SHG and SFG (77). Furthermore, the cascading of two second-order processes can result in a 14

large effective third-order nonlinearity, exceeding the material’s third-order nonlinearity, and whose sign can be controlled through the choice of sign of the phase mismatch. (91, 92). Near-infrared frequencies (150 to 400 THz) are of particular interest since they are low enough for Rayleigh scattering to be minimal, but high enough such that molecular absorption can be avoided in specific windows enabling low loss transmission through optical fibers and on photonic integrated circuits (PICs). This makes them particularly attractive for applications such as optical communications (1, 2), microwave photonics (93), quantum optics (9, 10), as well as free-space applications which operate outside the visible spectrum, such as light detection and ranging (LiDAR) and deep-space communication (94). Depending on the application, near-infrared frequencies can be generated in LN by a wide range of approaches, including Raman lasing and difference-frequency generation (DFG), which are processes that generate one or two new EM frequencies, as well as Kerr micro resonators, SCG, and electro-optic combs, which can generate tens to hundreds of new EM frequencies in the form of optical frequency combs. For example, the generation of photons at 185 THz was demonstrated using Raman lasing in a high-Q-factor TFLN resonator (95) pumped at 192 THz. A popular approach for the generation of widely tunable EM frequencies is the use of an optical parametric oscillator (OPO). Here, two EM waves are generated, the signal and idler, which can be tuned by adjusting the phase matching condition to enable generation of frequencies between 200 and 268 THz (signal) and 110 and 178 THz (idler) (96). Such OPOs, operating either continuous-wave or pulsed, are commercially available and offer wide spectral tunability, difficult to achieve by other means. Squeezed light sources have also been demonstrated in the near infrared as fundamental building blocks for quantum sensing and computing using degenerate parametric down conversion (97–99). Applications requiring the generation of many closely spaced EM frequencies can utilize optical frequency combs (OFCs). These combs have been demonstrated at near-infrared wavelengths using high-Q-factor, dispersion-engineered Kerr micro resonators in TFLN (51, 52, 100), which use the third-order optical nonlinearity of LN or by cascaded second-order optical nonlinearities (101). Optical pump frequencies in the center of the C-band (~192 THz) are often used due to the availability of inexpensive light sources, such as narrow-linewidth and mode-locked lasers and high-power fiber amplifiers. An alternative way to generate near-infrared wavelengths is to use SCG (similar to the process outlined for visible-frequency generation), which has been used for generating optical frequency combs that cover the full near-infrared frequency range and even reach into the visible (600 THz) and mid-infrared regions (100 THz) (59, 102). Electro-optic combs provide another powerful tool to generate OFCs, with hundreds of comb lines from 183 to 192 THz spaced by 25 GHz having been demonstrated (53, 103–105). Mid-infrared frequencies (10 to 150 THz) experience low losses in LN for frequencies above ~55 THz, when the phonon absorption starts to take place (106). Mid-infrared frequencies are attractive as they can be used to excite vibrational states of molecules (107) and hence are useful sources for spectroscopic sensors in applications such as air-quality monitoring in cities or for process monitoring of chemical plants and emission from pipelines. Such frequencies have historically been generated in OPOs using bulk periodically poled LN crystals. Using this process has enabled the generation of EM waves from near-infrared frequencies down to 56 THz in the mid infrared (83, 108, 109), which is at the edge of the transparency window of LN. More recently, DFG generating mid-infrared frequencies have been demonstrated in weakly confining (110, 111) as well as TFLN waveguides , with continuous wave conversion efficiencies improving by one to two orders of magnitude. TFLN waveguides can also be used to efficiently translate NIR frequency combs to the MIR due to the large conversion bandwidth afforded by dispersion engineering (112). 15

Kerr micro resonators (82) and SCG (102, 113) have both been used to generate optical frequency combs in the mid-infrared, with the former relying on high-Q TFLN ring resonators to generate an optical frequency comb from 139 to 162 THz (100). The frequency combs generated by SCG have a wider spectral width, and can reach all the way to 60 THz (113), limited by the phonon absorption of LN. In the TFLN platform, the material absorption of the silica cladding layer underneath the LN thin film can also limit the generated frequencies, which has recently been overcome by using sapphire as a low-index cladding material (112) or undercutting the waveguide region (114). THz radiation (0.3 to 10 THz) can penetrate paper, plastics and fabric and is therefore attractive for standoff sensing and security imaging, such as at airports. LN crystals are attractive for the generation of narrow band, high intensity THz frequencies by optical rectification, which is a second-order nonlinear-optic process and can also be described by intra-pulse DFG. In this process, the spectral frequency components of ultra-short optical pump pulses interact with each other and generate new EM frequency components. This process can generate frequencies up to the difference between the blue and red edges of the pulse spectrum, so that a 10 fs pulse centered at 800 nm, for example, could generate frequencies up to ~44 THz (6.8 µm). The pump pulses that have been used for optical rectification in bulk LN crystals are in the nearinfrared region due to the availability of high-power, ultra-short optical light sources, commonly generating pulsed THz radiation with frequencies from 0.2 to 4 THz (76, 115–118), but higher frequency generation is possible as indicated above. Pulse energies up to 1.4 mJ at 0.4 THz have been demonstrated by operating bulk PPLN crystals at cryogenic temperatures and using chirped laser pulses (76), with conversion efficiencies reaching up to 0.9% (119). The generation of continuous-wave THz radiation, tunable from 1.34 to 1.70 THz and 3.06 to 3.59 THz has also been demonstrated using cascaded optical parametric processes in a singly resonant OPO using a bulk PPLN crystal for phase matching (83). Microwave frequencies (0.3 to 300 GHz) are used in applications such as 5G and 6G communication, radar, and radio astronomy. In recent years, "RF photonics" has become a widely used term to describe applications like the generation of ultra-stable microwave sources or the lowloss remoting of source and transmitter, which uses light to carry modulated signals to an antenna and subsequent conversion to microwaves. In the nonlinear optic context, such microwave frequencies can be generated directly via a DFG process in a whispering gallery mode resonator for the optical and microwave mode (120) or by using TFLN-superconductor hybrid electro-optic systems (121). However, in most RF photonics applications, LN is used to translate microwave frequencies onto an optical carrier, which can then be transmitted and manipulated in the optical domain and subsequently generate microwave frequencies using a photodetector with an appropriate bandwidth. The main mechanisms of conversion from microwave to optical frequency rely on the electro-optic and acousto-optic effect in LN (see Figs. 2B, C). Electro-optic modulators have been demonstrated in bulk, weakly confining (38), and TFLN waveguides (49), the latter being particularly attractive due to the attainable high modulation speeds and low drive voltages. The bandwidth of these electro-optic modulators can reach over 100 GHz (49, 64, 122, 123) and even approach THz levels (79). Acousto-optic devices have also been demonstrated in bulk, weakly confining (124), and TFLN waveguides (125, 126), with interaction frequencies typically from MHz to GHz levels. Such acousto-optic devices are traditionally used to induce small frequency shifts, as beam deflectors, as well as moderately fast switches and power regulators (124–127). However, more recently they have enabled quantum transduction for superconducting qubits (121, 128), thanks to the small acoustic mode volume in nanophotonic resonators fabricated in TFLN. 16

Future of the Lithium Niobate Technology LN, in all three modalities (bulk LN, weakly and tightly confining LN waveguides) is widely used for nonlinear optic, acoustic and electro-optic processes to generate and manipulate EM frequencies over a wide spectral range. Since LN’s inception, the material and manufacturing processes have matured, resulting in mostly discrete components performing well-defined functions. In the future, bulk LN crystal components will remain important for EM frequency generation across the spectrum particularly for applications that require high optical powers, such as in high-power optical parametric oscillators, free-space acousto-optic and electro-optic modulators, as well as Q-switches in laser cavities. However, for the LN waveguide platforms, we foresee a rapid acceleration of developments across two dimensions: (i) complexity and (ii) spectral breadth (Fig. 5). Complexity will transition from millimeter-scale, single components on chip, to micrometer-scale nanophotonic circuits followed by complex multilayer networks where diverse materials are heterogeneously integrated with LN and co-packaged with electronic circuits. The spectral breadth of these devices will transition from operating primarily at near-infrared frequencies to generating and manipulating EM frequencies from visible to microwave frequencies on demand.

The continued development of quality engineering and production scalability drives the performance of photonic devices and systems, both of which jointly enable new applications. Emerging applications stimulate the improvement of material processing and high-volume device production. In the near-term (next 5 years), bulk crystals and weakly confining waveguides will remain relevant platforms for the generation of EM frequencies particularly in the near infrared and visible frequency region as they offer mature fabrication and packaging processes. The high-power handling capability and the low interface loss of such commercially available products make them particularly attractive for serving as individual devices in system demonstrations, e.g. as part of self-referencing systems for optical clocks, where the LN components can be connected to other sophisticated photonic infrastructure. Furthermore, the low-loss interfacing of weakly confined waveguides is important for quantum optics applications, for example for the generation of photon pairs via spontaneous parametric down conversion, as well as frequency translation between quantum nodes and long-haul fiber networks, as well as heterogeneous intra-node conversion. Although LN is a well studied material, more work is needed to investigate the optimization of light-induced absorption changes (photochromic effect), particularly for operations at short wavelengths (129). The wider adoption of tightly confining LN waveguides is currently mainly hampered by two shortfalls: (i) the poorly understood uniformity, repeatability, and reliability of the currently commercially available starting material and (ii) the lack of low-loss interfaces to standard optical fibers. Although there has been some progress in solving the interface problem for sub-micron waveguides in LN (130, 131), they currently still require specialized fibers and high-precision multi-step e-beam lithography and etching, which may not be practical or economical for largescale fully packaged solutions. A high-volume application, such as optical short-distance data communication, will provide the commercial means to address both shortfalls and maturation of TFLN technologies. It will also drive the reduction of TFLN wafer and manufacturing costs as well as increase diversity in TFLN suppliers. This high-volume communication application is motivated by the ever-increasing need for data center and cloud infrastructure, which requires increased speed, reduced power consumption, and lower cost for next-generation communication systems. Indeed, TFLN is an ideal candidate due to its low drive voltage, compatible with CMOS electronics (49), and high bandwidth. Long-haul telecom applications may also become commercial drivers to mature the TFLN platform as the major challenge for this application is performance, where TFLN’s demonstrated data (symbol) rate of 120 GBd and beyond is highly attractive (49). These devices can be drop-in replacements for existing guided-wave LN electro-optic modulators, allowing for low threshold commercial adoption, while offering performance advantages. For LiDAR applications, the recent breakthrough of Pockels cavity lasers (132) by integrating III-V semiconductor gain sections with TFLN-based external cavities offers unrivaled frequency modulation speed and reconfigurability to integrated lasers; injection locking of a laser diode into an external LN ring modulator can also achieve fast frequency modulation, while the wavelength tuning range is limited (133). An alternative approach is the adiabatic frequency conversion using LN-based resonators (134). These approaches potentially can provide a chip-based, low-cost solution to frequency-modulated continuous-wave (FMCW) LiDAR. Furthermore, the phase 18

modulation with minimal amplitude modulation in LN can be used to tune the emission angle of the light beam, without a sensitivity degradation during the scanning. Addressing these near-term industrial applications with relatively simple, high-yield designs, will help to mature the platform and achieve the statistical understanding of issues such as device yield, uniformity, repeatability, and reliability. They will also provide better understanding of photorefractive and charging effects (e.g. bias drift in electro optical modulators) in TFLN, which can have detrimental impacts on the performance and stability of LN PICs particularly when their scale becomes large. We anticipate that in the next five years significant investments will be made in this space, building the foundation of the wide-scale adoption of tightly confining LN waveguide circuits as commercial devices. On the other hand, we foresee rapid progress in the development of TFLN at the device level for high-efficiency frequency mixing applications, such as miniaturized OPOs and OPAs using integrated PPLN-based waveguides or microcavities, where earlier commercial adoption is feasible. For these applications, several devices can be manufactured on the same chip with parameter sweeps to account for uniformity and fabrication-tolerance issues, such that the best devices can be isolated and packaged (i.e. ‘hand-picked’). These devices will be of high value as they can provide ultra-broadband, ultra-high parametric gains reaching an octave using microwatt average powers or femtojoule-level pump energies (66, 135). The same approach can be used to develop widely tunable, on-chip oscillators for challenging spectral regimes, such as the visible and mid-infrared for addressing atomic and molecular systems used for metrology and spectroscopy, or efficient mm-wave/THz generation for wireless communication and sensing. Along this track of integrated OPOs and OPAs, squeezed-state generation for continuous-variable quantum computing, quantum random number generation, and quadratic dissipative soliton formation operating in a strongly nonlinear regime can be implemented. One essential trend we expect to happen in the next 5 years is the development of heterogeneous integration on the TFLN platform. Based on wafer-bonding, LN waveguides will be interfaced with different materials at the wafer scale, among which the integration of III-V semiconductor materials as laser sources, amplifiers and detectors will be crucial. Another trend that we expect will be developed is the co-packaging of photonic and electronic circuits, which is crucial for the control and monitoring of large-scale LN circuits. While these initial efforts will be mainly at the research level, they will pave the way for a future wafer-scale heterogeneous platform with full control, monitor, and communication functionality. In the mid-term (5 to 10 years), we anticipate that bulk crystals and weakly confining waveguides will continue being used as individual components for low-volume, price-sensitive visible to midinfrared frequency applications. Emerging applications such as 6G or THz sensing will benefit from the high power-handling capability of these devices for efficient microwave/THz generation. However, the relatively high material losses at the THz spectral region (136, 137) can be detrimental for the device performance, requiring the generation of the THz radiation near the LNair interface (76, 138). On the other hand, we predict that improved low-loss optical interfaces to sub-micron waveguides and fabrication processes developed for high-volume data communication applications, will result in an increased uptake of tightly confining TFLN waveguide circuits for near-infrared frequency applications. One of the challenges to be overcome in the mid-term is the reproducibility of nanophotonic components. For example, the need for a network of virtually identical degenerate OPOs with their high sensitivity of the designed nonlinear process imposes stringent requirements on the fabrication tolerance (waveguide dimensions) and wafer uniformity (film thickness, both 19

within a single wafer as well as wafer-to-wafer, and defect control). Active tuning and post processing might hold solutions to these challenges. Hence, similar to silicon photonic circuits, one could rely on thermal heaters for tuning, which is not attractive for energy efficiency reasons, or the integration of advanced refractive index tunable materials (139). Where available, noncritical designs should be implemented to increase dimensional tolerances (140, 141). This may be achievable when expanding the design space by considering sophisticated cladding structures, such as multilayer stacks. Along with the improved fabrication processes and reproducibility of nanophotonic components, we anticipate that data communication applications will push forward the co-packaging of photonic and electronic circuits and the heterogeneous integration of active materials for near infrared light sources and detectors (57), transferring the fabrication processes that have been developed for silicon photonic circuits (142) to the TFLN platform. The integration of lasers and detectors will also be highly appealing to numerous applications that demand large-scale, fully integrated PICs, such as analog microwave photonics, LiDAR, and artificial intelligence (AI). Although at this stage the scalability of optical phase arrays (OPA) or optical neural network (ONN) might not be as vast as that available in silicon photonics, these applications will benefit immensely from the TFLN technology because of the significantly reduced drive voltage, increased bandwidth, and low insertion loss. Additionally, it is plausible to anticipate complex and high-performance analog systems including 6G millimeter-wave systems, radio-frequency spectrum analyzers, and photonic analog-to-digital converters to be realized on TFLN. Importantly, these applications can benefit from the reduced cost, utilizing the matured, nearinfrared components, making the dream of wide adoption of photonic-based, wideband-radiofrequency systems a reality. In parallel, we envision a more complex system that operates from microwave down to visible wavelengths and is enabled by further integration of electro-optic, acousto-optic and all-optical signal-processing components. One of the foreseeable areas where LN can make a revolutionary impact is the optical frequency comb (OFC) technology. As a material with both second-order and Kerr nonlinearity along with high-bandwidth modulators, LN is extremely suitable for realizing integrated self-referenced optical frequency combs on a single nanophotonic platform with laser sources, amplifiers and high-speed photodetectors heterogeneously integrated, where ultraefficient nonlinear broadening and SHG are readily accessible functions. In addition, the realization of cascading a series of low-switching-voltage, high-bandwidth electro-optic modulators would promise, among many other applications, frequency-agile, electro-optic frequency combs with significant versatility of center wavelength and repetition rate as well as direct synthesis of femtosecond-class pulse sources without modelocking (143). Such sources naturally operate at repetition rates compatible with on-chip resonators and thus open a wide range of opportunities in highly efficient nonlinear frequency conversion using pulsed pumping schemes. Other mid-term applications might involve dynamic beamforming based on electro- or acoustooptic devices, essential for LiDAR, augmented and virtual reality displays as well as trapped-ion quantum computing systems. The major challenge in the mid-term is to establish novel system architectures which can utilize different light-matter interactions (between microwave, mechanical and optical sub-components) without paying a significant price in performance and manufacturing complexity. In the long-term (10 years and beyond), TFLN will be based on a large-scale (beyond 200-mm wafer) foundry process with diverse heterogeneously integrated materials and co-packaged electronic circuits. Such a platform will be an excellent choice for scaling up optical networking 20

schemes where large arrays of classical or quantum light sources or processing units need to be coupled, often in a programmable way. This will enable fundamentally novel applications such as fully integrated LiDAR and ONNs, quantum computing, fully integrated frequency synthesizers, massive radio-frequency signal processing networks and advanced sensors. For example, photonic AI accelerators require an array of low-energy-cost electro-optic modulators and nonlinear photonic activation components. In addition to neural networks, other photonic architectures for optical computing such as coherent Ising machines also demand a large number of photonic-based artificial spins connected by a reconfigurable all-to-all coupling matrix. Regardless of whether a time or spatially multiplexed approach is used, thin-film LN devices are critical to realize photonic spins based on degenerate OPOs as well as spin-spin coupling based on delay lines with amplitude and phase modulators or a Mach-Zehnder interferometer mesh. Topological studies in synthetic dimensions could also be interesting in such coupled resonator networks. A grand challenge in quantum engineering is to achieve extreme optical nonlinearity (ideally at the single-photon level) where both improvement of material processing and nanoengineering are required. We do see benefits from gradual improvements of the optical nonlinearity using LN in future quantum PICs. This will benefit quantum photonic systems where spatially multiplexed SPDC sources with fast feedback optical switches are required for the implementation of neardeterministic single-photon sources, or large scale continuous-variable computation where high quality squeezing states are required. Regarding quantum communication networks and all-optical signal processing, we envision that spectro-temporal shaping and quantum transduction techniques will have to be employed to overcome the inhomogeneity of quantum emitters or to bridge the spectral difference between heterogeneous quantum systems. Furthermore, LN’s fast modulation capability with low loss based on the EO effect will be essential in almost all the quantum systems to increase the processing speed and reduce the system losses. Fortunately, LN is an all-around high-performance optical material to generate and manipulate EM frequencies on demand over a spectral range that covers nearly 5 orders of magnitude with a proven history of reliability, and an ever-growing diverse portfolio of near- to long-term applications based on its nanophotonic platform. It is important to note that we anticipate that PICs that make use of TFLN require heterogeneous integration to enable the integration of the attractive material properties of LN with efficient light sources and detectors. This may be realized by heterogeneously integrating such PIC elements on the TFLN waveguide platform (57) or by integrating thin-films of LN onto other PIC material platforms (144). Independent of its modality (bulk LN, weakly or tightly confining LN waveguides) or indeed integration method, LN is in a great position to overcome the outlined challenges to become the material platform of choice to unlock the EM spectrum and continue to revolutionize optical science for years to come.
