Additive engineering for robust interphases to stabilize high-Ni layered structures at ultra-high voltage of 4.8 V Sha Tan1,8, Zulipiya Shadike 1,8, Jizhou Li 2,8, Xuelong Wang1, Yang Yang 3, Ruoqian Lin 1, Arthur Cresce4, Jiangtao Hu 5, Adrian Hunt3, Iradwikanari Waluyo 3, Lu Ma3, Federico Monaco 6, Peter Cloetens 6, Jie Xiao 5,7, Yijin Liu 2 ✉, Xiao-Qing Yang 1 ✉, Kang Xu 4 ✉ and Enyuan Hu 1 ✉ Nickel-rich layered cathode materials promise high energy density for next-generation batteries when coupled with lithium metal anodes. However, the practical capacities accessible are far less than the theoretical values due to their structural instability during cycling, especially when charged at high voltages. Here we demonstrate that stable cycling with an ultra-high cut-off voltage of 4.8 V can be realized by using an appropriate amount of lithium difluorophosphate in a common commercial electrolyte. The Li||LiNi0.76Mn0.14Co0.10O2 cell retains 97% of the initial capacity (235 mAh g–1) after 200 cycles. The cycling stability is ascribed to the robust interphase on the cathode. It is formed by lithium difluorophosphate decomposition, which is facilitated by the catalytic effect of transition metals. The decomposition products (Li3PO4 and LiF) form a protective interphase. This suppresses transition metal dissolution and cathode surface reconstruction. It also facilitates uniform Li distribution within the cathode, effectively mitigating the strain and crack formation.

A

mong numerous cathode candidates for next-generation battery concepts, the nickel-rich layered material LiNixMnyCo1–x–yO2 (NMC) stands out because of its high theoretical capacity (~280 mAh g−1), high working voltage (~4 V versus Li+/Li) and relatively low cost. Unfortunately, serious capacity fading occurs for NMC when it is subjected to high-voltage charging (above 4.2 V versus Li+/Li). Tremendous efforts have been devoted to understanding the capacity degradation. One mechanism of this fade is that the surface of NMC is reconstructed at high voltages through a phase transition from the layered to spinel/rock-salt structure, which is accompanied by oxygen loss1–6. The reconstructed phase, which is believed to be a poor lithium-ion (Li+) conductor, slows the ion transport at the cathode surface, increases the overall cell impedance and decreases the practical capacity. Another origin of capacity fade was identified as the dissolution of the transition metal (TM). The dissolved TM could migrate through the electrolyte and eventually, deposit onto the anode surface, which changes both the surface composition and structure of the cathode and disrupts the solid–electrolyte interphase (SEI) on the anode. Yet another interpretation for the capacity decay is based on the heterogeneity of Li distribution in the NMC lattice, leading to the formation of cracks7–9. Although diversified approaches such as surface protection10, bulk doping11 and microstructure engineering12,13 have been explored with varying degrees of success, obtaining stable cycling of a nickel-rich layered cathode at ultra-high voltage (>4.5 V) remains very challenging. The challenge for high-voltage operation not only comes from the cathode, but also from the electrolyte. For a typical commercial

electrolyte such as 1 M LiPF6 in ethylene carbonate and ethyl methyl carbonate (EC/EMC; 3:7 by weight), its voltage window is affected by both the intrinsic electrochemical stability and chemical reactions with the electrode. For example, although the electrochemical oxidation potential of the EC/EMC mixture on an inert metal surface (that is, Pt) can be as high as ~5.5 V, it could be easily oxidized at a much lower voltage when catalysed by the TM elements in cathodes. As a result, commercial EC/EMC electrolytes can maintain stability only to ~4.3 V in practical cells. At voltages higher than 4.3 V, oxygen molecules and oxygen radicals can be released from the surface of the delithiated cathode and react with the electrolyte. Therefore, commercial electrolytes fail to support high-voltage lithium-ion battery operations. Using a sacrificial additive is an efficient approach for improving the electrochemical performance at high-voltage operating conditions. A highly protective cathode–electrolyte interphase (CEI) can inhibit the electrolyte decomposition and minimize cathode degradation. For example, lithium difluoro(oxalato)borate (LiDFOB) was shown to generate a CEI that mitigates electrolyte oxidation and TM dissolution from LiNi0.7Mn0.15Co0.15O2 cathodes at 4.3 V (ref. 14). However, for the ultra-high-voltage operating region (>4.6 V), it has been quite difficult to identify additives that can address the multiple challenges simultaneously. Lithium difluorophosphate (LiDFP) was initially proposed as an electrolyte additive to improve the rate capability of graphite||NMC cells15 and was found to improve the low-temperature performance of graphite||NMC cells16. Such behaviours were ascribed to the decreased interphasial impedance upon the addition of LiDFP

It was believed that LiDFP generated a compact and thin CEI rich in fluorophosphates, and it was proposed that the formed Li3PO4 inhibited the detrimental LiPF6 decomposition due to the ‘common ion effect’20. The electrochemical stability of LiDFP was found to be dependent on the specific working electrode used17,20, which implies the possible catalytic effect of TM in the cathode. In this work, we choose LiDFP as the additive for high-voltage protection on NMC and explore its functionality at a high voltage of 4.8 V (versus Li metal). Through combined theoretical and experimental efforts, we clarify the underlying mechanism of LiDFP decomposition in forming a stable interphase. We find that such an interphase suppresses TM dissolution, inhibits cathode surface reconstruction and facilitates uniform Li distribution within the cathode, effectively mitigating the strain and crack formation.

Ultra-high-voltage protection by LiDFP additive

To investigate the effect of LiDFP in protecting Ni-rich LiNi0.76Mn0.14Co0.10O2 (NMC76) at high voltages, the cycling

behaviours of NMC76 in a baseline electrolyte and electrolytes with 1 wt% or 2 wt% LiDFP were compared at different cut-off voltages (that is, 4.4 V, 4.6 V and 4.8 V). At the lowest charging cut-off voltage (4.4 V; Supplementary Fig. 1), a negligible difference was observed in the electrochemical performances of these three electrolytes, with similar capacity retention (above 90%) after 200 cycles. Increasing the cut-off voltage renders a higher delivered capacity at the first cycle, that is, ~222 mAh g−1 at 4.6 V and ~230 mAh g−1 at 4.8 V (Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3). However, serious deterioration arises in the capacity retention (Supplementary Fig. 2 and Fig. 1). When charged to 4.6 V and 4.8 V, only 83.78% and 77.27% of the initial capacity are left, respectively, after 200 cycles for the baseline electrolyte. This deteriorated performance suggests incessant electrolyte decomposition in the highly oxidative environments at high voltage21,22, the oxidation of the interphase or degradation of the cathode itself during high-voltage cycling. By strong contrast, cells using electrolyte with an additive of LiDFP (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 3) demonstrate a much more stable capacity retention during long-term high-voltage cycling.

In particular, the cell using 1% LiDFP electrolyte maintained a high discharge capacity of 228.02 mAh g−1 (97.64%) even after 200 cycles at 4.8 V, suggesting that a new interphase is likely formed by adding LiDFP as an additive that effectively prevents continuous electrolyte decomposition and cathode degradation. Note that the electrochemical data is highly reproducible (Supplementary Fig. 4), and the 0.5% LiDFP additive showed inferior electrochemical performance compared to 1% LiDFP (Supplementary Fig. 5). The reasons for the capacity decay at high voltage could be the impedance hike due to incessant electrolyte decomposition, an unstable SEI, the formation of rock-salt species on the cathode surface or the combination of all of these reasons. To evaluate the impedance resulting from different electrolytes at high voltage, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was performed, which indeed reveals the impedance build-up from the initial cycle to the tenth cycle (Supplementary Fig. 6). The effect of the electrolyte on impedance becomes more pronounced after 200 cycles, where the LiDFP presence effectively suppresses impedance growth (Fig. 1b). Quantitative analysis of EIS by fitting the equivalent circuit model (Fig. 1c) is summarized in Fig. 1d. At 1% LiDFP, the extremely small charge transfer resistance (Rct) indicates the formation of a highly conductive interphase, enabling facile charge transfer reactions on the interphase even after long-term cycling. When the additive amount is increased to 2%, both surface film resistance (Rsf ) and Rct are increased, but the bulk electrolyte resistance (Rb) is mostly the same, suggesting that the worse performance in excessive additive is caused by interphasial resistance. The origin of this optimum LiDFP presence (Fig. 1a) might stem from the fact that it produces both LiF and HF simultaneously. While LiF is generally regarded as a desired interphase component, excessive LiF may make the interphase more resistive, while HF is known to be detrimental as it can attack the cathode surface and damage the interphase23–25. Slow kinetics due to impedance build-up could also be reflected by voltage polarization. The rapid increase of charge voltage and decrease of discharge voltage when using baseline electrolyte suggest a rapid growth of overpotential (Supplementary Fig. 7). By contrast, LiDFP-containing electrolyte appears to have effectively stabilized the average charge/ discharge voltage, with the best performance around 1% LiDFP.

A robust interphase that prevents cathode surface reconstruction

Linear sweep voltammetry results indicate LiDFP decomposition prior to that of electrolyte solvents and its participation in interphase formation (Supplementary Fig. 8). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was conducted to analyse the chemical composition of the CEI on NMC76 after 200 cycles. The P 2p spectra of the cathode recovered from the LiDFP-containing electrolyte show two pairs of peaks (Fig. 2a), corresponding to LixPFyOz and Li3PO4 (refs. 26,27), which are decomposition products from LiPF6 and LiDFP, respectively. The presence of ionic conductive Li3PO4 could lead to a fast ionic transport and low interphase impedance28. In addition, the F 1s spectra also indicate a higher LiF content in CEI formed from the LiDFP-containing electrolyte. These XPS results show that the CEI on NMC76 formed by LiDFP is Li3PO4 containing and LiF rich, agreeing well with theoretical calculations, which are discussed later. Although there are multiple reports on the LiDFP-induced improvement in high-voltage cycling performance for Ni-containing cathode materials, there has not been a consensus regarding its working mechanism20,29–31. Our density functional theory (DFT) study (Supplementary Notes) shows that the Ni-rich NMC surface provides Ni–O and Mn–O sites that favour PO2F adsorption and have catalytic effects on its hydrolysis decomposition. This process generates decomposition products *H2PO3 and *F, which are very likely the precursors for interphase layer components, Li3PO4 and LiF, respectively. The obtained mechanism helps explain why 1% LiDFP electrolyte

gives the best performance. As indicated in Fig. 2b, Li3PO4, LiF and HF can all be generated during the LiDFP decomposition. While Li3PO4 and LiF have generally been regarded as good interphase components, HF is not, because it attacks the NMC surface and leads to a cascade of detrimental effects. Therefore, the concentration of LiDFP additive needs to be optimized to balance the positive (Li3PO4 and LiF) and negative (HF) effects. Another reason is related to the limited solubility of LiDFP. LiDFP has been reported to have a solubility of ~3% in carbonate ester32. Therefore, excessive LiDFP in the electrolyte can negatively affect the transport property. To reveal the surface structure evolution in the NMC76 cathode during cycling, high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) imaging was obtained for the cycled samples (Fig. 3a). In baseline electrolyte, an approximately 2 to 3-nm-thick disordered rock-salt phase can be seen on the cathode surface, suggesting that the interfacial section of NMC76 undergoes a phase transition due to the interaction between NMC76 and electrolyte. By contrast, for the sample cycled with the presence of LiDFP, a layered structure with a clean surface is maintained without any detectable rock-salt phase under identical cycling conditions, indicating that the surface reconstruction has been effectively suppressed. Differing from HAADF-STEM and XPS, which provide information about the shallow surface (~2 nm), soft X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS), when used in total electron yield mode, probes deeper (~10 nm) structures and provides complementary information. The peak at ~534 eV of O K-edge spectra (Supplementary Fig. 9) for a pristine electrode is attributed to Li2CO3, which is a native passivation film common to all oxide-based surfaces. After electrochemical cycling, this peak disappeared as Li2CO3 was consumed during the cycling. A new species at ~532 eV appeared after NMC76 cycled in baseline electrolyte, which intensified after 200 cycles, indicating the emergence and growth of the rock-salt phase. TM L-edge spectra were collected for all three TM cations Mn, Co and Ni (Fig. 3b). A noticeable feature at low photon energy of the Mn L3 edge appeared after only one cycle in baseline electrolyte, and its intensity grew after 200 cycles. This feature, attributed to Mn2+ and Mn3+, reflects the reduction of Mn on the NMC76 electrode surface almost immediately in electrolyte, and the reduction continued during cycling. Meanwhile, the shoulder at ~778 eV in Co L3-edge spectra that is associated with Co2+ was absent initially but clearly intensified after 200 cycles, indicating that the reduction of Co proceeded in the long term. By striking contrast, both Mn and Co spectra for the cathode recovered from the LiDFP-containing electrolyte show little changes, if any, due to the excellent stability of a NMC76 surface that is well protected by the LiDFP-derived interphase. For the Ni L3 edge, spectra for both baseline and LiDFP-containing electrolytes display little changes during cycling, probably because once the surface is dominated by divalent nickel, it is difficult to be further reduced. On the other hand, TM L-edge spectra collected in partial fluorescence yield mode (Supplementary Fig. 10), which is more bulk sensitive (~100 nm) rather than interfacial, detected no reduced species, indicating that the reductions of these TMs were mainly confined to the surface of NMC only.

Suppressing TM dissolution

In addition to the cathode surface degradation at high voltage, TM dissolution at the cathode and the subsequent migration through the electrolyte as well as deposition at the anode surface also contribute to the overall electrochemical performance degradation. This phenomenon is accelerated when the charging voltage is increased. X-ray fluorescence (XRF) mapping was used to study the effectiveness of the LiDFP-derived interphase on suppressing the TM dissolution. As the fluorescence intensity can be directly related to the TM concentration, the deposited TM on the lithium anode surface can be mapped quantitatively (Fig. 4a).

Several interesting findings are noted. First, there are substantial inhomogeneities across the electrode, which applies to electrodes harvested from both baseline and LiDFP-containing electrolytes. The TM concentration in one area can be higher than that in another area by an order of magnitude. Note that Eldesoky et al.33 showed the inhomogeneous Fe deposition in graphite||LiFePO4, suggesting that inhomogeneous TM deposition is prevalent and emphasizing the importance of electrode-level characterization to avoid arbitrariness arising from the point probe. The inhomogeneity might be caused by several factors, including non-uniformity in pressure, current density and the anode surface morphology34–36. In the case of using lithium metal as the anode, the Li surface can be particularly non-uniform because of repeated Li stripping/plating during cycling. This may result in more serious TM deposition inhomogeneity compared with the case where graphite is used as the anode33. Second, among all three TMs, nickel experiences the most severe dissolution. This is completely different from the widely accepted argument that manganese is the most dissolved element24,37. One possible reason for such a difference is that the entire electrode was evaluated in this work, while previous work usually focuses on a randomly selected spot that may not be representative, considering the high inhomogeneities of the TM deposition across the electrode. In fact, nickel being the most dissolved element should not be surprising because it has the highest concentration in the pristine material, and nickel has been suggested to be more reactive towards the solvent molecules than cobalt and manganese38,39. Third, LiDFP can very effectively suppress TM dissolution (Fig. 4b). On average, the deposited TM is decreased by ~70% when

LiDFP is present. The suppressed TM deposition on the Li anode is likely attributed to the suppression of TM dissolution from the cathode into the electrolyte, which is suggested by the cleaner separator when using the electrolyte containing 1% LiDFP (Supplementary Fig. 11). Based on the XRF results, the deposited Ni amount on the Li anode is around 4.26 μg cm–2 (baseline electrolyte). If we assume that the amount of Ni dissolved in the electrolyte is on the same scale, then the Ni lost through dissolution accounts for only a small fraction (<0.1%) of what is in the active NMC material. Therefore, the impact of TM dissolution on the accessible capacity through the active material lost is minimal. On the other hand, TM dissolution may influence the surface composition of the cathode. XRF results indicate that among the three TMs, Co is relatively less deposited on the Li anode if the deposited amount is normalized by the stoichiometry in the bulk. If we assume that Co is also relatively less dissolved in the electrolyte, such TM dissolution implies that the cathode surface will gradually become more Co rich during cycling. A direct consequence of this preferential dissolution would be the enrichment of Co at the cathode surface, which benefits the cathode performance in terms of both electronic conductivity and a more stabilized layer structure against cation mixing. The quantitative analysis of the TM dissolution can serve as a good indicator for the effectiveness of cathode surface protection by the CEI. In other words, the less the TM dissolution, the better protection provided by the CEI. The negative impact of TM dissolution on capacity retention is not through the active material lost, but the degradation at the surface (rock-salt phase). Since the deposited TM needs to migrate through the electrolyte, it would be interesting to study TM dissolution in the electrolyte.

However, a direct measurement on the electrolyte is quite challenging because it is not easy to isolate the electrolyte from other components in the cycled cell; thus, we did our measurements on the separator harvested from cycled cells for the K-edge XAS of Ni, Co and Mn (Supplementary Fig. 12). As the data are not normalized, the height of the edge jump directly relates to the relative concentration of TM in the sample. The data clearly show that Ni has a relatively higher concentration than Co and Mn in the electrolyte, the same as on the Li metal anode. We further determined the TM valences at various stages of the TM cross-talk (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 13). One interesting observation is that the TM valence states in electrolyte and at the surface of the Li metal anode are closely related to their valance states on the cathode surface. When the cathode surface is well protected by a stable interphase, as in the case of 1% LiDFP, the reduction of TM valence states on the cathode

surface after cycling is negligible (Fig. 3b), and the reduction of the corresponding valence states in the electrolyte and on the lithium metal anode are also not serious. By contrast, for the unprotected cathode surface without LiDFP additive, the reductions of TM at the cathode surface are quite substantial, and the same is true for their valance states in the electrolyte and at the Li metal anode. Such a complete picture of TM dissolution is rarely given in the literature.

With this large dataset, we carried out machine-learning-assisted statistical analysis to reveal the impact of LiDFP on the bulk heterogeneity at the whole electrode scale with nano-scale resolution. The instance-aware identification and segmentation of NMC76 particles are automatically accomplished using our machine-learning model40. Building upon the accurate particle-identification result (Fig. 5a), we quantified the characteristics of every single NMC76 particle in its size, sphericity, SOC, SOC variation and anisotropic polarization (Supplementary Fig. 15). We clarify here that the SOC variation and the anisotropic polarization both quantify the degree of mesoscale charge heterogeneity within individual particles1 but with different emphases, reflecting different phenomena that could be associated with different degradation mechanisms. The SOC variation is a quantification of the particle heterogeneity that is not pattern specific, associating with a combined effect of charge heterogeneity, domain deactivation, chemo-mechanical

interplay and local phase transformation. On the other hand, the anisotropic polarization is pattern specific and is more relevant to the non-uniform electronic and/or ionic conductivities that result from the partial particle detachment and surface passivation, respectively. To elucidate the effect of particle size and shape dependence in their respective responses to the LiDFP-modulated surface chemistry, we divided the identified particles into four groups (small and large volume, low and high sphericity; Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 16). The relative values of SOC variation and anisotropic polarization for these four groups are compared to evaluate the impact of LiDFP presence (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 17). As indicated by the red arrows (pointing to lower left) in Fig. 5c, both the SOC variation and anisotropic polarization decrease with the presence of LiDFP. This observation holds true for all four groups, but the significance varies, as indicated by the different lengths of the red arrows. Our comparison suggests that the LiDFP-induced suppression of both the SOC variation and the anisotropic polarization is more pronounced in the particles with smaller volume and with higher sphericity. While the size dependence is consistent with the conventional wisdom, the sphericity dependence is somewhat unanticipated. The particles with low sphericity often exhibit a high

surface-area-to-volume ratio, and, therefore, the impact of surface chemistry was anticipated to be more notable, contradicting our experimental observation. We attribute this to the fact that irregularly shaped particles are more likely to experience non-uniform contact with the carbon and binder domain on their surfaces, leading to micro-morphology-related charge/redox heterogeneities that could persist even with the LiDFP presence. This interpretation is supported by the fact that, when we focus on the group of

particles with low sphericity, the LiDFP additive’s impact on the anisotropic polarization is more serious than on the SOC variation. To this end, our machine-learning-assisted statistical analysis has revealed the key morphological features that are most prone to the influence by the interphase. The functionalities of the CEI towards bulk properties has been observed in previous literature studies. For example, Li et al. used HAADF-STEM to show that electrolyte additives inhibited the growth of a rock-salt layer in cycled graphite||LiNi0.8Mn0.1Co0.1O2 cells41. Lu et al.42 showed that a good CEI can suppress TM dissolution. While these localized high-resolution experimental probes could offer valuable insights, our results demonstrate a statistical significance and construct an in-depth mechanistic understanding on how the interphase could influence the bulk properties of the cathode, including crack formation. Through machine-learning-assisted tomography work, we not only show the correlation between the interphase and bulk, but also quantify such an effect by analysing a large ensemble of particles (>2,000). Our work shows that small-sized and spherically shaped particles are most sensitive to such interphase–bulk correlation. Such a finding may provide guidance on material synthesis and help to optimize the NMC76 particle morphology to leverage the benefit of interphase engineering. The positive impact on bulk properties brought by interphase engineering with LiDFP, as quantitatively revealed by our imaging data, is quite interesting and surprising. Such impact is further confirmed by other bulk techniques like X-ray diffraction, hard XAS and X-ray tomography cross-sections. X-ray diffraction patterns of the cathodes recovered from both baseline and LiDFP-containing electrolytes can be fitted well using the R 3̄m layered structure after being charged to the voltage range of 2.8–4.8 V for 200 cycles (Fig. 6a). Our previous work studied the integrity of the NMC76 structure and showed that oxygen release can take place in the surface region, while the NMC76 bulk structure is stable against oxygen release even upon deep delithiation4,43. The lattice parameters a and c for the NMC76 cycled in the LiDFP-containing electrolyte at the discharged state deviate less from the values of the pristine material than from the values of those cycled in baseline electrolyte (Fig. 6b), suggesting that more lithium is restored in the NMC76 cycled in the additive-containing electrolyte than that in the baseline electrolyte44. We also characterized materials using another bulk-sensitive technique, hard XAS (Supplementary Fig. 18), but did not see obvious differences between the two cases, presumably due to the lower sensitivity of the latter technique in probing the average SOC. Cracks forming in the bulk cathode particles is the well-known degradation mechanism for high-voltage cycling. X-ray tomography cross-section results (Fig. 6c–e) confirm that much fewer cracks were formed and much fewer secondary particles were broken up for the NMC76 cycled in LiDFP-containing electrolyte. These statistically reliable results collected on a large number of particles provide much more convincing evidence for the better structural stability at the particle level, benefitting from the LiDFP-derived interphase.

Conclusions

While it is well accepted that NMC faces both bulk and interphasial instabilities at ultra-high voltages, we show here that these two issues can be solved simultaneously. Furthermore, compared to conventional mitigation strategies such as single-crystal synthesis and/or surface coating, our ionic additive LiDFP in the electrolyte has the benefits of involving inexpensive materials and simple processing. When cycled between 2.8 V and 4.8 V, the polycrystalline NMC76 cathode can deliver an initial capacity as high as 235 mAh g–1 and have a very impressive 97% capacity retention after 200 cycles if 1% LiDFP is used. The decomposition of LiDFP catalysed by the Ni-rich NMC surface promotes the formation of a protective CEI that is composed of Li3PO4 and LiF. Therefore, LiDFP

has multiple functionalities including suppressing surface reconstruction, mitigating TM dissolution and contributing chemical ingredients to form a conductive interphase. Bulk characterizations including machine-learning-assisted tomography at the electrode level indicate that the bulk, especially those small-sized and spherically shaped particles, are very sensitive to the LiDFP additive in a positive way. This is likely due to the faster lithium-ion transport across the better interphase formed by LiDFP that regulates a more uniform lithium distribution within the bulk particles, reducing the strain and the consequent crack formation. We also show that nickel experiences the most severe dissolution among three TMs. Future development of NMC or other layer-structured materials could focus on the stabilization of Ni as well as homogenizing the particle morphology by using new additives.

Methods

Materials. The NMC76 slurry was prepared by mixing 80 wt% LiNi0.76Mn0.14Co0.10O2, 10 wt% Super P carbon and 10 wt% polyvinylidene fluoride in N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone solvent, and then coated onto Al foil. Before further treatment, the electrode was dried inside a vacuum oven at ~120 °C overnight. Afterward, the electrode was pouched into a half-inch disc with a material loading of ~4–5 mg per electrode. The baseline electrolyte was composed of 1 M LiPF6 in EC/EMC (3:7, wt%). Then 1 wt% LiDFP and 2 wt% LiDFP were added into baseline electrolyte to obtain the additive-containing electrolytes. All the electrolytes were prepared inside an argon-filled glove box with controlled oxygen and water content. Electrochemical measurements. The cycling performance of batteries was tested on a Neware battery tester. Li || NMC76 2032-type coin cells were prepared inside an Ar-filled glove box by using a Li chip, as-prepared NMC76 electrode, a polypropylene Celgard 3501 separator and 70 µl as-prepared electrolytes. The Li || NMC76 cells were charged to 4.4 V and discharged to 2.8 V at C/10 rate in the initial two cycles for activation. After that, the cells were cycled at C/3 rate for the subsequent cycles. Here 1C means fully charge or discharge the cell in one hour. Following the same protocol, the Li || NMC76 cells were cycled in the voltage range of 2.8–4.6 V and 2.8–4.8 V as well. For the XRF mapping characterization, the Li || NMC76 cells were cycled at 1 C for 100 cycles after two formation cycles within 2.8–4.8 V. The EIS of the cycled Li || NMC76 cells was performed on the Biologic SAS impedance analyser at open circuit voltage with a 10 mV amplitude within the frequency range of ~1 MHz–1 mHz. Quantitative analysis of EIS by fitting the equivalent circuit model yields Rb, Rsf and Rct. Characterizations. Cycled Li anode and NMC76 electrodes were harvested from the disassembled cells for post-mortem characterizations. Before measurements, the NMC76 electrodes were washed in EMC and dried under vacuum to remove residual electrolytes. XPS of the cycled NMC electrodes was performed using a Kα X-ray source in a PHI Versaprobe 3 instrument with a spot size of 100 μm and a spot energy of 25 W. Samples were loaded into a sealed container in an Ar-filled glove box with a moisture content of less than 1 ppm H2O and an O2 content of 1 ppm. Samples were then transferred directly to the XPS sample chamber such that they were never exposed to the outside environment. For HAADF-STEM measurement, cycled NMC particles were dispersed in EMC solution homogeneously, and then deposited on a copper grid coated with a lacey carbon film. The as-prepared sample was then characterized on an FEI Talos F200X (200 keV) at the Center for Functional Nanomaterial at Brookhaven National Laboratory for imaging. Synchrotron-based powder X-ray diffraction of cycled NMC76 powder was collected at beamline 28-ID-2 of the National Synchrotron Light Source (NSLS) II at Brookhaven National Laboratory, using a photon wavelength of 0.18456 Å. The obtained X-ray diffraction data were integrated using Fit2D software and further refined using Rietveld refinement. For the TM K-edge hard XAS characterization, the harvested cycled NMC76 electrodes were sealed inside Kapton tapes and tested at the 7-BM QAS beamline of NSLS II. The separator was well immersed in the electrolyte to faithfully reflect the situation of TM dissolution in the electrolyte. The soft XAS measurements for obtaining O K-edge and TM L-edge information were performed in both total electron yield and partial fluorescence yield modes at the IOS beamline of NSLS II. The collected hard XAS and soft XAS data were analysed using the ATHENA software package. For characterizing the TM dissolution on the Li metal anode, the cycled Li metal anodes were sealed between Kapton tape and Mylar film. Then the sample was secured in an Ar-filled pouch to avoid side reactions and electrode contamination by oxygen and moisture during transportation. Synchrotron-based XRF mapping of lithium anodes recovered from the baseline and LiDFP-containing electrolytes after 90 cycles were performed at the SRX 5-ID beamline of NSLS II. The experiments were done at an energy of 8.5 keV, with a pixel size of 30 μm and a dwell time of 0.5 s. Fluorescence signals were collected with a three-element Vortex ME3 (Hitachi) silicon drift detector.

Page 10

Spectra were fitted and quantitatively calibrated with the freeware PyMCA45 to yield elemental maps in the unit of areal mass density. The X-ray phase contrast nano-tomography experiments were conducted at the ID16A-NI nano-imaging beamline at the European Synchrotron Radiation Facility in Grenoble, France. This beamline features a high-energy hard X-ray nano-probe46, delivering a focus down to ~20 nm with a brilliant photon flux (up to 1012 photons per second with high monochromaticity, ΔE/E ≈ 1%, where E is the X-ray energy and ΔE is the X-ray energy bandwidth). The nano-focus is achieved by two pairs of multilayer-coated Kirkpatrick–Baez optics working at 33.6 keV. For every tomography scan, 1,500 projections were acquired with 0.2 s exposure time. One complete phase contrast nano-tomography scan constitutes tomograms at four different focus-to-sample distances. These tomograms were subsequently used for phase retrieval to generate two-dimensional (2D) phase maps47. The 2D phase maps retrieved from all angular projections were then used as input for a tomographic reconstruction based on the filtered back projection algorithm method (European Synchrotron Radiation Facility PyHST software package)48. The cathodes used were collected from the cell at the fully discharged state to ensure a reasonable approximation for one-to-one mapping from electron density to SOC. Machine-learning model. The technical details of our machine-learning model and its performance evaluation for particle identification and segmentation can be found in our previous work40. Specifically, a state-of-the-art mask regional convolutional neural network is utilized and trained to derive useful features for particle detection. The particle’s external boundary cannot be easily defined using the conventional pixel-intensity-based approach. This is particularly the case when the boundary is not clear due to limited signal-to-noise ratio in the image. Compared with the conventional segmentation approach, our machine-learning model demonstrated greatly improved performance, especially for identifying cracked particles, which would otherwise be viewed as many smaller, irregularly shaped particles. Overall, the trained model achieved a precision and sensitivity of 92% and 86%, respectively, on the validation dataset. The source code of our development has been released to the public at https://github.com/YijinLiu-Lab/ LIBNet. The tomography data used in this study were acquired using the same experimental configurations and, therefore, exhibit similar image characteristics as the data used in ref. 40. The segmentation results were manually inspected to ensure the machine-learning model correctly identified NMC particles. In total, there are 2,245 and 2,349 NMC particles identified in the imaged electrodes, which were cycled with baseline and LiDFP-containing electrolytes, respectively. The large number of particles analysed in this work ensure the statistical representativeness and highlight the efficiency and effectiveness of our approach.
