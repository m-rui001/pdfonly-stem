Abstract The environmental and societal consequences of the increasing levels of carbon dioxide in our atmosphere are among the most significant challenges society currently faces. Carbon dioxide utilization, in which carbon dioxidfe is either used directly or converted into more valuable products, is likely to be one component of a broad strategy to reduce carbon dioxide emissions, a challenge that will require both technological and policy changes. Catalysis is crucial to the successful conversion of carbon dioxide into value-added products. Here, we provide a high level status report of chemical and biological systems for carbon dioxide conversion directed towards the readers of ACS Catalysis. We discuss both specific challenges related to the conversion of carbon dioxide into products such as carbon monoxide, formic acid, methanol, methane, ethylene, fuels, carboxylic acids, and polymers as well as general challenges for the field. We also compare and contrast different methods for carbon dioxide conversion, for example homogeneous versus heterogeneous catalysts or photosynthetic versus non-photosynthetic biological conversion, and highlight areas where one approach may have advantages over another. In a concluding section, we provide information about problems related to carbon dioxide conversion that will need to be addressed for technology to be viable and reduce carbon dioxide emissions. Keywords Carbon Dioxide; Catalysis; Chemical Conversion; Biological Conversion; Electrochemistry.

I.

Introduction

Since the industrial revolution, the concentration of carbon dioxide (CO2) in the atmosphere has increased by approximately 75%.1 The major cause of this increase is the burning of fossil fuels as well as other human activities such as deforestation and the production of cement.2 The rising levels of atmospheric CO2 have caused an increase in global temperatures, and this has resulted in environmental and societal problems such as rising sea levels, more frequent extreme weather events, and higher death rates, which are predicted to become even more severe if CO2 levels continue to rise.3 In fact, reducing the concentration of CO2 in the atmosphere is arguably the most significant challenge society is currently facing. The solution to this problem will require technical advances in areas including CO2 sequestration4 and the use of energy sources which do not release significant carbon emissions5 as well as policy changes. Additionally, CO2 utilization, in which CO2 is either directly used6 or converted into more valuable products, could play a valuable complementary role. Recently, the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine7 and the Mission Innovation Carbon Capture, Utilization, and Storage (CCUS) Experts’ Workshop8 independently released detailed reports about CO2 utilization. Both reports identified advances in catalysis as a crucial component to the development of improved systems for CO2 utilization. The catalysis community has already recognized its key role in this area, as approximately 95 manuscripts were published in 2018 in ACS Catalysis on CO2 utilization chemistry.9 In this perspective, we provide a high level status report of catalytic systems for CO2 utilization focused towards the readers of ACS Catalysis. There are three main pathways for CO2 conversion into valuable products: (i) mineral carbonation; (ii) chemical conversion; and (iii) biological conversion (Figure 1).10 Mineral carbonation, which involves converting CO2 into mineral carbonates, for example CaCO3, will potentially generate products for the construction industry, such as concrete. In contrast, both chemical and biological conversion could generate valuable platform or commodity chemicals, such as methanol or ethylene, and fuels as products. In terms of scale, building materials and fuels are produced and consumed on a much larger scale than chemicals.10 In fact, it is important to note that while annual CO2 emissions from anthropogenic activity in 2017 were around 40 gigatons,11 only approximately 0.35 billion tons of plastics are made per year.12 Therefore, CO2 conversion into chemicals is unlikely to significantly reduce CO2 levels in the atmosphere by itself. Nevertheless, there are often compelling financial or environmental benefits for converting CO2 into chemicals. 2

The challenges associated with improving methods for the conversion of CO2 into mineral carbonates primarily do not involve catalysis and will not be discussed in this perspective.13 However, catalysis will play a central role in the development of improved systems for the chemical and biological conversion of CO2, which is the focus of this perspective. Although we discuss both types of conversion separately, in many cases the potential products are the same, and it is possible that a chemical system will be better in one geographic location or for one product but a biological system will be preferred in a different location or for a different product. We do not comprehensively cover the field or provide a large amount of technical detail (as this can be obtained from the numerous review articles on CO2 reduction14), but instead use selected examples to outline significant progress and identify overarching problems in the field. In a concluding section, we discuss challenges that are not directly related to the development of improved catalysts but will need to be solved in order to commercially implement many new systems for CO2 conversion. We also discuss what is required for these technologies to reduce CO2 emissions. This final section emphasizes that the development of efficient catalysts for CO2 utilization is a necessary but not sufficient condition for a technology to be commercially viable and beneficial to the environment.

II.

Chemical Conversion of CO2

There are only a small number of chemicals made industrially using CO2 (Table 1).15 The two most notable examples are salicylic acid, which was first made from CO2 in the 19th century,16 and urea, which is made from CO2 on a scale of approximately 112 million tons per year.14m However, neither of these processes require catalysts. Most of the major commercial catalytic CO2 utilization processes involve the coupling of CO2 with epoxides. Depending on the catalyst, CO2 can be coupled with epoxides, such as ethylene or propylene oxide, to generate cyclic carbonates17 or undergo polymerization reactions to form either polycarbonates or polyether carbonates (Figure 2).18 In methanol synthesis, CO2 is often incorporated into the CO and H2 stream (see section II.A.3), but catalysts for the large scale synthesis of methanol from CO2 and H2 have remained elusive. The limited number of examples, despite the large amount of research that has been performed, highlights the significant challenges associated with developing practical catalytic systems for CO2 utilization. From a chemical perspective, these challenges include the limited range of products that can be produced from CO2 due to its thermodynamic stability and the large energy barriers that often need to be overcome due to its kinetic stability. C1 Products: Carbon Monoxide (CO), Formic Acid, Methanol, Methane

The vast majority of research into the catalytic reduction of CO2 has focused on the formation of C1 products. These products are typically CO or formic acid from two electron reduction (or the addition of one equivalent of H2), formaldehyde from four electron reduction, methanol from six electron reduction, and methane from eight electron reduction (Figure 3). In fact, controlling the extent of reduction so that one product, for example CO, is formed selectively is a major challenge for the field, particularly when reactions are performed electro- or photocatalytically. The process is economically feasible only in Iceland because of a combination of three factors: (i) the availability of low cost electricity, required to generate the H2, (ii) the high concentration of CO2 in the flue gas (85-90%) that is utilized, which substantially lowers the cost of CO2 compared with more traditional flue gas streams which contain lower amounts of CO2, and (iii) the requirement in the European Union that a certain fraction of liquid fuels, such as methanol, must be produced from sustainable feedstocks. The scale of the plant is also significantly smaller than most traditional methanol production plants. Accordingly, it would not be economically feasible to implement this technology in other locations. Some of these problems are also issues for scaling up pilot plants for the production of CO and methane from CO2. Instead, electrochemical systems offer the greatest potential for the catalytic conversion of CO2 to CO.21 Two different strategies have been adopted, one which operates at high temperature and the other at low temperature. Solid oxide electrolysis cells (SOECs), which comprise of two electrodes and a solid oxide (O2-) conducting electrolyte, typically operate at high temperature.22 In SOECs, CO2 is reduced to CO and O2- at the cathode, which often features a supported nickel catalyst. Then in a process which requires temperatures between 700-900 °C, O2- diffuses through the electrolyte to the anode where it is oxidized to O2. The electrolyte material is often a yittria-stabilized zirconia, while the anode is commonly a strontium-doped lanthanum manganite. Some of the advantages of SOECs for CO2 reduction to CO include that they can produce pure product streams of CO, operate at high current densities, and achieve high energy efficiency.22a,23 In fact, Haldor-Topsoe currently sells a commercial SOEC system that provides CO in purities ranging from 99.0-99.999% for on-site applications. Nevertheless, improvements are required to find systems with greater stability, electrolytes that conduct O2- at lower temperatures, and catalysts that are tolerant to impurities in the CO2 stream.

Perhaps no reaction related to CO2 utilization has attracted more recent attention than the development of molecular and heterogeneous electrocatalysts for low-temperature CO2 reduction to CO.24 High Faradaic efficiencies (up to 98%) can now be achieved with a range of different catalysts in both neutral and acidic electrolytes. First, homogeneous catalysts are inherently less likely to be practical for the production of cheap chemicals, such as CO, due to problems associated with product separation and catalyst recovery, especially when expensive ligands are used. Using a synthetically challenging ligand may be valuable for performing mechanistic studies or developing guidelines for improving catalysis, but can be a major drawback when comparing a system that has an expensive ligand with a system that has a cheap ligand, even if catalyst activity and productivity are enhanced. Second, when considering both homogeneous and heterogeneous systems (which are significantly more likely to provide a practical solution for CO2 reduction to CO), it is important to consider the overall reaction conditions. For example, to maximize the rate of CO production, it is common to use CO2 flow rates that exceed the rate of CO production. As a consequence, the gas stream at the end of the reaction contains significantly more CO2 than CO, which creates a separation problem. Additionally, CO2 can react with OH- that is present in the electrolyte to form carbonates, which can foul the electrode or be reduced to CO2 at the anode. If the anodic reaction is water oxidation, this means that the gas stream at the anode will be a mixture of CO2 and O2. A recent report from Dioxide Materials that uses an anion-conductive membrane based electrolyzer with a silver cathode catalyst may solve this problem by preventing CO2 diffusion to the anode, but further work is required in this area.30 Thirdly, the stability of current catalysts needs to be improved. This is related to the more general point that academic researchers both in the area of CO2 reduction 8

In the context of both CO2 reduction to CO and catalysis in general, we would encourage the research community to increase the value it places on catalyst stability. II.A.2 Formic acid Formic acid can be catalytically synthesized from CO2 thermally (using H2), electrochemically, or photochemically with either homogeneous or heterogeneous catalysts.14a,14i,21b,24a,33 Given that formic acid, like CO, is a low value chemical, homogeneous catalysts are probably most valuable for gaining mechanistic insight rather than for practical purposes. Additionally, the rates of formic acid production in photochemical systems are currently low, and it is not certain if photo-reactors that allow the reaction to be performed on a large scale can be developed.21b It is also not clear if systems which directly use photons to produce formic acid will necessarily provide advantages over an indirect approach that initially uses photovoltaics to generate electrons, followed by an electrocatalyst to synthesize formic acid. Therefore, either thermal or electrochemical heterogeneous catalysts are the most probable to provide a practical system. The hydrogenation of CO2 to formic acid is thermodynamically unfavorable in the gas phase and in organic solvents, but is slightly exoergic under aqueous conditions.34 As a consequence, CO2 hydrogenation is generally performed in the presence of a base, which creates a driving force for the reaction by deprotonating the formic acid and generating the formate ion (Eq 2). Unfortunately, this also lowers the atom economy of the reaction and leads to the formation of waste products. At this stage, the most active and productive catalysts are supported palladium nanoparticles on activated carbon, but several immobilized homogeneous catalysts have also shown promising performance.14i,35 Again the ligands on these homogeneous catalysts will need to be simplified to make more practical systems. However, the major challenges in CO2 hydrogenation to formic acid are to find systems that do not require a stoichiometric amount of a base. This could be achieved either by developing methods to recycle the base or by designing reactors that allow formic acid to be directly removed from the reactor at low conversion to drive the reaction forward.

II.A.3 Methanol Methanol, which is industrially synthesized from syngas (H2 + CO), is one of the largest scale chemicals produced in the world (approximately 100 million tons are produced annually).14i,39 The first step in the synthesis of methanol from syngas is the water-gas shift reaction in which CO and H2O are converted into CO2 and H2 (reverse of Eq 1). As a result, in industrial systems that are currently used to synthesize methanol, the syngas feed can contain up to 30% CO2,40 which means that a significant amount of CO2 is already converted into methanol. Nevertheless, given the enormous scale on which methanol is produced and the fact that syngas is generated directly from fossil fuels, methanol is an attractive target for production from a feed containing only CO2 and H2. As described earlier (Table 2), there are operational pilot plants for the direct synthesis of methanol from CO2, but these are not practical on a large scale. For a long time, research into heterogeneous catalysts for the direct hydrogenation of CO2 to methanol focused on exploring copper catalysts related to those optimized for syngas conversion to methanol.14a,14i,34 In fact, the pilot plants that are operational use copper based catalysts, but high rates and high methanol selectivity have only been possible using high pressures (>300 bar).15a The major by-products that need to be avoided are hydrocarbons and CO, which is produced through the reverse water-gas shift reaction. This concept is illustrated by work relating to the discovery of zeolite based catalysts for the conversion of methanol to 2,2,3-trimethylbutane (triptane), a hydrocarbon with a high octane number.42 Secondly, in principle it may be possible to immobilize highly active homogeneous catalysts on inert solid supports to generate heterogeneous catalysts, although there are few examples where this approach has been successful.43 Finally, the fundamental knowledge gained from studying CO2 hydrogenation to methanol may be relevant to organic transformations that are important in the synthesis of pharmaceuticals and agrochemicals, where homogeneous catalysts are often preferred. II.B

Ethylene (& ethanol)

Ethylene is used as feedstock to synthesize a wide range of different chemicals and is a crucial monomer in many common polymers.52 Based on its annual production (over 154,000 million tons were produced in 201739d), more ethylene is produced each year than any other organic compound. Ethylene is currently obtained primarily from fossil fuel derived feedstocks, and as a result, there is considerable interest in synthesizing it from CO2. To date, two main approaches have been utilized to catalytically generate ethylene from CO2. The first involves using heterogeneous iron catalysts to hydrogenate CO2 to ethylene.53 At low conversion, these systems generate ethylene and other light olefins with selectivity of up to 50%, but many lower value chemicals such as alkanes (including methane) and CO are also produced. At higher CO2 conversion, the selectivity deteriorates markedly. Without large improvements in selectivity, this route is unlikely to generate significant yields of ethylene. The other route for catalytically generating ethylene from CO2 is electrochemical. At high overpotentials, heterogeneous copper catalysts can achieve selectivities greater than 40%, via a CO intermediate.54 Faradaic efficiencies of 60-70% at rates of 160-250 mA/cm2 can be obtained by alloying silver with the copper catalyst, which promotes the formation of the CO intermediate.55 Interestingly, these systems typically also produce ethanol with Faradaic efficiencies of between 10-30%. The development of electrocatalysts for CO2 reduction to ethylene is still in an early stage, and improvements are required in catalyst selectivity and stability. II.C

Fuels

Fuels (defined in this perspective as mixtures containing C2 or higher hydrocarbons) are one of the most desirable targets for CO2 utilization. An enormous amount of CO2 is released into the atmosphere from the combustion of fuels each year. If fuels could be synthesized from CO2, the carbon cycle relating to fuels would essentially be closed, and this, more so than the synthesis of 13

Page 14

any other molecule or group of molecules from CO2, would reduce global CO2 emissions. We note that this statement will only be accurate if the H2 or electrons used to reduce CO2 are generated with low or zero CO2 emissions. This issue is discussed in more detail in section IV. It may also be the case some of the C1 compounds which can potentially be made from CO2, such as methane or methanol (see sections II.A.3 and II.A.4), could be used as fuels. In this case, it may not be necessary to make fuels based on C2 or higher hydrocarbons, which is chemically more challenging due to the need to form C–C bonds. The Fischer-Tropsch process is an established industrial scale method for generating liquid fuels from CO and H2 (Eq 3).14m The CO and H2 feedstocks that are used in Fischer-Tropsch plants are typically generated from fossil fuels. However, in principle, an electrocatalyst for the conversion of CO2 to CO (see sections II.A.1) could be used along with a catalyst that can renewably produce H2 from water to generate the feedstocks for the Fischer-Tropsch process in a sustainable fashion. In this approach, different reactors and different catalysts are required for the production of CO and the Fischer-Tropsch process (along with another reactor and catalyst for H2 production). In contrast, several systems have been developed that use a single catalyst and reactor to convert CO2 to CO and perform the Fischer-Tropsch process (the H2 is still supplied separately).56 In these systems, CO is normally generated from CO2 using the reverse water-gas-shift reaction (Eq 1). A stoichiometric equivalent of H2 is converted to H2O for every equivalent of CO generated. Subsequently, conventional Fischer-Tropsch chemistry is performed to convert the CO and H2 to liquid fuels. Two demonstration plants, which both use a heterogeneous iron catalyst, are operational for this process, but are not economically feasible due to the high cost of H2 (Table 2).14m A major problem with this chemistry is that the concentration of CO present in the reaction mixture is typically low, and as a consequence, the iron catalysts currently used produce light hydrocarbons predominantly, which are not suitable as liquid fuels. More research is required to develop catalysts that can produce hydrocarbons in the liquid fuel range for this type of strategy to be viable.

The electrochemical conversion of CO2 to fuels is another strategy that is being actively pursued.24b,24c However, finding catalysts that can form C–C bonds instead of generating the types of C1 products described in section II.A is challenging. To date, heterogeneous copper 14

Carboxylic acids

Carboxylic acids are an important class of chemicals that are produced for a plethora of different applications on a wide range of scales. For example, carboxylic acids such as acrylic and adipic acids are large scale commodity chemicals,58 whereas others such as salicylic acid are the active ingredients in pharmaceuticals.16 At this stage, the vast majority of carboxylic acids are prepared on an industrial scale via the aerobic oxidation of hydrocarbons.7 However, the corrosive reaction conditions typically used for these oxidations require the use of expensive reactors. An alternative, atom economical method for the preparation of carboxylic acids is through the formal insertion of CO2 into a C–H bond. At this stage, a variety of different carboxylic acids have been prepared using this synthetic route, but as described below, most of these catalytic reactions have limitations. The major problem associated with the synthesis of carboxylic acids via CO2 insertion into a C–H bond is that these reactions are often thermodynamically unfavorable, which means that expensive stoichiometric bases or Lewis acids are commonly utilized. Additionally, C–H bond activation is often kinetically challenging, which can necessitate the use of harsh reaction conditions and reagents. II.D.1 Acrylic and Methacrylic Acid The synthesis of acrylic acid through the coupling of CO2 and ethylene (or methacrylic acid through the coupling of CO2 and propylene) has fascinated researchers for more than forty years (Eqs 4 & 5).58b This is because both acrylic and methacrylic acid are large scale commodity chemicals that are currently produced from feedstocks derived from fossil fuels. The formation of acrylic or methacrylic acid from CO2 and the appropriate olefin is thermodynamically disfavored, and the reactions are normally performed using a stoichiometric amount of a base to drive the reaction through the formation of a carboxylate salt. This reduces the atom economy of the process. To date, homogeneous nickel or palladium catalysts have been used for catalytic acrylate 15

formation.59 The best systems give low turnover frequencies (<0.1 h-1) and turnover numbers (<1,000). Additionally, these values are only obtained when a stoichiometric amount of zinc dust is added. The exact mechanistic role of the zinc is unclear, but it is proposed to regenerate the active nickel(0) catalyst from inactive nickel(II) salts. Significant improvements are required in catalyst activity and productivity and the need for stoichiometric additives in order to develop practical systems for CO2 conversion to acrylic acid.

II.D.2 Furan-2,5-Dicarboxylic Acid One area where research into CO2 utilization has remained stagnant is in the types of molecules that are targeted for synthesis. Many of the molecules that are actively being pursued today are the same as those that were targeted fifty years ago. Although this in part reflects the difficulty in developing catalytic processes involving CO2 as well as the relatively small number of molecules that are thermodynamically favorable to form from CO2, it is also indicative of our general failure to look for new commodity chemicals – beyond those traditionally derived from fossil fuels – that may be useful. Furan-2,5-dicarboxylic acid (FDCA) is an example of a chemical that only recently has been considered for synthesis from CO2. Specifically, FDCA could be used as a monomer for polyester synthesis, as it leads to a polymer with better gas barrier properties than polyethylene terephthalate, one of the most common plastics, when it is condensed with ethylene glycol.60 In 2016, it was shown that at elevated temperatures (200-350 °C), simple alkali carbonate salts can be used to promote the conversion of 2-furoic acid to FDCA.61 Subsequently, it was demonstrated that this chemistry can be performed on a 1 mole scale (Eq 6).62 No solvent or transition metal catalyst is required in the reaction, and 2-furoic acid is readily synthesized from inexpensive furfural, which can be generated from most terrestrial plants and is not edible. II.D.3 Benzoic Acid and Related Aryl Carboxylic Acids The generation of carboxylic acids through the reaction of CO2 with an arene could have applications in the synthesis of both commodity chemicals and pharmaceuticals.7 In the simplest example, benzoic acid, a chemical which is produced on a scale of approximately 600,000 tons per year, would be synthesized through the reaction of benzene with CO2.7 There are currently a number of systems that can carboxylate benzene, but they require the addition of stoichiometric amounts of aluminum additives and the reaction conditions are relatively harsh.63 Boogaerts and Nolan reported a simpler system for the carboxylation of arenes with acidic C–H bonds such as oxazoles and azoles.64 A homogeneous gold catalyst can catalyze these reactions at room temperature using 1 atm of CO2 to make a series of carboxylic acids that are relevant to the pharmaceutical industry (Eq 7). Nevertheless, the development of catalysts for the carboxylation of arenes with non-acidic C–H bonds remains a significant challenge.

Polymers

The synthesis of polymers is a rare example of a catalytic process involving CO2 that is already practiced industrially. There are examples of commercial processes both for the synthesis of polycarbonates, which have limited applications because they typically have low-glass transition temperatures, and polyether carbonates, which are suitable for a much wider range of applications including insulating in electronic components and cushioning materials.71 A notable example of a processes used to generate polycarbonates from CO2 on a commercial scale is the Asahi-Kasei process in which CO2, ethylene oxide, and bisphenol A are combined to make a polycarbonate (Figure 5).72 In this process, CO2 is converted into ethylene carbonate in a catalytic reaction involving CO2 and ethylene oxide. The ethylene carbonate is subsequently converted into diphenyl carbonate (via a dimethyl carbonate intermediate) and then treated with bisphenol A to generate polycarbonate. II.F.1 Catalyst Design and Development Although catalysts have been developed to generate a range of different products from CO2, there are relatively few examples in which new C–C bonds are formed between two molecules of CO2. It is clear that there is a lack of information about the requirements to facilitate C–C bond formation, and more mechanistic understanding about this topic would be valuable. It may also enable the pursuit of catalysts for non-traditional products from CO2. Additionally, the focus of most academic research to date has been in optimizing catalyst activity and selectivity. Both of these parameters are important, but emphasis also needs to be placed on catalyst stability, as this is crucial for developing practical systems. To this end, studies which explicitly determine the deactivation pathways of catalysts would provide helpful information. Understanding catalyst decomposition may also assist with another key area in CO2 utilization chemistry: the integration of CO2 utilization with CO2 capture.75 To date, these fields have mainly progressed in parallel, but they are intimately connected. For example, if low purity CO2 can be used in catalytic applications, it will affect the choice of method for CO2 capture, as well as the waste streams available for practical use. For this purpose, it would be beneficial to know if catalysts can maintain activity when dilute CO2 streams are used (most catalysts are only tested using pure CO2 streams) and if they can tolerate the types of impurities that are present in flue gas. In turn, this will influence reactor design, and developing specialized reactors that can integrate capture and utilization is an area of opportunity. Innovative reactor designs may solve another major problem for many catalysts for CO2 conversion, namely, the need for expensive stoichiometric reagents for thermodynamic reasons. For example, hydrogenating CO2 to formic acid is not as attractive if a stoichiometric base is required to deprotonate the formic acid and generate a formate salt. II.F.2 Electrochemical Conversion In order to efficiently catalyze the electrochemical reduction of CO2 with high rates of product formation, CO2 must be readily transported to the catalyst, while high ionic conductivity is maintained. Gas diffusion electrodes and flow cells are able to meet these requirements. However, the major problems with many systems utilizing this technology is that only a small amount of CO2 (<20%) is converted to product and CO2 reacts with OH- in the electrolyte to form carbonates. The low single-pass conversion means that energy is required to separate the unreacted CO2 from the product and reintroduce it into the reaction mixture. The reaction of CO2 with the electrolyte lowers the yield of the reaction (which is especially a problem if effort has been taken to generate high purity CO2) and can degrade both the electrolyte and electrode (as a result of carbonate deposition). Many of these factors are not considered when analyzing the strengths and weaknesses of different systems and need to be accounted for in determining the overall efficiency of a process. Another factor that has a large influence on the overall energy requirement of an electrochemical CO2 reduction reaction is the process that is occurring at the anode. In most electrochemical systems for CO2 reduction, water oxidation to O2 is the reaction at the anode. Thermochemical analysis indicates that approximately 90% of the total energy required to drive the overall CO2 reduction process is used at the anode. This is problematic given the low commercial value of O2. The lower thermodynamic potential for the oxidation of common chemicals such as glycerol and glucose (0.8-1.1 V lower than water oxidation) means that performing these reactions at the anode requires significantly less energy. In areas where these chemicals are readily available, it may be advantageous to use the electrons and protons generated from their oxidation for CO2 reduction. The oxidized products would subsequently need to be sold as a product themselves, recycled or disposed, which would decrease the atom economy of the process. III. III.A.1 Green algae Green algae are eukaryotic, single-cellular, photosynthetic organisms from a highly diverse phylum. To date, most large-scale efforts for CO2 conversion into valuable products have capitalized on green algae’s ability to grow rapidly in outdoor cultivation and resist predation from invasive species.81 The majority of examples have focused on renewable fuel production, however other applications have become viable in recent years. The development of genetic tools for green algae lag behind those of other, more developed hosts, such as Saccharomyces cerevisiae, therefore genetic engineering to date has been limited to 1-2 genes that can be expressed in either the nuclear or chloroplast genomes, and Chlamydomonas reinhardtii has been primarily used as the model organism for genetic manipulation.82 To date, the most developed products, which are summarized in Figure 8, have been derived from naturally isolated green algae strains. III.A.1.i Biodiesel and renewable fuels Biodiesel is the product of lipid conversion to fatty acid methyl esters (FAMEs) and was one of the earliest products produced from algae biomass cultivation. Many green algae store energy in the form of triacylglycerides, and these can be easily extracted and converted into FAMEs.83 Although biodiesel can be used as a drop-in fuel for many diesel engines, its hygroscopicity commonly leads to the corrosion of engines and fouling of holding tanks, making it a non-ideal substitute for petroleum diesel. Renewable fuels including gasoline and jet fuel can be produced through the reductive decarboxylation of lipids or FAMEs through a catalytic hydrogenation process known as “hydrotreatment”, followed by conventional fuel refining.84 Although the cost of the capital infrastructure required to carry out hydrotreatment on a commercial scale is large, the fuels produced do not suffer from the same problems as biodiesel and are more likely to be practical in the long term. III.A.1.ii Protein The production of biofuels from biomass inherently creates waste products, such as protein, from unused biomass. The development of strategies to use these waste products is important because it makes it more economically feasible to produce biofuels from biomass. Green algae biomass can produce up to 50-fold higher protein content per acre than common crops.85 Therefore, algae is an attractive source of dietary protein, both for animal feeds and human consumption, and considerable research is occurring in this area, but initial success is yet to be verified on scale.86 27

III.B.i Chemolithotrophs Chemolithotrophs fix carbon through the oxidation of reduced inorganic compounds, allowing light independent CO2 fixation.109 Acetogens, a subset of chemolithotrophs, can function under strictly anaerobic conditions to bypass the carbon loss that occurs when systems operate under mixotrophic fermentation conditions. Indirect Challenges for Developing Practical Systems for CO2 Utilization The introductions of many talks and publications from academic groups can often unintentionally give the impression that the development of highly active, stable, and economically viable catalysts or algae for CO2 utilization is all that is required for practical systems that will reduce CO2 emissions. Although there is a considerable need for active and stable catalysts or algae (see sections II and III) in order for most technologies to become commercially viable and significantly reduce CO2 emissions, there are also a number of other problems that must be addressed. Here, we provide a brief summary of some of these other challenges. IV.A

The production of H2 or electricity with low or zero CO2 emissions

The vast majority of catalytic processes for CO2 reduction use either H2 or electricity. Currently, H2 is produced on a large scale via steam reforming of methane (Eq 8).113 This is an endothermic process that is performed at temperatures between 700 and 1100 °C and requires a large amount of energy. The energy is typically supplied from fossil fuels, and as a consequence, H2 production generates significant CO2 emissions. Additionally, the water-gas shift reaction is often used to generate additional H2 from the CO produced from steam reforming, a process which directly produces CO2. Therefore, for CO2 utilization technologies which utilize H2 as a co-reagent to be truly effective in reducing CO2 emissions, the development of scalable strategies for H2 production are critical. One approach is the generation of H2 by electrolysis from renewable electricity and water, but this is currently too expensive at large scale.114 The use of renewable electricity that has low or zero CO2 emissions (for example from photovoltaics instead of fossil fuels) is also critical for processes that use electrons to reduce CO2 to lower CO2 emissions.

IV.B

Scale

As described earlier, even if a chemical can be produced from CO2, the relatively small scale on which even the largest chemicals are produced (as compared to anthropogenic CO2 emissions) means that the new synthetic route is unlikely to reduce CO2 levels in the atmosphere. This does not imply that there will not be benefits to the environment from the new synthetic route, simply that CO2 utilization by itself will not be the solution to the rising levels of CO2 in the atmosphere. 33

Page 34

Researchers need to be careful to make this distinction when presenting their results on CO2 utilization strategies. IV.C

Land and water use

In order to cultivate algae biomass, large sources of water are required. This can be a problem for algae that need freshwater, which can be scarce, to survive. In these cases, an environmental analysis needs to be performed to assess whether the need for freshwater will have detrimental societal consequences before a technology is implemented. We note that many algae can survive in saltwater, and this provides a method for CO2 utilization, which does not impact freshwater resources. In both cases, consideration needs to be given to how the water used to cultivate algal biomass will be recycled or disposed, and this may prevent a technology from being commercialized. Along with considerations related to water use, land use is another important factor in biological CO2 utilization. Large amounts of land are required for algae cultivation, and the environmental and economic consequences of this requirement need to be evaluated prior to commercialization. IV.D

Regulatory issues

There are often regulatory issues that need to be satisfied for a new technology to be commercialized. For example, in commodity chemical production, there are strict regulations about the types of impurities that are permitted. As a consequence, if a new synthetic route involving CO2 utilization – one that contains different impurities than the traditional route – is developed, changes in regulation may be necessary before the chemical can be sold. In biological CO2 utilization, the production of a chemical using genetically modified organisms in open water streams may have to overcome regulatory restrictions before it is commercialized. IV.E

Consequences associated with changing feedstocks for the chemical industry

In the chemical industry there is a complex relationship between processes, which is related to our current feedstocks. For example, acrylonitrile is produced by the catalytic ammoxidation of propylene.115 This process produces acetonitrile as a by-product. Therefore, if a method is developed for the synthesis of acrylonitrile from CO2, this will have a significant impact on acetonitrile production. Further, changes in starting feedstocks or even intermediates, will likely cause subsequent changes downstream in other processes. For instance, the shift from cracking naphtha to cracking natural gas liquids resulted in a decrease in the availability of propylene and 34

Page 35

butadiene. As a consequence plants for the direct production of propylene were more likely to be commercially viable. The examples presented in this section demonstrate that although it is possible to change feedstocks, for example the chemical industry switched from using acetylene as a feedstock to ethylene, it is complicated. The academic community should be aware that simply developing one new process using CO2 as a feedstock may have a range of consequences, which could make the chemical industry reluctant to invest in a new CO2 based technology. V.

Concluding Remarks

In this perspective we have summarized the current status and major challenges and opportunities in the conversion of CO2 into more valuable chemicals using chemical catalysis and biological processes. Although there has been a significant amount of research in both areas, practical systems have proved elusive. In the area of chemical catalysis, key issues include the need for more active, stable, and selective catalysts, and systems that do not require stoichiometric additives, as well as the development of reactors that can integrate capture and utilization. In the area of biological conversion key issues include the need for better methods to identify and characterize organisms with unique attributes for CO2 conversion, and to enhance the productivity and stability of organisms which can convert CO2, as well as the development of computational tools for in-depth modelling. Problems general to both approaches include the need for purpose built reactors that promote product formation, which will likely require collaboration between engineers and chemists, as well as the identification of impurities in CO2 streams which inhibit conversion. Finally, the range of target molecules from CO2 has not increased significantly in recent years, and the identification of innovative approaches to find new commodity chemicals which can be synthesized from CO2 could have transformative implications. Another goal of this work was to make the catalysis community aware of some of the challenges related to practical CO2 utilization and emissions reduction that will not be addressed directly by the development of more efficient catalysts or algae for CO2 conversion. These include the needs for large scale systems for the production of H2 or electricity that generate minimal carbon emissions and the evaluation of land and water use consequences of a new technology. Additionally, in some cases regulatory changes will be required for a new system to be implemented. These challenges highlight that developing new systems for practical CO2 utilization is difficult and will require cooperation and collaboration between chemists and specialists in many 35

fields. Nevertheless, examples such as the Covestro process for the synthesis of polyols from CO2, demonstrate that if active and stable catalysts are developed, the barriers are not insurmountable. Overall, this perspective highlights that CO2 utilization will likely have a role to play in future strategies for the management of carbon emissions and the development of catalysts will be central to efforts in this area.
