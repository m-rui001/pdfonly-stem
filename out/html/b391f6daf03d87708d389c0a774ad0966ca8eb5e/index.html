Six-junction III-V solar cells with 47.1% conversion efficiency under 143 suns concentration

John F. Geisz1*, Ryan M. France1, Kevin L. Schulte1, Myles A. Steiner1, Andrew G. Norman1, Harvey L. Guthrey1, Matthew R. Young1, Tao Song1, and Thomas Moriarty1

Single-junction flat-plate terrestrial solar cells are fundamentally limited to about 30% solar-to-electricity conversion efficiency, but multiple junctions and concentrated light make much higher efficiencies practically achievable. Until now, four-junction III-V concentrator solar cells have demonstrated the highest solar conversion efficiencies. Here, we demonstrate 47.1% solar conversion efficiency using a monolithic, series-connected, six-junction inverted metamorphic structure operated under the direct spectrum at 143 suns concentration. When tuned to the global spectrum, a variation of this structure achieves a one-sun global efficiency of 39.2%. Nearly optimal bandgaps for sixjunctions were fabricated using alloys of III-V semiconductors. To develop these junctions, it was necessary to minimize threading dislocations in lattice-mismatched III-V alloys, prevent phase segregation in metastable quaternary III-V alloys, and understand dopant diffusion in complex structures. Further reduction of the series resistance within this structure could realistically enable efficiencies over 50%.

The path to extremely high solar-to-electric conversion efficiencies for photovoltaic devices calls for near-perfect materials, multiple absorber bandgaps, and high optical concentration. Reaching the detailed balance, or Shockley- Queisser 1, limit to solar cell conversion efficiency requires suppression of all forms of non-radiative recombination (i.e. materials with 100% internal radiative efficiency) while achieving perfect light extraction from the solar cell (i.e. devices with 100% external radiative efficiency) 2,3.

Multijunction (MJ) devices surpass the detailed balance limit of single junction solar cells by collecting a large portion of the broad solar spectrum. They also mitigate thermalization loss by binning bands of photons using several materials with bandgaps matched to the photon energies of each bin 4. As the number of junctions increases, the potential conversion efficiency increases asymptotically to 65% at one sun 5. Optical concentration of the incident light also increases the density of photogenerated carriers in the semiconductor, which further separates the quasi- fermi levels and pushes the voltage of each junction toward the bandgap limit. Thus, 85% efficiency could theoretically be achieved for an infinite number of junctions at maximum concentration in the detailed balance limit 5.

Epitaxial single-crystal III-V alloys provide the highest quality optoelectronic materials available and have the broad range of bandgaps needed for high efficiency conversion of the solar spectrum. The optimal bandgaps under representative solar spectra have been calculated as

a function of the number of junctions 4-6 using a variety of reasonable assumptions. The most conservative of these calculations 6 indicates that 62% solar cell efficiency could be achieved at 1000 suns with six junctions that each have 1% external radiative efficiency (ERE). The ERE is the best quantification of the junction voltage performance 7 due to the reciprocal nature of the relation between light emission and solar cell performance 8. An ERE of 1% is a reasonably achievable value for many typical III-V alloys. To provide context, the best ERE reported at one-sun is about 22% for GaAs and 0.6% for silicon 7.

Achieving high III-V material quality with the desired bandgap becomes more challenging when the alloy is lattice-mismatched with respect to the substrate (resulting in dislocations) or when is not thermodynamically temperatures (resulting in phase separation) 9. Nonetheless, several advanced III-V growth strategies have enabled the use of challenging III-V alloys in recent years. In particular, metamorphic strategies that gradually grade the lattice constant and relax the resulting strain with minimal dislocation formation have enabled devices with optimal bandgap combinations but low performance losses that arise from dislocations 10-12 . In addition, advances in the science of epitaxy have enabled the development of lowdefect implementations of materials that would normally tend to phase segregate, by using kinetically stabilized organo-metallic vapor phase epitaxy (OMVPE) growth conditions 13-15 . These meta-stable alloys provide a wider design space from which to build MJ devices.

Inverted metamorphic multijunction (IMM) devices 17 are monolithically grown on a single GaAs substrate, and lattice-mismatched involve both subcells to achieve an optimal bandgap combination. Growth begins with the high-bandgap latticematched junctions, leaving these high power-producing junctions without dislocations, and thus the growth direction traditional multijunction solar cells. Then compositionally graded buffers (CGB) access the lower bandgap junctions with a larger lattice constant. “Metamorphic” layers refer to low threading lattice-mismatched dislocation density as a result of using CGB. Four-junction IMM solar cells have achieved 45.6% efficiency at 690 suns 18 .

In this paper, we present extremely high solar-toelectrical conversion efficiencies using a six-junction (6J) IMM solar cell design. Under the one-sun global spectrum (AM1.5G), we demonstrate a 6J IMM solar cell with (39.2 ± 1.3)% (absolute) efficiency. We have also designed the 6J IMM device for operation under high concentration, to enable even higher efficiencies and potential use in CPV systems. The efficiency under the direct spectrum (AM1.5D) peaks at (47.1 ± 3.2)% (absolute) under 143 suns concentration and maintains an efficiency of 44.9% out to 1117 suns concentration. This is achieved by understanding and mitigating sources of internal resistance. Further improvements to the 6J IMM are projected to surpass 50% efficiency.

High Quality Metamorphic Materials

Each mismatched GaInAs junction is about 2-3 µm thick to fully absorb the appropriate portion of the solar spectrum. Such thicknesses far surpass the critical thickness 24 for these lattice-mismatched alloys, and so CGB layers are grown to relieve the strain and confine misfit dislocations within inactive material. III-V solar cells are extremely sensitive to the recombination associated with dislocations and demand a threading dislocation density (TDD) of about 106 cm-2 or lower in the active region to operate efficiently 25. In order to minimize the defect density in active layers, dislocations are encouraged to glide long distances within the CGB by eliminating impediments to glide, such as phase separation and surface roughness, and using growth conditions that encourage glide 12. This 6-junction IMM device utilizes three CGBs (light blue layers in Fig. 1b) in order to access the desired lattice constants, and thus bandgaps, of each of the three GaInAs junctions. AlGaInAs and GaInP CGBs had previously been developed for this reason 26. While AlGaInAs CGBs can be used to access GaInAs with low Indium-content, phase Inseparation concentrations (>30% of cation sites containing In) 27, which dramatically lowers the limits dislocation glide and performance of the highly-mismatched 0.69 eV Ga0.44In0.58As junction. In this work, GaxIn1-xP CGBs are used to access the lattice constant of each GaInAs junction, with the third buffer grading to lattice constants beyond InP using InP1-xSbx. CuPt-type atomic ordering is present in the GaInP CGBs, which encourages glide 28 and results in low TDD in the active junctions. Joule losses (I2R) eventually dominate for every finite series resistance as the current density is increased through concentration. In seriesconnected MJ devices, the peak tunneling current through the tunnel junctions also limits the practical concentrations. The 1-sun current density of a 6-junction device is much lower than for other PV technologies because the photon absorption is distributed evenly amongst the junctions. The efficiency of the series-connected MJ device is still very high because the voltages of the individual junctions add up to a high total voltage. Low current density reduces Joule losses and potentially enables significantly increased efficiency at high concentrations. However, materials considerations specific to the 6J IMM make achieving a low series resistance within the semiconductor structure challenging. The 2.1 eV bandgap material Al0.18Ga0.33In0.49P used as the top junction absorber has a low electron mobility, which is related to defects concomitant with Al incorporation. The sheet resistance of the top junction emitter is a key limiting resistance required for lateral conduction between metal grid fingers. To raise the mobility (and thus lower sheet resistance) of the AlGaInP emitter, we lowered the Al content of the n-type emitter to 6% while maintaining 18% in the p-type base, creating a “reverse Al content heterojunction” 22,35,36 where the emitter has a lower band gap than the base. While the mobility of the emitter increases with decreasing Al content, the top junction voltage also decreases because the band gap is reduced, creating a trade-off to the reduced emitter sheet resistance36. Improvements in the aspect ratio and shape of the metal grid fingers could also help reduce resistances related to the front grid without increasing shadow loss 37,38 but were not used here.

Internal resistances within the 6J IMM have proven very challenging, resulting in poor fill factors of initial 6J devices at concentration 22,39. The relatively long growth times (~7.5 hours) and sustained elevated temperatures during epitaxy encourage dopant diffusion and thus affect the effective internal series resistance. We have found diffusion from highly Zn-doped phosphide back surface field (BSF) layers to be particularly problematic lattice-matched GaAs junctions 40 . Due to the high thermal load, Zn preferentially diffuses out of phosphide BSF layers to nearby tunnel junctions and arsenide absorber layers, which can create barriers to conduction that limit high concentration performance. Zn has been shown to diffuse long distances due to a Ga-interstitial assisted mechanism 41,42. For the GaAs junction, a solution is using a carbon-doped AlGaAs BSF 43 rather than Zn-doped GaInP. We have identified a similar internal resistance related to the metamorphic portion of the 6J IMM solar cell. Here, we have replicated this problem in a simpler two junction (2J) test structure similar to the 4th and 5th junction of the 6J IMM as shown in Fig. 3a. Initially poor concentrator JV results of this 2J test structure shown as the red curve in Fig. 3c were improved

by changing the thickness and Al composition of a Zn-doped (Al)GaInAs diffusion barrier between the junctions. The AlGaInAs diffusion barrier that resulted in the blue curve of Fig. 3b was instrumental to reduce the resistance in the 6J IMM reported here. Secondary mass spectroscopy (SIMS) in Fig. 3b of the test structure corresponding to the green curve in Fig. 3c shows significantly more Zn in the TJ and base of the 4th junction than intended. In addition to the Zn- III-V alloys diffusion mechanism mentioned above , the Zn diffusion in this metamorphic test structure may also be enhanced around dislocations 42.

6J Device Characterization and Performance Six-junction solar cell devices were characterized with standard solar cell methods, modified for six junctions: external quantum efficiency (EQE) with simultaneous specular reflectance, and current density-voltage (JV) indoor solar simulators. Careful measurements under consideration of spectral tuning solar simulators for 6J measurements is detailed in Methods.

6J IMM devices were designed for both one-sun AM1.5G conditions and for high concentration AM1.5D conditions. Slight differences in these spectra required re-optimization of the junction bandgaps and thicknesses for current balancing as shown in the EQE of Fig. 4a. The flexibility of the bandgaps and thicknesses of the junctions is critical to achieve high current in a series-connected device 18. Differences in expected device current levels necessitated modifications to tunnel junction design, grid design and mesa area for optimal performance under the appropriate conditions.

The EQE shown in Fig. 4a indicates that each of the six junctions is collecting over 80% of the photons within its bands, despite non-zero reflectance losses. Given the measured short-circuit current densities (JSC) of the 6J IMM solar cells demonstrated in Fig. 4, these 6J IMM solar cells are collecting at least 74% and 73% of all the photons of the AM1.5G and AM1.5D spectra, respectively. Compare this to a perfect silicon solar cell that would convert 70% of the AM1.5G photons or a perfect GaAs solar cell that would convert 48% of the AM1.5G photons, both at drastically lower voltage than the 6J IMM.

The performance of a 0.25 cm2 aperture-area one-sun 6J IMM device under 1000 W/m2 AM1.5G conditions is shown in Fig. 4b. An efficiency of (39.2 ± 1.3)% (absolute uncertainty) was measured with open-circuit voltage (VOC) of 5.55 V, short-circuit current density (JSC) of 8.46 mA/cm2, and fill factor (FF) of 83.5%. This is the highest one-sun conversion efficiency demonstrated by any technology. However, it should be noted that the device area is too small to be recognized as an outright one-sun record where 1 cm2 is required 44. The highest outright one-sun record efficiency is 38.8% for a five-junction bonded III-V solar cell 45.

Methods OMVPE growth. The III-V layer structures were grown in a custombuilt, atmospheric-pressure (~620 torr) OMVPE system. GaAs substrate wafers were cleaved to 2x3 cm and lightly etched in NH4OH:H2O2:H2O (2:1:10) before growth. The substrate was placed on a graphite susceptor and heated by RF induction. The growth temperature was varied between 550°C and 750°C to control alloy growth quality and doping. included trimethylgallium (Ga), triethylgallium (Ga), trimethylaluminum (Al), (P), (In), arsine triethylantimony (Sb), dimethylhydrazine (N), diethylzinc (Zn), carbon tetrachloride (C), hydrogen selenide (Se), and disilane (Si). The full 6J IMM structure consisted of 140 layers, including individual compositional step-graded buffer layers as shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. The total growth time was 7.5 hours. In-situ multi-beam optical stress (MOS) and single wavelength (660 nm) reflectance as shown in Supplementary Fig. 2 were measured to understand and improve misfit strain relaxation, and roughening during OMVPE growth 50. Note that the average curvature in the GaInAs junctions is nearly constant, implying precise latticematching of these metamorphic junctions to the underlying compositionally graded buffer and thus, nearly stress-free growth of the active junctions. Differences in the curvature between the X[-110] and Y[110] directions indicate an anisotropy of relaxation or mechanical properties (e.g. due to atomic ordering51). Oscillations in reflectance data can be used to determine growth rates of some layer 52. The overall rise in the inverse reflectance during the last two graded buffers indicates an increase in

roughness due to crosshatch 53. Ex-situ X-ray diffraction (not shown) was also used to optimize compositionally graded buffer growth conditions 54,55.

Processing. Photolithographic processing of the 6J IMM devices was performed as outlined schematically in Fig. 2. Gold was electroplated over the entire back of the inverted device for a back contact. This side was attached to a silicon wafer (used as a flat rigid handle) with a low viscosity epoxy, then the GaAs substrate was removed by etching to a GaInP stop etch with a selective NH4OH:H2O2 etchant. The substrate could be reused for cost savings if epitaxial liftoff 56,57 or spalling techniques 58 were used instead. The top Ni/Au metal grid was electrodeposited using standard lithography, and devices were mesa isolated down to the back gold using a non-selective HBr:Br:H2O etchant. The top GaInNAs/GaAs contact layer was removed using the metal grids as a mask and a 4-layer MgF2/ZnS/MgF2/ZnS broadband antireflective coating was deposited by thermal evaporation 59.

Solar cell performance measurements. Standard characterization was performed by the III-V fabrication team and independently verified by the Cell & Module Performance (CMP) team at NREL. Both results closely agreed. Certified results from the CMP team are shown in Supplementary Figures 3-5.

EQE measurements of each junction were obtained by light- biasing all other junctions with high brightness LEDs (III-V team) or filtered white light (CMP team) as well as voltage biasing to overcome the effects of reverse-bias breakdown. The 3rd, 5th, and 6th junctions showed reverse-bias breakdown in the power producing quadrant and so were voltage-biased slightly less than the voltage at maximum power point (MPP) 60. The effects of luminescent coupling were removed as described elsewhere 61. The EQE results from the III-V team are shown and integrated in Fig. 4a.

The cell area is clearly defined, as the IMM device is etched down to the back gold. The maximum area was measured under a dedicated microscope system with calibrations governed by our ISO certification using the CMP team's standard protocol for area measurements. Electroluminescence for subcell voltage analysis. EL of the 6J IMM was measured as a function of forward-bias current injection. A spectroradiometer was used to collect a small solid angle of light emitted from the solar cell and calibrated relative to the measured dark JV curve of the total 6J IMM as described in 46,49. The fraction of the emitted EL light coupled from each junction to the next junction was experimentally determined by systematically varying the intensity of light on each junction while measuring the full JV curve and used to correct the EL analysis 46.

Transmission Electron Microscopy. Focused ion beam (FIB) TEM cross-section samples were prepared in a FEI NanoLab 200 dual beam FIB workstation using standard lift out methods after protecting the sample surface first with electron beam and then ion beam deposited Pt layers. Samples were finished off in the FIB with a Ga+ ion beam energy of 3 kV. Ga+ ion FIB damage was removed by low energy (< 1 kV) Ar+ ion milling in a Fischione Model 1040 NanoMill with the sample cooled using liquid nitrogen. Diffraction contrast transmission electron microscope (TEM) imaging was performed in a FEI Tecnai G2 30 S-Twin TEM operated at 300 kV.

Cathodoluminescence (CL). Plan-view CL images were obtained from unprocessed 6J IMM epilayers etched down to the metamorphic junction of interest. Panchromatic CL images were acquired using a parabolic mirror collection system to direct emission to a liquid nitrogen cooled Ge photodiode. An accelerating voltage of 15kV and beam current of 1.5nA were used to excite excess charge carriers in the samples that were cooled to 80K to enhance emission intensity. Threading dislocation density was calculated by averaging dislocation counts over an area of 4.3 x 10-3 cm-2. The 4th and 5th junctions had well-defined spots and a threading dislocation density of 106 cm-2 was measured. The 6th

junction appears to have a dislocation density below 106 cm-2, but the error is highest in this sample due to differences in dislocation distribution and surface roughness, and so we conservatively estimated a density of 106 cm-2.

Secondary Ion Mass Spectroscopy (SIMS). SIMS was performed using a Cameca IMS 7f instrument. The depth profile in Fig. 3b used a 10kV oxygen primary ion beam with an impact energy of 5kV as the sample was at 5kV. The Zn signal in Fig. 3b is quantified and references the left axis. The In signal is not quantified and references the right axis. Both Zn and In were measured as positive secondary ions. The Zn signal was quantified using relative sensitivity factors generated from Zn ion implant standards in GaInAs and GaInP for the corresponding layers.
