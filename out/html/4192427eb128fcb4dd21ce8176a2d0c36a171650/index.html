CONSPECTUS: Electrochemical organic oxidation reactions are highly appealing because protons are often effective terminal electron acceptors, thereby avoiding undesirable stoichiometric oxidants. These reactions are often plagued by high overpotentials, however, that greatly limit their utility. Single-electron transfer (SET) from organic molecules generates highenergy radical-cations. Formation of such intermediates often requires electrode potentials far above the thermodynamic potentials of the reaction and frequently causes decomposition and/or side reactions of ancillary functional groups. In this Account, we show how electrocatalytic electron-proton transfer mediators (EPTMs) address this challenge. EPTMs bypass the formation of radical-cation intermediates by supporting mechanisms that operate at electrode potentials much lower (≥1 V) than analogous direct electrolysis reactions. The stable aminoxyl radical TEMPO (2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine N-oxyl) is an effective mediator for electrochemical alcohol oxidation, and we have employed such processes for applications ranging from pharmaceutical synthesis to biomass conversion. A complementary electrochemical alcohol oxidation method employs a cooperative Cu/TEMPO mediator system that operates at 0.5 V lower electrode potential than the TEMPO-only mediated process. This difference, which arises from a different catalytic mechanism, rationalizes the broad functional group tolerance of Cu/TEMPO-based aerobic alcohol oxidation catalysts. Aminoxyl mediators address long-standing challenges in the "Shono oxidation", an important method for a-C–H oxidation of tertiary amides and carbamates. Shono oxidations are initiated by a high-potential SET step that limits their utility. Aminoxyl-mediated Shono-type oxidations have been developed that operate at much lower potentials and tolerate diverse functional groups. Analogous reactivity underlies a-C–H cyanation of secondary cyclic amines, a new method that

Page 3

enables efficient diversification of piperidine-based pharmaceutical building blocks and preparation of non-natural amino acids. Electrochemical oxidations of benzylic C–H bonds are commonly initiated by SET to generate radical cations, but such methods are again plagued by large overpotentials. Mediated electrolysis methods that promote hydrogen-atom-transfer (HAT) from benzylic C–H bonds to Fe-oxo species and phthalimide N-oxyl (PINO) support C–H oxygenation, iodination, and oxidative-coupling reactions. A complementary method merges photochemistry with electrochemistry to achieve amidation of C(sp3)–H bonds. This unique process operates at much lower overpotentials compatible with diverse functional groups. These results have broad implications for organic electrochemistry, highlighting the importance of "overpotential" considerations and the prospects for expanding synthetic utility by using mediators to bypass high-energy outer-sphere electron-transfer mechanisms. Principles demonstrated here for oxidation are equally relevant to electrochemical reductions.

1. Introduction Oxidation reactions are among the most important transformations in organic chemistry because of their ability to increase chemical complexity, incorporate new heteroatoms and other functional groups into organic molecules, and streamline synthetic routes to target molecules. Nonetheless, challenges associated with poor reaction selectivity and/or functional group compatibility often restrict the effectiveness of existing oxidation methods with complex molecules, such as those encountered within the pharmaceutical and agrochemical industries. Common chemical oxidants, such as metal oxyanions (e.g., Cr2O7–, MnO4–) and hypervalent iodine reagents, generate toxic or otherwise undesirable stoichiometric byproducts that limit large-scale applications. 1 Progress continues to be made in the development of "green" chemoselective oxidation methods, 2 - 4 but further advances are needed, and electrochemistry offers unique opportunities in this domain. Many electrochemical oxidation reactions employ protons as the terminal electron acceptor, generating H2 as the sole byproduct of the reaction. This appealing atom economy is complemented by the tunability of the anode redox potential, which enables precise control over the rate and selectivity of electron transfer (ET). The latter feature is highly advantageous for electrochemical reactions initiated by single-electron transfer (SET), which correspond to a large fraction of existing electrochemical oxidation methods.5–9 The beneficial characteristics of electrochemical reactions are offset by their unique challenges. In addition to the requirement for specialized or non-standard equipment and instrumentation, the mechanism of outer-sphere electron transfer at a heterogeneous electrode presents kinetic challenges for organic oxidation reactions. Most oxidation reactions in organic chemistry consist of two-electron processes, and the net reaction corresponds to dehydrogenation or dehydrogenative coupling of the substrate(s). Initiation of these reactions by SET generates

Page 5

high-energy radical-cations, but the high electrode potentials required to access these intermediates often limits the functional group compatibility and synthetic utility of the reactions. In this Account, we highlight the importance of this "overpotential problem" in organic electrochemistry and show how "electron-proton transfer mediators (EPTMs)" may be used to address this problem. The discussion begins with a survey of thermodynamic and overpotential considerations relevant to organic electrochemistry and EPTMs and is followed by a presentation of several case studies that illustrate important applications of these concepts together with their implications beyond these specific applications.

The standard potential for the oxidation reaction of interest (e.g., Table 1a-c) corresponds to the anode potential that will lead to an isoergic equilibrium (DG° = 0) between the oxidized and reduced molecule under standard state conditions, when protons are reduced to H2 at the cathode. As shown by the Nernst equation (eq 1), the anode potential must be increased by >60 mV to drive the reaction to completion (i.e., >99:1 product:starting material). In practice, much higher applied anode potentials are required to overcome kinetic barriers and achieve reasonable reaction rates. The difference between the potential applied during the reaction and the equilibrium potential under the specified reaction conditions (which often differ from standard state conditions) is defined as the "overpotential". Overpotential is a prominent concept within the field of

Page 7

electrochemical energy conversion because electrochemical overpotentials limit the energy efficiency of fuel cells and electrolysis devices, and they play an important role in the performance characteristics and economic viability of these systems. In contrast, overpotential is seldom discussed in the field of organic electrochemistry, but it also plays an important role in these reactions, albeit for different reasons. The concept of overpotential is illustrated by the energy diagram for a direct electrolysis reaction in Figure 1A, which proceeds via outer-sphere electron transfer (ET) from the organic molecule to the electrode, generating a radical-cation intermediate. Substantial overpotential is typically needed to access this high-energy intermediate and promote the reaction with a reasonable rate. The magnitude of this overpotential has direct implications for the synthetic scope of these reactions because many molecules of interest will have ancillary functional groups that could undergo decomposition or deleterious side reactions at the potentials required to generate the radical cation intermediate. This "overpotential problem" severely constrains the utility of many electrochemical organic oxidation reactions. Indirect/mediated electrolysis methods provide an alternative strategy to conduct electrochemical oxidation reactions.18–20 Such methods employ a mediator (alternately called an electrocatalyst) that undergoes heterogeneous electron transfer at an electrode and then promotes homogeneous oxidation of the substrate in solution. Several different types of mediators have been developed (Figure 2). ET mediators, such as triarylamines18–21 and ferrocene,22,23 serve as electrontransfer shuttles and allow SET-initiated substrate oxidation to take place in bulk solution rather than at the surface of an electrode. The benefits of this approach have been elaborated in a recent review by Franke and Little,20 and they include prospects for improved reaction selectivity and the ability to operate with electrode potentials several hundred mV lower than reactions conducted by direct electrolysis.24

Aminoxyl mediators exhibit good reactivity for alcohol oxidation, but their efficiency in preparative electrolysis is limited by diffusion of the catalyst to the surface of electrode.39 For example, cyclic voltammetry (CV) studies revealed turnover frequencies (TOFs) of 1228 h-1 for ACT-mediated benzyl alcohol oxidation, but this rate only reflects the catalyst species present at the electrode surface. TEMPOH, RCHO Shono oxidation is among the most widely employed methods in organic electrochemistry, as

it allows for functionalization of C–H bonds adjacent to nitrogen in saturated heterocycles, with applications ranging from natural product construction to drug metabolite synthesis.53,54 These reactions are initiated by SET to generate a radical-cation, and the overall ET/PT/ET sequence generates an iminium intermediate that reacts with MeOH or H2O to afford the corresponding N,Oacetal (Figure 8A). The high applied potential required to initiate SET from the substrate, however, limits the substrate functional group compatibility, in addition to limiting the scope of

Page 17

nucleophiles. For example, the presence of a 4-methoxyphenyl substituent on piperidine methyl carbamate completely inhibits the reaction (Figure 8A). Innovative strategies have been developed to address this limitation, including “cation pool”, electroauxiliary, and flow-based methods,8,55,56,57 but these approaches introduce other constraints. Mechanistically, the ET/PT/ET sequence corresponds to a net hydride transfer (H–T), and we reasoned that a hydride-transfer mediator might provide the means to address some of the constraints that have been encountered in these reactions. Previous studies had shown that a-C–H functionalization of activated carbamates and amides (i.e., tetrahydroisoquinolines) could be accomplished with stoichiometric oxoammonium reagents 58 or under biphasic electrochemical conditions with TEMPO/Br2-based mediators. 59 TEMPO derivatives proved unsuccessful as mediators for the Shono oxidation; however, electrondeficient ABNO derivatives, including PhCO2ABNO and ketoABNO, were effective in these reactions (Figure 8B).60 Piperidine derived carbamates bearing diverse functional groups, such as methoxyphenyl and heterocycles, proceed smoothly under the mediated electrolysis conditions to afford the desired products in good yields (Figure 8C).

3.4 Electrochemical a-C–H Cyanation of Secondary Piperidines Piperidines are the most common heterocycles found in commercial drugs, and a survey of several hundred compounds in a building-block library provided by AbbVie highlighted secondary piperidines as the most abundant class of available compounds. Direct functionalization of C–H bonds adjacent to the nitrogen atom in secondary piperidines provides an appealing target for structural diversification and increasing molecule complexity. We envisioned that aminoxyl19

Page 20

mediated dehydrogenation of the secondary piperidine in the presence of cyanide nucleophile could enable functional-group-tolerant a-C–H cyanation (Figure 10A). Numerous precedents exist for a-C–H cyanation of amine derivatives, but useful examples are almost entirely limited to Nprotected (e.g. N-Boc) or N-aryl/alkyl tertiary amines.61 The desired reactivity was realized by using ABNO as mediator, TMSCN as cyanide source, and methanol as an additive.62 ABNO+ generated at the anode mediates dehydrogenation of the piperidine to generate the imine intermediate, which undergoes addition of cyanide to afford the desired product. The ABNOH byproduct undergoes reoxidation to ABNO+ at the anode, and this process is facilitated by a Brønsted base, which is produced from the hydrogen evolution reaction at the cathode. Constant current potential traces provide valuable insights into the ABNO-mediated reaction (Figure 10B). Under the optimized reaction conditions (red trace), the anodic potential is stable at ~150−250 mV throughout the reaction. In the absence of MeOH (blue trace), the potential rise after initial 1e– oxidation of ABNO to ABNO+ is attributed slow proton-coupled oxidation of ABNOH to ABNO+ in the absence of an effective Brønsted base (i.e., methoxide). In the absence of both MeOH and ABNO (green trace), 700-800 mV higher electrode potential is needed to sustain the set current. The yield of product is substantially lower under the latter two conditions. These observations highlight the importance of considering the chemistry at the counterelectrode reaction when optimizing electrochemical reactions.

4. C–H Bond Functionalization with HAT Mediators The C–H cleavage reactions described in section 3 achieve lower overpotentials by mediating concerted hydride transfer (1H+/2e–). In complementary efforts, we have explored prospects to achieve similar benefits via mediated hydrogen-atom-transfer (HAT). 4.1 Fe-TAML-Mediated Oxidation of Benzylic C–H Bonds and Alcohols Electrochemical proton-coupled oxidation of Fe–OH2 complexes provides a means to generate high-valent Fe-oxo species.63 Such reactivity has been extensively investigated in the context of electrocatalytic water oxidation (Figure 12),64-66 but preparative-scale electrochemical oxidation of organic molecules involving this approach had not be demonstrated. Generation of the Fe-oxo species electrochemically bypasses the need for stoichiometric chemical oxidants because the

Page 23

reaction is coupled to proton reduction at the cathode, generating H2 as the sole byproduct. After evaluating several previously reported Fe complexes, we focused on an iron complex bearing TAML (TAML = tetraamido macrocyclic ligand), an ancillary ligand pioneered by Collins and co-workers for iron-catalyzed oxidation of organic molecules with hydrogen peroxide. 67 CV studies revealed the presence of FeIV/III and FeV/IV redox processes at 800 mV and 1250 mV respectively (vs Ag/AgCl). Bulk-electrolysis C–H oxygenation and alcohol dehydrogenation of a number of different substrates proceed at both potentials, but the rate is more rapid at high potential, implicating much higher activity of a FeV-oxo species. 6. Summary and Outlook The results presented herein highlight the ability of mediators, particularly those capable of mediating hydride and hydrogen-atom transfer reactions, to significantly lower the overpotentials 30

Page 31

required for electrochemical oxidation of organic molecules. Prominent applications include chemoselective oxidative functionalization of C–H bonds adjacent to nitrogen in piperidine derivatives and other saturated heterocycles, oxygenation and iodination of benzylic C–H bonds, and intramolecular amination of benzylic and aliphatic C–H bonds. The lower operating potentials provide the basis for much broader functional group combability and synthetic scope. We anticipate that broader adoption of the principles elaborated herein will greatly expand the utility and impact of electrochemical redox reactions in laboratory- and process-scale applications.
