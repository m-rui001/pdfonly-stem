Abstract. Fe-N-C catalysts are the most promising platinum group metal (PGM)-free oxygenreduction reaction (ORR) catalysts but suffer from a low density of active metal sites, causing inadequate mass activity. Also, two types of active sites co-exist in catalysts, in which pyrrolic Ncoordinated FeN4 sites (S1) are intrinsically more active but less stable than pyridinic Ncoordinated sites (S2). Hence, increasing site density with improved intrinsic activity and stability remains challenging to break the activity-stability trade-off. Here, we report a highly active FeN-C catalyst prepared by adding optimal H2 to the traditional inert pyrolysis atmosphere (i.e., 10% H2/Ar forming gas), comprising significantly increased active site density. It also contains a relatively higher content of stable S2, therefore presenting encouraging stability improvement. Notably, based on extensive characterization, we proposed that the S1 and S2 sites are hosted in different areas of carbon in catalysts. The intrinsically stable S2 sites are likely in well-graphitized double graphene layers, while S1 sites exist in less-graphitized carbon. H2 could favor removing unstable S1 sites and retaining stable S2 sites, improving catalyst durability during the ORR. We further theoretically elucidated that, during thermal activation, H2 favors the formation of both S1 and S2 active sites with increased density. H2 also stabilizes the pyridinic N more significantly than pyrrolic N under high temperatures (e.g., 1100 oC), yielding more S2 sites with better stability. 2

Thermal activation under forming gas leads to shortened Fe-N bonds of FeN4 moieties, favoring intrinsic stability enhancement. A membrane electrode assembly (MEA) containing this Fe-N-C cathode catalyst demonstrated compelling activity and improved durability, generating a current density of 50.8 mA cm−2 at 0.9 ViR-free (H2-O2) and 191 mA cm−2 at 0.8 V (H2-air), the best activity of current PGM-free catalysts. The highly active Fe-N-C catalyst, therefore, concurrently demonstrated improved stability during life tests and accelerated stability tests (ASTs). This work represents a critical step toward developing viable PGM-free catalysts with simultaneous high activity and enhanced stability for proton-exchange membrane fuel cells.

Introduction The development of platinum-group-metal (PGM)-free catalysts significantly benefit the cost reduction of hydrogen proton-exchange membrane fuel cells (PEMFCs) for sustainable large-scale applications.1,2 The atomically-dispersed and nitrogen-coordinated FeN4 sites hosted in carbon (Fe-N-C) have exhibited encouraging activity and stability for the cathodic oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) in the challenging acidic environment of the PEMFC.3-8 However, over the past several decades, performance and durability improvements of Fe-N-C catalysts are still significantly limited due to the insufficient understanding of active sites, low site density, poor stability, and an activity-stability trade-off.9 Like Pt active sites, the adsorption strength of O2 and ORR intermediates on FeN4 active sites is too strong.10,11 The activation barrier to cleave the O-O bond is too high for the preferred 4e− transfer pathway. In addition, achieving comparable or even better stability to current Pt catalysts remains a significant challenge, primarily due to the possible demetallation via Fe-N bond breaking during the ORR.12 Regulating the electronic and geometric structures of FeN4 active sites 3

Page 4

to modulate adsorption energies, reduce activation energies, and strengthen Fe-N bonds is critical for improving the intrinsic activity and stability of FeN4 sites.13,14 One of the recent breakthroughs is the identification of two types of FeN4 sites in Fe-N-C catalysts based on operando Mössbauer and X-ray absorption spectroscopy experiments.15 An FeN4C12 moiety contains pyrrolic N ligations, often denoted as the S1 site. The other FeN4C10 moiety with pyridinic N environments is denoted as the S2 site.15 S1 sites are more intrinsically active but are irreversibly and rapidly degraded to inactive ferric oxides during the ORR, causing dramatic activity loss. S2 sites are more stable against demetallation but less active due to stronger adsorption of oxygen and intermediates.6 Therefore, it is highly desirable to synthesize a catalyst containing significantly increased densities of S1 and S2 sites with enhanced intrinsic activity and stability simultaneously. However, achieving this goal using traditional synthesis approaches is nearly impossible based on previously reported catalysts in literature.16,17 Herein, we discovered that local carbon and nitrogen coordination structures of FeN4 sites and their site density could be regulated by adding H2 to a traditional inert Ar atmosphere (i.e., forming gas) during the catalyst synthesis. Significantly, due to the accelerated carbon gasification process in the presence of H2 during the thermal activation, the use of forming gas could favor the retention of stable S2 sites in well-graphitized carbon and remove the unstable S1 sites in amorphous carbon in catalysts. Unlike previously identified FeN4 sites, the S1 and S2 sites contain shortened Fe-N bonds due to the hydrogenation of carbon atoms adjacent to the FeN4 moieties during the thermal activation under the forming gas. This critical change in Fe-N bonds was experimentally verified using X-ray absorption spectroscopy, which was theoretically predicted to yield enhanced intrinsic stability while maintaining high activity. The resulting Fe-N-C catalyst thus leads to exceptional activity and improved stability. A

Results and Discussion Catalyst synthesis, activity, and stability Pyrolyzing precursors under an inert atmosphere (e.g., N2 or Ar) has long been the prevailing approach for forming FeN4 active sites in Fe-N-C catalysts.18-20 Dodelet and Kramm et al. found that treatment in NH3/N2 following this pyrolysis step can efficiently enhance ORR activity due to the formation of substantial micropores to host increased numbers of active sites.21-24 In principle, NH3 could be further decomposed into H2 and N2 at the elevated temperature of up to 900oC during

We hypothesized that, besides the benefits of directly using NH3, the optimal H2 content combined with controlled heating temperatures and duration may play a role in tuning active site structures and locations. Here, we uncover that the optimal H2 content in forming gas (FG) (i.e., H2/Ar mixture) can efficiently enhance ORR activity and stability by simultaneously increasing metal site density, modifying carbon morphologies and structures, and regulating the Fe-N bond structures as illuminated in Figure 1a. During the catalyst synthesis, Fe2O3-enclosed ZIF-8 nanocrystals were used as a precursor due to the effectiveness of forming atomically dispersed single ion sites with increased density.6 When the H2 content in the forming gas was increased from 0 (i.e., Ar atmosphere) to 2 mol.% (denoted as Fe-N-C-2%FG), the ORR activity of the catalyst in 0.5 M H2SO4 electrolyte was greatly promoted (Figure 1b), evidenced by an increase of half-wave-potential (E1/2) from 0.84 to 0.88 V vs. RHE under established testing conditions (600 µg cm−2, 25 oC, and 900 r.p.m). When the H2 content was increased to 5 mol. %, the catalyst exhibited an E1/2 up to 0.92 V, outperforming a baseline Pt/C catalyst at a standard loading of 60 µgPt cm−2 in an acidic electrolyte (0.1 M HClO4). Further increasing H2 content to 10 mol.% only led to a minor improvement in ORR activity, achieving an E1/2 of 0.93 V with better reproducibility (Supplementary Figure 1). Although reductive pyrolysis atmospheres were explored in the preparation of Fe-N-C catalysts,23,24,26,27 notably, a combination of the ZIF-8/Fe2O3 precursors, optimal H2 content, and favorable thermal conditions is the key to producing active Fe-N-C catalysts in this work (Supplementary Table 1). Catalyst Characterization for Mechanistic Understanding The newly developed Fe-N-C-FG catalysts demonstrated compelling performance and durability in hydrogen and direct methanol fuel cells. We further carried out extensive characterization to elucidate active sites and relevant mechanisms of performance improvements. First, 57Fe Mössbauer spectroscopy, which is highly sensitive to the oxidation state, electron configuration, and atomic coordination environment of Fe nuclei,37-39 was acquired at both room and low temperature (6.5 K) to reveal the impact of pyrolysis atmosphere on coordinating environments and local structures of FeN4 sites in catalysts (Figure 3, Supplementary Table 9 and 10). Generally, the Mössbauer spectra of the Fe-N-C catalysts were mainly fit with two doublets associated with single Fe sites coordinated with nitrogen. In addition, insignificant singlet and sextet peaks were assigned to Fe nanoparticles remaining in catalysts. In particular, D1 doublets with lower isomer shifts (δ) and quadrupole splitting (ΔEQ) values were assigned to the high-spin 11

Page 12

S1 moiety as discussed before, in which a Fe nucleus coordinates with four porphyrinic (i.e., pyrrolic) nitrogen ligands. The D2 doublets featuring larger QS values were assigned to a low- or intermediate-spin S2 moiety, where the Fe nucleus coordinates with four pyridinic nitrogen ligands. At first, the S1/S2 ratios were decreased from 1.56 for the Fe-N-C-Ar catalyst to 0.92 for the FeN-C-10%FG catalyst, indicating that the highly stable S2-type FeN4 sites become dominant due to forming gas. At room temperature, superparamagnetic Fe-nano-oxides contribute with a doublet signal similar to the ‘true’ D1 signal.40,41 To exclude the interference Fe-nano-oxides, lowtemperature 57Fe Mössbauer spectroscopy was performed at 6.5 K (Figure 3b and Supplementary Table 11), showing no superparamagnetic Fe-nano-oxides in the Fe-N-C-10%FG catalyst. To quantitatively determine the absolute S1 and S2 sites in various catalysts, the Fe content was determined by ICP-OES (Supplementary Table 12). The Fe content increased from 0.15 wt. % in 57Fe-N-C-Ar to 0.73 wt. % in 57Fe-N-C-10%FG. Notably, the Zn content of the 57Fe-N-C catalysts decreased to beneath the detection limit when the H2 percentage was > 2 mol.%. This is in general agreement with the increase of side density by a factor of 4.3 found by electrochemical measurements (see Figure 1c). Based on HR-TEM images (Figures 6b, 6c, 6e, and 6f), the ZIF-8-derived Fe-N-C catalysts often contain two types of carbon structures in catalysts: double graphene layers separated by illstructured single graphene layers (Supplementray Figure 15). The inter-distance of the layers in the double graphene layer is of the order of 0.48 ± 0.10 nm, which is somewhat more significant than a typical inter-planar distance, d = 0.335 nm, measured in graphite. The carbon layers with less graphitization react faster than the better-graphitized ones. Usually, FeN4 sites need carbon support to carry out the ORR in the catalysts. If the carbon support disappears due to gasification, the catalytic sites will also disappear. Upon addition of H2 during the pyrolysis, Fe sites located in the mesopores of carbon could be decreased due to carbon gasification, while those in the double graphene layer are hardly lost. The Mössbauer spectroscopy analyses enable us to identify the specific structures of FeN4 sites in catalysts. Theoretical elucidation via DFT calculations We further performed the first principles density functional theory (DFT) calculations to understand the influence of thermal activation atmosphere on the electrochemical activity and stability of S1 and S2-typed FeN4 sites embedded in carbon. Considering that the forming gas environment used in our synthesis process could introduce additional hydrogen bonded with carbon, we constructed two new S1-4H and S2-4H types of sites with four hydrogen atoms bonded with the carbon atoms immediately adjacent to the FeN4 moieties (Figure 6a, Supplementary Note 2 and Figure 16). Enhanced stability. In addition, DFT calculations were performed to study the stability of these possible active site structures against demetallation under electrochemical environments. The proposed demetallation process of Fe is presented in Supplementary Note 3, Table 16, and Figure 18,57 which shows that Fe, together with adsorbed O2, moves away from an N4-coordinated state into an N2-coordinated state. At the same time, the remaining two N atoms form N-H bonds with protons from the electrolyte. The Gibbs free energy for this demetallation process was predicted to be −0.02 eV for the S1 type FeN4 site, lower than that of 0.26 eV for the S1-4H site (Figure 6d). This result indicates that the stability against demetallation of the S1 site could be significantly improved by hydrogenating those carbon atoms immediately adjacent to a FeN4 19

Page 20

moiety, along with shortened Fe-N bond caused by H2 adsorption during the thermal activation. In comparison, the S2-4H site shows a Gibbs free energy for the demetallation process of 1.31 eV, slightly higher than that of the S2 type FeN4 site (1.27 eV), unveiling that the incorporation of hydrogen would enhance the stability of S2 site against demetallation just to a small degree. This computational finding could be ascribed to the reduction of Fe-N bond length by ~0.04 Å in S1 and that by ~0.00 Å in S2 type FeN4 site after hydrogen attached to carbon. Therefore, our DFT calculations support our experimental observations and provide additional insights, elucidating that the new S1 and S2 sites in the Fe-N-C-FG catalyst have enhanced intrinsic stability than traditional FeN4 sites. We predicted that the Fe-N bond length was 2.09 Å in S1-type FeN4 and 2.05 Å in the S1-4H site, whereas the S2-4H site showed the same Fe-N bond length of 1.89 Å as S2 site. This prediction is consistent with the reduction in R-values of the Fe-N bond in our XAS analysis of the Fe-N-C-FG catalyst. Further electronic structure analysis indicates that the bonding of H on C atoms in the S1-4H site could shift all the anti-bonding orbitals of central Fe above the Fermi energy level and thus lead to stronger Fe-N bonds with shortened length. In addition, the higher concentration of S1 and S2 FeN4 sites modified by 4H is one of the reasons to improve overall ORR activity in the Fe-N-C-FG catalyst. Effect of adjacent metallic Fe. As Fe NPs have been observed in the highly active Fe-N-C-FG catalyst, DFT calculations were further performed to elucidate the role of Fe NPs encapsulated by graphene layers on the ORR activity of the catalysts. According to Mossbauer spectroscopy and electron microscopy analyses, the Fe NP existed in the Fe-N-C-FG catalyst and was encapsulated in compact carbon shells (up to 14 layers). In our calculations, we used Fe(110) surface to model the extended facets of Fe NPs since (110) was predicted to be the energetically most favorable surface in body-centered cubic Fe.58 For simplification, only one layer of graphene was placed between a graphene layer containing a FeN4 moiety and Fe(110) surface (Supplementary Figure 22). We denoted this model as FeN4-G-Fe (110) and predicted its limiting potential for ORR to be 0.68 eV (Figure 6f). In comparison, we predicted the limiting potential for ORR on a FeN4 site without a Fe surface underneath to be 0.66 eV, indicating that the encapsulated Fe(110) surface had negligible impact on the ORR limiting potential of the FeN4 site. Conclusions Current Fe-N-C catalysts still cannot provide comparable performance to replace Pt in compelling hydrogen fuel cells. The primary limitations are due to insufficient metal site density and the likely activity-stability trade-off simultaneously containing active/unstable S1 sites and stable/less active

Page 22

S2 sites. In this work, we developed an effective approach that significantly increases active metal site density and begins to break the activity-stability trade-off by using an optimal H2/Ar forming gas for thermal activation. The newly developed Fe-N-C catalyst exhibited exceptionally high ORR activity in MEAs and generated 50.8 mA cm−2 at 0.9 ViR-free (1.0 bar O2) and 191.0 mA cm−2 at 0.8 V (1.0 bar air), significantly exceeding DOE 2025 targets. Notably, the initial activity and performance are nearly comparable to a typical Pt/C catalyst in RDE and MEA. Although the catalyst still degraded during the harsh and long-term stability ASTs, the significantly enhanced stability is very encouraging, superior to other highly active Fe-N-C and traditional Pt/C cathodes. Extensive spectroscopy techniques and DFT calculations have been combined to elucidate the origin of improved activity and stability. We theoretically elucidated that the presence of H2 facilitates the formation of S1 and S2-typed FeN4 sites with significantly increased active site density. Importantly, hydrogenating those carbon atoms immediately adjacent to FeN4 moieties during the thermal activation under forming gas leads to shortened Fe-N bonds, which was further experimentally verified by using X-ray absorption spectroscopy. The shortened Fe-N bonds contribute to enhanced intrinsic stability against the demetallation during the ORR. Also, the hydrogenated pyridinic N-coordinated FeN4 sites (S2) are more thermally stable than S1 sites than pyrrolic N-coordinated S1 sites, exhibiting enhanced stability. Meanwhile, based on extensive experimental characterization, we suggested that these two different S1 and S2 sites may locate in different areas of carbon in catalysts. The intrinsically stable pyridinic N-coordinated FeN4 sites (S2) are easily embedded in the highly graphitized double 22

Page 23

graphene layers, while the less stable but more active pyrrolic N-coordinated FeN4 sites (S1) are primarily hosted in less-graphitized and defective carbon planes. The newly proposed theory can well explain the promotional role of H2 in promoting the gasification of less stable carbon in catalysts and S2 site formation with increased mesoporosity and enhanced stability during the ORR. Therefore, new types of S1 and S2 FeN4 with increased site density existing in the Fe-N-C catalyst simultaneously contribute to the compelling activity and improved stability, for the first time, demonstrating a great promise of PGM-free catalysts to replace the Pt in PEMFCs for various applications.
