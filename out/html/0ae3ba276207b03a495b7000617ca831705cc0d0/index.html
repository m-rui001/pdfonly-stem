Abstract Electrochemically converting nitrate ions, a widely distributed nitrogen source in industrial wastewater and polluted groundwater, to ammonia represents a sustainable route for both wastewater treatment and ammonia generation. However, it is currently hindered by low catalytic activities especially under low nitrate concentrations. Here we report a high-performance Ru dispersed Cu nanowire catalyst which delivers an industrial-relevant nitrate reduction current of 1 A cm–2 while maintaining a high NH3 Faradaic efficiency of 93%. More importantly, this high nitrate reduction catalytic activity enables over 99% nitrate conversion to ammonia, from an industrial wastewater level of 2000 ppm to a drinkable water level < 50 ppm, while still maintaining over 90% Faradaic efficiency. Coupling the nitrate reduction effluent stream with an air stripping process, we successfully obtained high purity solid NH4Cl and liquid NH3 solution products, suggesting a practical approach to convert wastewater nitrate into valuable ammonia products. Density functional theory calculations reveal that the highly dispersed Ru atoms provide active nitrate reduction sites while the surrounding Cu sites can suppress the main side reaction, the hydrogen evolution reaction.

Main Introduction Ammonia (NH3) is a versatile and foundational compound in modern society, used in everything from chemical synthesis and fertilizers to fuels and clean energy carriers1-4. Today, global ammonia demand exceeds 150 million tons per year5,6, while consuming 2% of the world energy and releasing 1.4% of global CO2 emissions6-8. This is mainly due to the energy-intensive Haber-Bosch ammonia synthesis process that currently dominates the worldwide production. In the Haber-Bosch process, steam-reformed hydrogen (H2) reacts with N2 under elevated temperatures (~500°C) and pressures (>100 atm), which not only contributes a significant carbon footprint, but also requires large infrastructure9-11. Recently, electrocatalytic methods have emerged as a clean energy route for decentralized ammonia production at room temperature, at a variety of infrastructure scales, and potentially driven by locally generated renewable energy12-15. While traditional ammonia production route is highly likely to persist in the near future considering the gigantic global demand, the electrochemical synthesis could significantly contribute to decarbonizing ammonia production. Electrochemical N2 reduction reaction (NRR) for ammonia synthesis has been extensively studied for the past decade16-20. However, the current NH3 production rate through NRR is extremely low (~0.1 mA cm–2) due to the sluggish dissociation step of the highly stable N≡N bond15,18,21. This low production rate is far from industrial requirements and may even make experimental quantifications unreliable due to trace impurities21-23. Conversely, the electrochemical nitrate reduction reaction (NO3RR) does not require the dissociation of high energy bonds, and thus achieves faster reaction rates for NH3 production3,23-25. Moreover, nitrate (NO3–) is an abundant nitrogen source particularly in industrial wastewater and polluted groundwater that contributes to the global nitrogen cycle imbalance26-28. Therefore, NO3RR not only stands out as a promising pathway for renewable NH3 production, but simultaneously facilitates the denitrification of wastewater and balances the perturbed nitrogen cycle23,29,30. Developing efficient electrocatalysts for nitrate reduction is a prerequisite. Commonly NO3RR is directed through the half-cell reaction: NO3― + 6H2O + 8e―→NH3 + 9OH― #(1)

Page 4

where the thermodynamic potential is 0.69 V versus reversible hydrogen electrode (vs. RHE). The goal of catalysts is to direct reduction all the way to ammonia, while minimizing side products from different NO3RR pathways, or hydrogen from hydrogen evolution reaction (HER), to achieve high NH3 Faradaic efficiencies (FEs). Previous studies have demonstrated different catalysts for selective NO3–-to-NH3 conversion31-40, but most of them were evaluated under relatively high nitrate concentrations where the competing reactions can be minimized. However, many practical nitrate resources such as industrial wastewater has low concentrations ranging from hundreds to thousands of ppm29,30. To date, it is still challenging to achieve both high current densities (> 100 mA cm–2) and high ammonia FE (> 90%) under low nitrate concentrations due to the vigorous HER competition. Here we developed a NO3RR electrocatalyst by dispersing Ru atoms into a Cu nanowire matrix (Ru-CuNW) for highly selective and active ammonia generation under low nitrate concentrations. Our Ru-CuNW catalyst organically combines Ru’s low nitrate activation barrier and Cu’s inert HER activity. As a result, our Ru-CuNW delivers an industrial-relevant NH3 production rate of ~ 1 A cm–2 while maintaining a high NH3 FE of 96% when operating at NO3– concentrations of typical industrial wastewater (2000 ppm), outperforming previously reported catalysts31-40. More importantly, this high NO3RR activity enables an over 99% nitrate conversion to ammonia from an industrial wastewater level to a drinkable water level. By coupling electrochemical NO3RR with air stripping, we demonstrate a full conversion process from low-concentration nitrate sources to highpurity ammonia products for direct downstream applications. Our density functional theory (DFT) study reveals that the high nitrate-to-ammonia performance arises from the highly dispersed Ru active site, while the supporting Cu sites suppress the parasitic side reactions.

Main text Catalyst synthesis and morphology characterizations. Ru-CuNW catalyst was synthesized through a cation exchange method on Cu nanowire matrix (Fig. 1a; see Methods). Cu nanowire (CuNW) and Ru nanoparticle (RuNP) control samples were also synthesized and characterized to compare catalytic performances (Supplementary Figs. 1a and 2). Other control samples, including different Ru doping levels, and different noble metal dopants such as Rh, Os, and Ir, which have been proposed by previous simulation studies to be active NRR candidates16,17,41, were also prepared, characterized, and evaluated (Supplementary Figs. 1b–d, 3–5). Scanning electron microscope (SEM) images (Supplementary Fig. 6) showed the morphology changes from copper hydroxide nanowire (Cu(OH)2NW) to Ru-doped CuO nanowire (Ru-CuONW); Transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Supplementary Figs. 7–9) and high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM, Fig. 1b–d and Supplementary Fig. 10) further revealed their morphology differences. The diameter of the nanowire expanded from ~50 (Fig. 1b) to ~100 nm after the Ru-ion exchange process (Fig. 1c,d), and became ultra-porous with an 5-fold increase in its electrochemically active surface area (ECSA, results will be discussed later in detail). Interestingly, we observed a hollow nanowire structure (Fig. 1c) in Ru-CuONW, which can be attributed to the Kirkendall effect42-44. This implies the lattice Cu atoms diffuse outward and exchange with the Ru ions during the synthesis. Notably, the hollow core was filled again after the electrochemical pre-reduction process, which reduced the surface oxide to metal and rearranged the lattice while maintaining the high-surface-area porous structure (Fig. 1d). We performed high-resolution HAADF-STEM and confirmed that the lattices in Ru-CuNW correspond to metallic Cu (Fig. 1e,f, Supplementary Fig. 11). Energy dispersive spectroscopic (EDS) elemental mapping revealed uniform dispersion of Ru dopants in the CuNW matrix in our catalysts (Fig. 1g, Supplementary Figs. 12–14), with a Ru:Cu atomic ratio of approximately 1:9 (Supplementary Fig. 15). Electrocatalytic NO3RR performance. The electrochemical NO3RR performance of RuCuNW catalyst was investigated under ambient temperature/pressure in a standard threeelectrode H-type cell (See Methods). In this study, NH3, NO2–, and NO3– were quantified

Page 6

by ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectrophotometry with calibration curves (Supplementary Figs. 16–18; see Methods). An industrial wastewater relevant nitrate concentration of 2000 ppm29,30 was used in our electrolyte for standard electrochemical characterizations of the catalysts. A continuous electrolyte flow system was employed to avoid the impacts of decreased nitrate concentration on measured catalytic performances (Supplementary Note 1). The two major control samples, CuNW and RuNP, presented distinct electrocatalytic behaviors compared to Ru-CuNW (Fig. 2a,b). RuNP showed an early onset of ammonia generation with ~ 80% FE at 0.2 V, suggesting a facile NO3RR kinetics on Ru sites. However, under higher overpotentials, particularly those more negative than HER thermodynamic potential (0 V), the ammonia FE starts to drop dramatically due to the strong HER competition (Supplementary Fig. 19c). This is unsurprising given that Ru is a widely recognized HER-active catalyst. In a sharp contrast, CuNW presented a sluggish ammonia onset. It required significant overpotentials to reach high ammonia FE, which also indicated the Cu’s inertness for HER (Supplementary Fig. 19b). Our Ru-CuNW catalyst organically integrates the favorable catalytic properties in Ru and Cu catalysts to deliver high-performance ammonia generation. Overall, Ru-CuNW not only showed a good onset potential, but also suppressed HER and undesired NO2– byproducts at high current densities and negative potentials (Supplementary Figs. 19–21). The I-V plot (Fig. 2a) of Ru-CuNW showed an impressive 1 A cm-2 current at only –0.13 V vs. RHE. Additionally, Ru-CuNW reached a maximum NH3 FE of 96% at 0.04 V and maintained a high FE plateau across a wide potential range (Fig. 2b), which was dramatically improved from either CuNW or RuNP. These performance metrics represent a high ammonia partial current of 965 mA cm–2 or 76500 μg h–1 cm–2 ammonia generation rate (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 22), suggesting a significant improvement from previous reports (Supplementary Table 1)31-40. We examined isotope-labeled NO3– coupled with 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) analysis and confirmed all our NH3 products were generated by electrochemical NO3RR rather than any contaminations (Fig. 2d). To compare the intrinsic activities of the catalysts, we performed electrochemical double-layer capacitance (Cdl) tests to normalize the current density over the ECSA (Supplementary Figs. 23, 24 and Supplementary Table 2). While these catalysts present similar ECSA-normalized I-V curves, Ru-CuNW still

Page 7

showed the best intrinsic activity for NO3RR toward NH3, followed by RuNP and then CuNW (Supplementary Fig. 24b). These results clearly demonstrate the good performance of Ru-CuNW is not only from its high surface area, but also from higher intrinsic activity. Different nitrate sources may have a wide range of nitrate concentrations; therefore, we examined the Ru-CuNW catalytic performance at different NO3– concentrations to demonstrate its broad adaptability. Other than the 2000 ppm concentration (32.3 mM), 10 mM and 100 mM NO3– were selected to cover the range of pollutant concentrations expected in household and textile/heavy-industrial wastewater, respectively23,26,27. While Ru-CuNW preserved its high ammonia FE and activity under higher nitrate concentration, the catalyst also presented impressive performance under 10 mM NO3–, delivering a 400 mA cm–2 current density with ~ 90% ammonia FE (Fig. 2e, Supplementary Figs. 25 and 26). In general, delivering both high nitrate removal efficiency and high ammonia FE is challenging, since the competing HER could dominate when the remaining NO3– is reduced. To evaluate Ru-CuNW’s capability in nitrate removal, we performed a batch conversion test with initial 2000 ppm NO3– and measured the remaining products (Fig. 2f). Impressively, nearly all N-sources were converted into NH3 within 1 h. The NO3–-to-NH3 selectivity reached 99.8%, and the overall NH3 FE remained over 90%. After 1 h of electrolysis, NO3– and NO2– are both significantly reduced below the World Health Organization (WHO) regulations for drinking water25,27,29. Product ratios over time (Supplementary Fig. 27) show we have successfully collected all the N-sources. These results demonstrate that Ru-CuNW not only achieves outstanding activity and NH3 FE, but also serves as a promising catalyst for complete nitrate removal/conversion in wastewater. Detailed structure understanding. A detailed understanding of the electronic and atomic structure of Ru-CuNW is required to unravel the reaction mechanism underlying the outstanding performance. There are two important questions need to be investigated. First, the oxidation state of Ru, whether the metallic or oxidized, must be determined since the electronic state underpins the electrocatalytic activity. Second, the Ru structure, either as isolated atoms or metal clusters, needs to be clarified. To answer the first question, we first conducted X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis. The XPS survey spectrums (Supplementary Fig. 28) show the existence of

Page 8

obvious Cu and Ru peaks in Ru-CuNW. In the high-resolution Cu 2p spectrum (Supplementary Fig. 29), Ru-CuONW shows both Cu2+ and Cu0/Cu+ peaks and the corresponding Cu2+ satellite peaks. However, Ru-CuNW only shows Cu0/Cu+ peaks after the pre-reduction process, indicating its surface Cu is reduced. Raman spectra also suggests both CuO and Cu2O peaks in Ru-CuONW vanished after the pre-reduction process (Supplementary Fig. 30). High-resolution Ru 3d XPS spectrums (Fig. 3a) was conducted to analyze the Ru electronic structure changes. After Ru-CuONW reduced into Ru-CuNW, negative 1.0 eV energy shifts in Ru 3d peaks were observed, suggesting the electronic state of Ru is reduced from Ru4+ to Ru0 after the pre-reduction step45,46. Surface elemental ratio analysis (Supplementary Table 3) suggests the surface Ru:Cu is approximately 16:84, which is consistent with the EDS mapping result. X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) measurements provide further insight into the electronic properties of Cu matrix and coordination structures of Ru dopants. The ex situ Cu K-edge X-ray absorption near-edge spectroscopy (XANES) and white line (Supplementary Fig. 31) show the Cu oxidation state in Ru-CuNW is identical to the reference Cu foil, suggesting CuO reduced to the metallic state after the pre-reduction. To further confirm this, we performed synchrotron-based in situ XAS (Supplementary Fig. 32), and clearly demonstrated that the Cu oxidation state of Ru-CuONW pre-catalyst was gradually turned from original Cu oxides into metallic Cu during the pre-reduction (Fig. 3b). In situ XAS analysis of Ru-CuNW during NO3RR also suggest the metallic Cu state was well maintained (Supplementary Fig. 33a). The in situ and ex situ Ru K-edge XANES profile and energy absorption threshold (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 34) indicate the Ru oxidation state was shifted from positive in Ru-CuONW to metallic state in Ru-CuNW. In situ XAS during NO3RR further confirmed the metallic Ru state is the active phase under the reaction conditions (Supplementary Fig. 33b). The corresponding Fourier-transformed extended X-ray absorption fine structure (FTEXAFS) spectrum provides information of the coordination structures (Fig. 3d). RuCuONW shows an obvious Ru–O peak (1.50 Å) but no Ru–Ru peak (2.39 Å), suggesting the Ru dopants were atomically dispersed in CuONW matrix after the ion exchange process and did not form nanoclusters. This initial doping structure could minimize the accumulation of Ru metal clusters during the pre-reduction. Please note that it is difficult

Page 9

for FT-EXASF to differentiate the structure of Ru atoms in Ru-CuNW, since the Ru–Ru (cluster) and Ru–Cu coordination (atomic dispersion) in FT-EXAFS are within 0.1 Å from each other and are indistinguishable47-49. However, based on the further characterizations below, we later conclude the peak at 2.37 Å in Ru-CuNW should be attributed to the Ru– Cu coordination since no Ru clusters were observed. We conducted synchrotron-based high-energy X-ray diffraction (HEXRD) measurements to analyze the detailed phase present. After integrated the two-dimensional HEXRD raw images (Supplementary Fig. 35) into figure 3e, we found Ru-CuNW showing only Cu peaks. Note that no Ru peaks were observed, confirming the absence of aggregated Ru particles. These results are consistent with our regular X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurement (Supplementary Fig. 36) using low-energy Copper K–α radiation. Synchrotron-based pair distribution function (PDF) analysis was performed to further probe the local bonding environments, as it is a powerful tool for studying the atomic pair correlations50,51. The first peak of Ru-CuNW has a maximum at 2.56 Å (Fig. 3f), which exactly matches the Cu–Cu nearest neighbor distance based on the known crystal structure (PDF: 00-004-0836) and the bond length of Cu–Cu and Cu–Ru predicted by DFT model (Supplementary Table 4). The first peak of Ru foil (2.70 Å) is close to the expected distance for Ru–Ru and matches well with the DFT model. The RuO2 has a Ru–O peak at 1.98 Å, but no such peaks were observed in Ru-CuNW (Supplementary Fig. 37), which further demonstrates that Ru in Ru-CuNW was fully reduced. Cu–Ru distances are expected to broaden Cu–Cu peak slightly, but no apparent shoulder is observed, suggesting the absence of Ru–Ru bonds in Ru-CuNW. The PDF results therefore provide supporting evidence for the absence of significant Ru clustering. To further verify the atomic dispersion of Ru in Cu matrix, we conducted the atom probe tomography (APT), a technique which can provide reconstructed three-dimensional information down to the atomic level. The Ru-CuNW specimen was first prepared for the APT reconstruction (Supplementary Note 2 and Supplementary Fig. 38). The overall reconstruction in figure 3g shows the Ru-CuNW fragments with Pt filling the empty space. In the analyzed zone, we found a Run+:Cun+ ratio of 16.9%, which is consistent with the EDS and XPS results. Figure 3h depicts the Ru density contour map of a cross-section of the reconstruction. The Ru-rich zones are mostly found at the boundary of the Cu-rich area,

which indicates the Ru are supported on the surface of the Cu nanowire. A closer examination of the Ru-rich area (Fig. 3i) demonstrates that the Ru atoms are highly dispersed in Cu matrix rather than forming clusters. Nearest neighbor distribution analysis indicates 84% of Ru are isolated as single atoms, with no neighboring Ru–Ru bond formed. Therefore, based on the 3D tomography information from APT and all the characterizations above, we confirmed that the Ru dopants are in metallic electronic state, and are highly dispersed as isolated atoms in the Cu substrate. Theoretical analysis. To explain the high NH3 FE over Ru-CuNW compared to CuNW and RuNP, we used DFT to calculate the minimum energy pathway of NO3RR and HER . The NO3RR pathway on the electrode surface includes: adsorption of NO3–, deoxygenation of N-species, hydrogenation on N-species and/or N–N bond formation, and desorption of reduced species. Figure 4a shows the overall pathway for NO3RR at 0 V vs. RHE at pH 14. DFT calculations predict that NH3 formation is the most favorable NO3– reaction pathway at the Ru site on Ru-CuNW, because the activation barriers along the NH3 path are much lower than the rate controlling barrier for the other pathways (NO2, NO, N2O and N2, Supplementary Fig. 39 and 40). Ru-CuNW minimizes the HER as NO3– adsorption is 0.92 eV more favorable than H+ adsorption at the Ru site (Fig. 4b) and HER is unlikely to occur on the Cu sites due to endothermic adsorption (+0.47 eV) of H2O*, the HER reactant at pH 14 (Supplementary Fig. 41). Although more negative potential increases the likelihood of NO2– desorption by reducing the desorption energy, the barrier for NO2– release remains higher than the NH3 pathway until ~ –0.9 V vs. RHE. The highly dispersed and isolated Ru active site additionally acts to restrict the NO3RR to the Ru atom due to high reaction energy (0.39 eV) required for N* to migrate from Ru–Cu to Cu–Cu sites (Supplementary Fig. 40), thus minimizing N2 formation with N atoms from other Ru sites. Overall, the combination of suppressed HER, endothermic NO2– desorption and restricted movement of N* species on the surface lead to high NH3 FE for Ru-CuNW over a wide range of potentials. The poorer performance of CuNW compared to Ru-CuNW is attributed to the weaker adsorption of NO3–, poor ability to hydrogenate reduced N atoms, and the sensitivity of NH3 vs. NO2– path to the potential. First, the activation barrier for NO3* decomposition to

Page 11

NO2* is ~ 0.02 eV higher than NO3– desorption at 0 V vs. RHE, thus it is expected that desorption is just as likely as further reaction (Fig. 4b). The NH3 FE is highly sensitive to the slight potential changes which contributes to the low NH3 FE at positive potentials (Supplementary Note 3, Supplementary Figs. 42 and 43). Moreover, water adsorption is endothermic by 0.46 eV on CuNW, thus there is limited H* for NH3 formation. The NO3RR pathway on RuNP is similar to Ru-CuNW, but the higher number of active sites available for HER and lower energy requirements for the H2O adsorption increases the probability of H2 formation and decreases NH3 FE at negative potentials (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Note 3, Supplementary Figs. 44 and 45). In summary, these results explain why Ru-CuNW has a superior performance for NH3 production via NO3RR compared to CuNW and RuNP, and highlight the importance of highly dispersed Ru in Cu matrix. Ammonia products collection. With the impressive NO3RR performance of our RuCuNW catalyst, we further demonstrated its practical applications by coupling the electrocatalysis with an air striping method to continuously collect high-purity ammonia products (Fig. 5a). The catalyst’s good stability under industrial relevant current density is a prerequisite for such applications. We first conducted a long-term chronopotentiometry (CP) stability test of Ru-CuNW in a H-cell with continuous electrolyte flow. The voltage required to achieve 400 mA cm–2 over our catalyst remained stable over 100 hours, with over 90% NH3 FE maintained (Fig. 5b). The effluent with generated NH3 in it was air stripped (see Methods) for an efficient ammonia product collection due to the high ammonia vapor pressure in alkaline environment52-54. As a result, we successfully stripped out over 99.7% of the generated ammonia from the electrolyte, with only 1.7 ppm remaining, suggesting a full denitrification of the water source and simultaneous generation of valuable ammonia (Fig. 5c). The stripped-out ammonia vapor was either trapped in a HCl solution or cold condensed into high-purity ammonia water. Most of the flowing out ammonia vapor (~ 97%) can be trapped in the acid solution, and finally formed high-purity NH4Cl(s) powder, an important fertilizer, after performing rotary evaporation. Secondarily, the liberated NH3 was subject to a cold condensation process to produce pure NH3(aq) with a NH3 concentration up to 0.3 wt.%. The overall collection efficiency of both products is over 85% (Fig. 5c). The generation of NH4Cl(s) was confirmed by XRD (Fig. 5d) and NH3(aq)

Page 12

by 1H NMR (Fig. 5e). The collection efficiencies and product purities were confirmed by UV-Vis (Supplementary Figs. 46 and 47). Overall, we demonstrated a complete process to directly convert nitrate-containing influent into practical ammonia products using our RuCuNW catalyst.

Conclusions In summary, we synthesized a high-performance NO3RR catalyst of highly dispersed Ru atoms on Cu nanowires, which delivers industrial-relevant ammonia generation current while maintaining high FE and stability. DFT revealed that the uniformly distributed Ru site shows an activated NO3–-to-NH3 pathway and the neighboring Cu sites help suppress the undesired competing HER. The effluent from our electrolysis of NO3RR was combined with an air stripping process to produce NH4Cl fertilizer and pure NH3 aqueous products. Future studies can be focused on how to translate this excellent catalytic performance into membrane electrode assembly devices where no liquid electrolyte is needed for more practical implementations.

Methods Catalyst synthesis. In a typical procedure, 1 × 1 cm2 Cu foam was first washed with isopropyl alcohol and then 0.1 M HCl to clean the surface. Next, the washed Cu foam was soaked in 0.1 M (NH4)2S2O8 with 1 M NaOH under ambient conditions for 1 h to oxidize Cu into Cu(OH)2NW. The synthesized Cu(OH)2NW was then washed with deionized water and soaked in 10 mM RuCl3 solution under ambient conditions for 12 h to go through a cation exchange process. After ion exchange, the product was dried at 70 °C in an oven for 1 h and then annealed in a flowing Ar atmosphere at 200 °C for 2 h to convert it into RuCuONW. Finally, an in-situ electrochemical pre-reduction step was performed after annealing, which operated by the CP method at 100 mA cm–2 for 12 h and was followed by 700 mA cm–2 for 1 h to obtain the final Ru-CuNW catalyst. Different noble-metal doped catalysts were synthesized via the same procedure, except 10 mM of RhCl3·xH2O, OsCl3·xH2O, and IrCl3·xH2O solutions were used instead of RuCl3. Ru-CuNW with different Ru doping levels were synthesized following the same procedure, except 2 mM, 30 mM, and 50 mM of RuCl3 solutions were used. CuNW was synthesized by performing the same annealing and electrochemical pre-reduction procedure on Cu(OH)2NW without ion exchange process. RuNP was synthesized by mixing 0.2052 g of RuCl3 and 0.4 g of carbon black (Cabot, BP2000) into 150 mL of 1 M HCl and sonicated for 2 h. Next, the mixture solution was dried by rotary evaporator, and the collected powder was annealed in a flowing Ar/H2 atmosphere at 900 °C for 2 h to synthesize the final RuNP product. Material characterization. SEM was performed on an FEI Quanta 400 field emission scanning electron microscope. TEM, HRTEM, HADDF-STEM, HR-HADDF-STEM, and EDS elemental mapping were performed using an FEI Titan Themis aberration-corrected transmission electron microscope at 300 kV and Talos F200X transmission electron microscope at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. XPS data was conducted on an on a PHI Quantera spectrometer, using a monochromatic Al Kα radiation (1486.6 eV) and a lowenergy flood gun as the neutralizer. All XPS spectra were calibrated by shifting the detected carbon C 1s peak to 284.8 eV. Raman spectrum was obtained on a Raman Microscope using 633 nm laser excitation wavelength. XAS measurements were performed at the Sector 20-BM beamline of the Advanced Photon Source at Argonne National Laboratory. The samples were measured in fluorescence mode, and the data processing for FT-EXAFS were performed using the Athena program. HEXRD measurements was carried out at 17BM at the Advanced Photon Source at Argonne National Laboratory, and the obtained 2D diffraction patterns were converted to 1D patterns using GSAS II software. Regular XRD data was collected on a Rigaku SmartLab X-ray diffractometer with Copper K-α radiation. Total scattering data for PDF was collected on the Brockhouse High Energy Wiggler beamline of the Canadian Light Source using 65 keV X-ray radiation. The Ru-CuNW foam was mounted directly on the beamline. A thin sheet of Ru metal was also measured as a reference and a RuO2 powder reference sample was measured in a 0.63 mm inner diameter Kapton capillary. Appropriate background measurements were made for each sample. The pair distribution functions, G(r), were produced using a Qmax of 27 Å–1. Atom probe tomography. APT analytical imaging is based on the combination of the field evaporation and the principles of the time-of-flight mass spectrometry (ToF-MS). Field

Page 21

evaporation defines the phenomenon during which individual atoms can be evaporated from the surface they come from as ions if a strong electric field (several tens of V nm–1) is applied on the surface. To reach such intense electric field, the samples were first fabricated as a sharp needle. APT sample preparations were performed using the standard focused ion beam (FIB) lift out process55 with a FEI Quanta dual beam56 (Supplementary Fig. 38). Considering the important presence of voids in the Ru-CuNW, Pt welding was used to fill and consolidate the final needle structure. Field evaporation is controllably provoked with the application of either short voltage pulses (~10 to 100 ns duration) or assisted with laser beam pulses combined with a steady electric field. Atoms are extracted one by one and projected towards a position sensitive detector, that will situate the detected ion in 3D space for the reconstruction. The spatial positions and the chemical identification by ToF-MS are used to reconstruct 3D chemical map the evaporated samples. A full description of the technique can be found in the previous work57. In this work, we used a LEAP 4000X-HR Local Electrode Atom Probe system by CAMECA Instruments equipped with a 355 nm UV laser. Analysis was performed with 125 kHz laser pulse frequency, 100 pJ power at 45.5 K. 3D reconstructions and analysis have been conducted using CAMECA’s Integrated Visualization & Analysis Software (IVAS) version 3.8.8. Reconstruction parameters were determined with the help of tip profile recorded by SEM after its fabrication by FIB-SEM (Supplementary Fig. 38c). Electrochemical measurement. All electrochemical measurements were carried out in a customized H-type cell with bipolar membrane (Fuel Cell Store) under ambient condition in this study. Electrochemical responses were recorded by a BioLogic VMP3 workstation. In general, 2000 ppm NO3– with 1 M KOH was used as the electrolyte in both cathode and anode unless otherwise specified. Initial electrolyte volume in each H-cell compartments were 25 mL, and the electrolyte was continuously flowed in and out as a flow-system Hcell measurement (Supplementary Note 1) unless otherwise specified. A stir bar was used in the H-cell at a stirring rate of 1600 r.p.m to minimize the mass transfer limitation in the porous electrodes. In a typical three-electrode system, a Ni foam and a saturated calomel electrode (SCE, CH Instruments) were used as the counter and reference electrode, respectively. In this work, all potentials measured against SCE were converted to the reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) scale by: E (vs. RHE) = E (vs. SCE) + 0.241 V + 0.0591×pH. In the 1 M KOH electrolyte used in this work, the pH value is 14, as determined by an Orion Star A111 pH Meter (Thermo Scientific). The synthesized RuCuNW, Ru-CuONW, and CuNW were directly used as working electrode. For RuNP working electrode, 5 mg of catalyst was added into 1 mL isopropyl alcohol and 20 µL Nafion 117 containing solution (5%, Sigma-Aldrich) and sonicated for 1 h to obtain a welldispersed catalyst ink, and then 80 µL of the catalyst ink was drop-casted on a 1 × 1 cm2 Toray Carbon Paper (Fuel Cell Store), resulting in a loading of 0.4 mg cm–2. Solution resistance (Rs) was measured by the potentiostatic electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (PEIS) at frequencies from 0.1 Hz to 200 kHz, and all the measured potentials were 50% iR-compensated unless otherwise specified. Chronoamperometry (CA) tests were performed at a stirring rate of 1600 r.p.m. at constant potentials for 10 min or until a stable current to confirm a steady-state is reached. The current densities were recorded and the electrolytes were collected to analyze the catalytic performances. LSV tests were recorded at a scan rate of 5 mV s–1. Ammonia detection. NH3 concentration was spectrophotometrically detected by the indophenol blue method with nodification58. First, the outlet electrolyte product was collected and diluted to the detection range. Next, 1 mL of diluted sample was mixed with 1 mL of 1 M NaOH, 5 wt% salicylic acid, and 5 wt% sodium citrate solution. Then 0.5 mL of 0.05 M NaClO solution and 0.1 mL of 1.0 wt% C5FeN6Na2O (sodium nitroferricyanide) solution was added and rest for 2 h under ambient condition. Next, the absorption spectrum was measured by using a UV–vis spectrophotometer (UV-2600), and the formation of indophenol blue was determined using the absorbance at 655 nm wavelength. A standard concentration–absorbance calibration curve was made beforehand using a series of standard NH4Cl solutions. NH3 product concentration was then calculated based on the tested absorbance and the standard curve. In addition, 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR, 500MHz) was also used to detect ammonia in the isotope-labeling measurement. The collected NH3 sample was first diluted to the detection range and adjust to pH 2.0 by adding 0.1 M HCl. Next, 0.5 mL of the sample solution was mixed 0.1 mL DMSO-d6 with 0.04 wt% C4H4O4, where DMSOd6 serves as a solvent and maleic acid (C4H4O4) as the internal standard. Finally, the prepared mixture was tested by a 500 MHz SB Liquild Bruker Avance NMR spectrometer at ambient condition and the NH3 product peaks were analyzed. Nitrate detection. The nitrate concentration was measured by UV-vis spectrophotometry according to the standard method59. First, an electrolyte sample was collected and diluted to the detection range. Next, 40 µL 1 M HCl with 4.0 µL 0.8 wt% sulfamic acid solution was added and mixed with 2.0 mL diluted sample and rest for 20 min at ambient condition. The absorption intensity at a wavelength of 220 nm and 275 nm was then recorded by UVvis spectrophotometry (UV-2600). The final absorbance was calculated using the equation: A = A220nm – A275nm. The concentration-absorbance curve was calibrated using a series of standard potassium nitrate solutions with linear fitting in advance. Nitrate product concentration was then calculated based on the tested absorbance and the standard calibration curve. Nitrite detection. The nitrite concentration was measured by UV-vis spectrophotometry according to the standard method59. First, a mixture of p-aminobenzenesulfonamide (4 g), N-(1-Naphthyl) ethylenediamine dihydrochloride (0.2 g), deionized water (50 mL) and phosphoric acid (10 mL, ρ = 1.685 g mL–1) was used as a color reagent. Electrolyte sample was collected and diluted to the detection range. Next, 40 µL color reagent was added into the 2.0 mL sample solution and mixed thoroughly and rest for 20 min at ambient condition. The absorption intensity at a wavelength of 540 nm was then tested by UV-vis spectrophotometry (UV-2600). The concentration-absorbance curve was calibrated using a series of standard potassium nitrite solutions with linear fitting beforehand. Nitrite product concentrations were then calculated based on the tested absorbance and the standard curve. Computational methods.
