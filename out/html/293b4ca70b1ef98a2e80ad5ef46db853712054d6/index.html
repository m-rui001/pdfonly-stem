ABSTRACT Catalytic hydrogenation of CO2 with renewable H2 to methanol represents a promising pathway for reducing anthropogenic CO2 emissions. Catalysts play a key role in enhancing both the hydrogenation rate and methanol selectivity. Among different catalysts, ZrO2 is a promising catalyst support, promoter and even active species for CO2 hydrogenation due to its versatile properties and weak hydrophilic character. Over the past decades substantial progress has been made in designing high performance catalysts and understanding the hydrogenation mechanisms over ZrO2supported catalysts. ZrO2interacts with metals and/or other oxides and consequently may affect the CO2 adsorption and activation, enhance the dissociation of H2 and spillover of atomic hydrogen, change the reaction pathways and/or the binding of key reaction intermediates for further conversion. The synergistic effects of ZrO2 could be achieved by improving the metal dispersion, modifying surface basicity and interacting with other components (metals, co-supports or promoters). However, although experimental and computational investigations have been extensively performed, the multiple roles of ZrO2 in the catalytic process are still under debate. In the current perspective we use ZrO2-containing catalysts as a model system to elucidate the governing principles for designing high performance catalysts with multiple active components for CO2 hydrogenation to methanol.

1. INTRODUCTION The rising anthropogenic CO2 emissions have been a major concern due to the significant and negative effects on the global environment.1-2 Taking into account that CO2 is an alternative carbon feedstock, catalytic hydrogenation of CO2 with renewable H2 to value-added products (e.g., methanol, long chain hydrocarbons and alcohols) gains particular interest in both academic and industrial research.3-9 Among the various hydrogenation products, the formation of methanol (CO2 + 3H2 → CH3OH + H2O) is preferred because methanol can be used not only as a sustainable source of liquid fuel but also as a reactant to produce formaldehyde, olefins, dimethyl ether, and acetic acid, among other valuable chemicals.10-15

The industrial synthesis of methanol from the mixture of CO/CO2/H2 is typically conducted in a high-pressure hydrogenation reactor at relatively high temperatures (200° to 300 °C).16 Catalysts play a key role in enhancing both the hydrogenation rate and methanol selectivity. The significant challenge is to achieve high catalytic activity, selectivity and stability at CO2 conversion levels that are acceptable for industrial processes. Since CO2 is a very stable molecule, the catalytic synthesis of methanol from CO2 typically requires high temperatures. However, although the increase in temperature promotes CO2 activation, the formation of undesirable CO is also significantly enhanced via the reverse water-gas shift (RWGS) reaction, reducing the selectivity of methanol. Furthermore, the presence of water vapor (either from the

3

Page 6

hydrogenation process or from the RWGS) in the products may suppress the methanol formation and lead to catalyst deactivation by accelerating the metal particle growth for supported catalysts.17-20 Ideal catalysts for CO2 hydrogenation are required to possess three types of capabilities: (i) to adsorb and activate CO2 and stabilize the active intermediates for hydrogenation to methanol instead of RWGS; (ii) to dissociate the HH bond in H2 and facilitate its reaction with the intermediates to produce methanol and (iii) to inhibit the poisoning effect of water on the active sites.

Multicomponent catalyst systems, consisting of at least two interdependent components, are considered as promising materials to balance the three abovementioned capabilities. Oxide-supported metals, working via the interfacial effect at the oxide-metal interfaces8, 20-28 or the formation of a specific alloy in supported metal particles29-34, have emerged as promising catalysts. Extensive efforts in searching for active metals, spanning from platinum-group metals to non-precious transition metals, have been performed. In general, supported-metals that show high performance for CO2 hydrogenation to methanol include Cu, Pd, Au, Pt, Re, and Rh. Among them, Cu is regarded as one of the most promising catalysts due to the high performance and low cost8, 35-38. Recently, it is also found that atomically dispersed Pt39, sub-nanoclusters (< 2 mn) of Au40 and Re28, as well as alloys of Pt (Pt3Co)41 and Rh (Rh75W25)42 show relatively high activity and excellent methanol selectivity at low-temperature (≤180 ℃) conditions. Meanwhile, the selection of oxide supports is broad (e.g., ZnO, Al2O3, ZrO2, SiO2, TiO2, CeO2, Ga2O3, MgO, Cr2O3, and Nb2O5). Since metals are generally 4

Page 7

accepted as the active sites, the properties of metals (e.g., particle size, dispersion, specific surface area and alloy components) and their interactions with the supports have been critically discussed to corelate with catalytic performance in several Reviews and Perspective articles.3, 7-9, 20, 25, 43-53

On the other hand, researchers are increasingly aware of the importance of supports, which not only help the dispersion of metals and create the support-metal interfacial sites, but also change the reaction environment on the catalyst surface (surface acidity or basicity, oxygen vacancy concentrations, etc.) via inducing synergy with other additives.21, 54-71 For example, ZnO shows unique characters as a support for Cu to enhance catalytic activity via inducing the formation of defective Cu (Zndecorated Cu),72-75 generating oxygen defects in the interface,8, 15, 23, 76-77 or forming a specific Cu–Zn surface alloy.16, 78-79 Ga2O3 is an attractive support for Pd catalysts due to the presence of Pdn+ stabilized by the defects in GaxOy 80 or the formation of bimetallic PdGax81-84 that plays a crucial role to enhance CO2 adsorption and H2 dissociation. Among different oxides, ZrO2 shows unique properties as a support or promoter for CO2 hydrogenation due to its weak hydrophilic character that benefits the desorption of produced water, enhancing both the methanol production rate and the selectivity.48, 68-69, 71, 85

Over the past decades, centering on the role of ZrO2, substantial progress has

been made in designing high performance catalysts and understanding the hydrogenation mechanisms. The presence of ZrO2 in catalysts could affect the CO2 adsorption and activation,86-93 tune the dissociation of H2 and subsequent spillover of 5

Page 8

atomic hydrogen,94-99 stabilize the active species,96, 100-102 change the reaction pathways75, 103 and bind the key reaction intermediates for further conversion,21-22, 24, 66 via improving the metal dispersion and surface area18, 57, 69, 101, 104-110, modifying surface properties (e.g., basicity and defect concentration)59, 69, 102-103, 111-112 and/or interacting with other components (metals, co-supports or promoters)24, 86, 98-99, 113-120. Recently, it was observed that the ZnO-ZrO2 solid solution in the absence of metals also shows good activity for CO2 hydrogenation to methanol.121 Although experimental and computational investigations have been extensively performed on the synergistic effects in the catalyst systems, the multiple roles of using oxides (especially ZrO2) as supports are still under debate and the knowledge in guiding the design of highperformance catalysts with desired oxide supports remains inadequate. Using ZrO2-containing catalysts as a model system, we aim to offer insights into the modification of the structural and surface properties of catalysts driven from the oxide supports and the resulting effects on the catalytic activity and selectivity for CO2 hydrogenation to methanol. A key issue addressed is the fundamental understanding of the interactions among different components (active metals, oxide supports and doped ions) with a focus on oxides (instead of metals) in determining the reaction mechanism and catalytic performance. This understanding on the ZrO2 induced synergies should help elucidate the governing principles for designing high-performance catalysts with multiple active components.

In this Perspective we will first summarize the role of ZrO2 as an independent support for different metals (including alloys) or oxides in binary catalyst systems. Then, 6

we extend the discussion to ternary and more complex catalyst systems by identifying the multicomponent synergy among ZrO2 and other species. We conclude the Perspective with suggestions on future research directions by briefly discussing the reaction pathways controlled by the presence of ZrO2 and the enhanced catalytic activity using advanced synthesis methods.

2.1 Cu/ZrO2 catalyst Copper has been used as an essential component in the industrial catalysts (e.g., Cu/ZnO/Al2O3) for methanol production from CO2 containing syngas. In general, Cu species generated via strongly interacting with oxide supports are proposed to be the active sites for CO2 and H2 conversion, and the nature of the support strongly affects the selectivity to methanol.3, 15-16, 73, 78, 127-128. Denise et al.70, 129-130 observe that Cu on MgO, La2O3 and Sm2O3 are much active for methane generation from CO/H2 than CO2/H2, while Cu/Al2O3 and Cu/ZnO is inactive toward CO/H2. On the other hand, Cu/ZrO2 and Cu/ZnO/Al2O3 show relatively high activity for the transformation of both CO/H2 and CO2/H2 into methanol. It is worth to highlight that at one bar pressure Cu/ZrO2 with low Cu loading (1-2 wt%) possesses comparable methanol yields from CO2 hydrogeneration as the Cu/ZnO/Al2O3 (45-50wt% Cu) catalyst. They proposed that the ZrO2 support can be considered as an integral part of the “active site” because it plays a determining role in the mechanism.130 Amenomiya 71 also compare binary catalysts among Cu/ZrO2, Cu/ZnO, Cu/TiO2, Cu/SiO2 and Cu/Al2O3 for CO2 hydrogenation to methanol, and the results indicate that the CuO(40wt%)/ZrO2 catalyst shows the highest unit mass activity and selectivity. A selectivity of 81% is obtained at 220 ℃ and 5 MPa with 4% CO2 conversion, and none of the third components (Al2O3, ZnO, SiO2, ThO2 and graphite) added to Cu/ZrO2 improvs the methanol selectivity.71 More specifically, Nitta et al.104 observe that CuZrO2 is more effective for methanol formation from CO2 than Cu-ZnO, especially at relatively high temperatures (>200 ℃). The presence of ZnO in the Cu-ZrO2 catalysts can improve the CO2 conversion via increasing the surface area and the dispersion of 9

Page 12

Cu but considerably decrease the methanol selectivity. In this case, they conclude that the role of ZrO2 is more essential than that of ZnO in the selective production of methanol. On the other hand, Kanoun et al.128 propose that ZrO2 in Cu/ZrO2 catalysts is a textural promoter upon the dispersion of Cu and it interacts with metallic Cu to create active sites for selective synthesis of methanol. The crystallization degree of ZrO2 particles strongly affects the catalytic performance, and the crystallization of the amorphous ZrO2 results in significantly lower activity and selectivity.123 Kilo et al.131 observe that the Cu/ZrO2 catalyst obtained by vacuum drying precipitate at 130 ℃ shows very high activity and selectivity for methanol formation, while a partial deactivation occurs after calcination at 350 ℃ due to the crystallization of amorphous ZrO2. Similar phenomena are also observed on the Cu/ZrO2 aerogels. The Cu/ZrO2 aerogels thermally treated by low-temperature supercritical drying (which results in amorphous zirconia) are markedly more active and similarly selective for methanol synthesis than the samples dried at high temperatures (which afford mainly tetragonal ZrO2).132 The co-existence of amorphous ZrO2 and small Cu particles also promotes CO2 hydrogenation to methanol. By exposing the amorphous Cu-Zr alloy under CO2 hydrogenation conditions, a Cu/ZrO2 catalyst with the coexistence of disordered small Cu particles, larger Cu crystals and amorphous ZrO2 is prepared, which is accompanied by a large increase in the Cu surface area.29 This catalyst shows comparable activity as the Cu/ZrO2 catalyst prepared by coprecipitation, indicating similar active sites. For the amorphous Cu70Zr30 alloy dispersed in a carbon matrix catalysts, a 3.5 times higher methanol yield (with 99% methanol selectivity at 225 ℃ and 1.0 MPa) is obtained after 10

Page 13

the first 1.5 h of reaction, which is attributed to the oxidation of Zr to amorphous ZrO2 and the formation of Cu clusters during the reaction.133 Köeppel et al.134 find that an the amorphous ZrO2 matrix can stabilize small Cu particles in the Cu/ZrO2 catalysts, which contributes to the efficient CO2 conversion. On the other hand, researchers also reveal that there is no general correlation between the catalytic activity and the Cu surface area, and the interfacial area is proposed to play an important role in determining the catalyst activity.134 A very interesting phenomenon observed by Nitta et al.135 suggest that methanol formation is favored on larger crystallites of Cu, resulting in a relatively high selectivity, while high dispersion of Zr species could greatly enhance the CO2 conversion. More specifically, by comparing a series of Cu/ZrO2 catalysts prepared using different methods (impregnation, coprecipitation, sol-gel synthesis and controlled oxidation of amorphous Cu-Zr alloys from reference), Wambach et al.48 also conclude that the catalytic behavior of these materials is greatly influenced by the interfacial contact area between the metal and ZrO2 support. It is also reported that the crystal form of ZrO2 influences the catalytic performance, with monoclinic (m-) ZrO2 in catalysts being 4.5 times more active in methanol synthesis from CO2 and H2 than those on tetragonal (t-) ZrO2 due to the higher concentration of active intermediates.136 This suggests that the m-ZrO2 (which may interacts with Cu) can help to bind the active intermediates. However, as shown in Figure 1, Samson et al.102 suggest that the significant difference in the surface hydroxylation and consequently differences in their acidity on m-ZrO2 and t-ZrO2 are more important than the crystal structures, because the surface properties directly 11

influence the adsorption capacity and the formation of intermediates. They find that an increase in the t-ZrO2 content in Cu/ZrO2 catalysts leads to a decrease in the amount of metallic Cu and an increase in the content of the Cu+. Combined with quantum calculations and experimental results, they propose that oxygen vacancies are easily formed in the t-ZrO2 at temperatures below 280℃ (the temperature region for CO2 hydrogenation), and the complexes originating from the interaction of Cu cations with oxygen vacancies on t-ZrO2 act as the active sites for methanol synthesis. Therefore, the catalytic activity toward methanol increases with increasing t-ZrO2 content.

Although multiple publications suggest that the crystal forms (monoclinic and tetragonal) of ZrO2 is also closely related to the catalytic performance, there is no general agreement. For example, both t-ZrO2 and m-ZrO2 are observed to enhance methanol synthesis by changing the oxygen vacancy formation or modifying the surface properties.102, 136 2.2 Other binary catalysts Apart from Cu/ZrO2, other zirconia supported metal catalysts (e.g., Au/ZrO2, Ag/ZrO2, and Pt/ZrO2) and mixed oxide catalysts (e.g., In2O3/ZrO2 and ZnO-ZrO2 solid solution) are also reported for CO2 hydrogenation to methanol. Au/ZrO2 is most active for the reverse water gas shift reaction and thus exhibits very low selectivity to methanol.62, 123 However, Wu et al.40 observe that the Au sub-nano particles (< 2.0 nm) on ZrO2 show high catalytic activity and excellent selectivity for methanol formation from CO2, especially at relatively low temperatures (160-180 ℃), due to the strong interaction between the Au clusters and the ZrO2 support. Although Pd/ZrO2 shows high methanol formation rates from CO and H2, it is almost inactive for CO2 hydrogeneration to methanol.106 PtCo/ZrO2 preferentially catalyzes the CO2 hydrogeneration to methane.21 Frohlich et al.139 report that Ag/ZrO2 is more selective than Cu/ZrO2 in methanol synthesis from CO2/H2, but its activity is drastically decreased after calcination at 350 ℃ in air. On the other hand, Köppel et al.132 find that CO2 conversion over Ag/ZrO2 aerogels is lower than that of the corresponding Cu/ZrO2 aerogels, whereas methanol selectivity is similar. In contrast to Cu/ZrO2, no activity for methanol formation is observed for the uncalcined Ag/ZrO2 aerogel. Grabowski et al.101 observe no correlation between the catalytic activity in the methanol synthesis and the dispersion of metallic 14

Ag, and they propose that the Ag+ stabilized by oxygen vacancies on nonstoichiometric t-ZrO2 is the active phase. In this case, the oxygen vacancy concentration plays a crucial role in determining the catalytic activity. They also observe that, for the Ag/ZrO2 catalyst, the addition of ZnO has no obvious effect on the methanol formation, which is very different with the Cu/ZrO2 catalyst. Modification of Cu/ZrO2 by Ag can significantly improve the methanol selectivity while the activity remains virtually constant.139-140 As shown in Figure 3,140 the synergistic interaction among Cu, Ag and ZrO2 results in the formation of the Ag+ and Zrq+ (q<4) , which are not present in the Cu/ZrO2 and Ag/ZrO2 catalysts alone, and these species combining with Cu-rich alloy appear to be the main active species for CO2 hydrogenation to methanol.

Two mixed-oxide catalysts without the presence of metal also show very promising performance for CO2 hydrogenation to methanol, as shown in Figure 4. One is In2O3/ZrO2, which shows 100% selectivity to methanol at all temperatures tested (200-300 ℃, 5MPa, 16000 h-1) and is stable for 1000 h on stream (300 ℃, 5MPa, 16000 h-1)126. This strongly contrasts to the substantial loss of activity for the benchmark CuZnO-Al2O3 catalyst (see Figure 4b). The creation and annihilation of oxygen vacancies are the key steps during the catalytic process, and ZrO2 plays a unique role because it not only interacts with In2O3 to create more and/or new oxygen vacancies but also prevents the sintering of the In2O3 phase. DFT calculations using the defective sites in the ZrO2-In2O3 interface as active sites suggest that CO2 hydrogenation mainly follows a formate route and the hydrogenation of HCOO to H2COO species is the rate determining step.66 The ZrO2 support could bind the H2COO species on the surface of In2O3 catalyst and enhance the formation methanol.66 Similarly, the ZnO-ZrO2 solid solution catalyst can achieve methanol selectivity up to 91% with CO2 conversion higher than 10% under reaction conditions of 5.0 MPa, 24000 mL g-1 h-1, and 320 to 315°C.121 More importantly, it is resistant to sintering at higher temperatures and in the presence of 50 ppm SO2 or H2S.

3.1 Cu/ZnO/ZrO2

Cu-ZrO2 and Cu-ZnO are among the most promising catalysts for CO2 hydrogeneration to methanol. Although the Cu/ZrO2 and Cu/ZnO catalysts obtained by various methods show different catalytic performance, it is generally accepted that Cu/ZrO2 is more effective for the selective formation of methanol from CO2, while Cu/ZnO shows higher activity for CO2 conversion. In this case, it is logical to combine the two to create a new ternary catalyst. The Cu/ZnO/ZrO2 catalyst indeed shows very efficient performance for methanol formation owning from the multiple interactions between Cu-species and ZnO-ZrO2 oxides. 3.1.1 General performance

ZrO2 as a co-support is known to enhance the Cu/Zn dispersion and the redox property of Cu species, thus improving the catalytic activity.149-150, 157-158 Regardless of the reaction temperatures, GHSVs and preparation methods, the Cu/ZnO/ZrO2 ternary catalysts show much higher catalytic activity than Cu-ZnO and Cu-ZrO2, and the advantages are more obvious at relatively low temperatures (180-240 °C).68, 100, 107-108, 156

In addition, the Cu/ZnO/ZrO2 catalyst also shows enhanced methanol productivity

compared to the Cu/ZnO/Al2O3 and Cu/ZnO/CeO2, because ZrO2 strongly modifies the textural and chemical effects (e.g., specific surface area, Cu dispersion and Cu surface area) and the adsorption properties.89-90, 148 A very important finding in the comparison between Cu/ZnO/ZrO2 and Cu/ZnO/Al2O3 is the weak hydrophilic character of the ZrO2 support, which benefits 22

Page 25

the desorption of produced water and thermodynamically promotes the formation of methanol.68 On the other hand, it is also observed by EXAFS analysis that the presence of ZrO2 in the Cu/ZnO catalysts lowers the Cu coordination number and increases the Cu–O distance, indicating the presence of less well-ordered structures of Cu likely due to the enhanced Cu-support interaction.107 As a result, the ZrO2-doped Cu/ZnO catalyst shows much higher activity and selectivity towards methanol synthesis than the Cu/ZnO catalyst. The above phenomena suggest that both surface and structural modifications of ZrO2 on Cu/ZnO contribute to the high catalytic performance. It is also proposed that the presence of ZrO2 in Cu/ZnO/ZrO2 contributes to the selective formation of methanol, while the addition of ZnO to Cu-ZrO2 enhances conversion of CO2 at relatively low temperatures.104 The crystallinity (or crystal size) of ZrO2 strongly affects the Cu-ZrO2 interaction,123 consequently influencing the catalytic activity of Cu/ZnO/ZrO2. As a result, The co-presence of amorphous ZrO2 and crystalline Cu/ZnO enhance the synergy effect over Cu/ZnO/ZrO2, accelerating the dissociative adsorption of CO2 and the selective conversion CO2 to methanol.88 Another interesting finding is that, when dispersing ZnO and ZrO2 nanoparticles on large Cu particles, the ZnO particle size is closely related to the CO2 conversion and methanol yield (see Figures 5c), while there is no correlation between the Cu surface areas and methanol formation (see Figures 5d).155 In addition, among Cu/ZnO, Cu/ZrO2 and Cu/ZnO/ZrO2 catalysts (keeping comparable sizes of Cu, ZnO and ZrO2 particles over the three catalysts), the Cu/ZnO/ZrO2 catalyst shows the highest methanol yield, while Cu/ZrO2 possesses the highest methanol selectivity.155 This 23

The authors further propose that the ZnO-ZrO2 interaction, which is strongly affected by the particle sizes of ZnO and ZrO2, mainly contributes to the adsorption of CO2 and binds the formate intermediate, playing a crucial role in determining the catalytic performance.

3.1.2 Improved performance by different synthesis methods

It is generally accepted that the structures, adsorption ability and surface properties of catalysts strongly affect the catalytic performance. Among the various viable strategies to improve the catalytic performance, optimizing the compositions, preparation methods and/or morphologies are very attractive and are relatively easy to achieve. The Cu/ZnO/ZrO2 ratio strongly affects the dispersion of Cu, the Cu surface area and the interaction between metal and oxides, and these properties determine the adsorption and activation ability for CO2 and H2, consequently influencing the catalytic activity.91 However, the optimal ratio usually depends on the preparation method. Co-precipitation is one of the most effective methods for obtaining high performance Cu/ZnO/ZrO2 catalysts. Raudaskoski et al.145 have found that prolonged suspension time for co-precipitated Cu/ZnO/ZrO2 catalysts results in finer crystallite structures that improve the reducibility of Cu oxides, obtaining higher CO2 conversion and methanol selectivity. In addition, the variation of precipitation and ageing temperatures also strongly affects the particle size, pore volume and size, specific surface areas, and redox behavior of the catalysts.159 The catalysts from amorphous precursor obtained at low precipitation temperatures show relatively high methanol yield, although they are less active. The physical state of the precursor is another important parameter to adjust the properties of the active catalysts. Ma et al.143 have reported that solvent with small surface tension and large viscosity is beneficial to the formation of a disordered and isomorphous precursor, which produces highly active ultra-fine Cu/ZnO/ZrO2 catalysts upon calcination. 25

Page 28

The effects of preparation methods (i.e., the conventional sodium bicarbonate coprecipitation, complexation with citric acid and gel-oxalate coprecipitation method) on the structure and catalytic behavior of Cu/ZnO/ZrO2 catalysts for CO2 hydrogenation have been investigated by Cordaro et al.117 A suitable distribution among metal and oxide sites (i.e., metal surface area (MSA)/oxide surface area (OSA) ratio) can be obtained by the gel-oxalate co-precipitation method, which is a crucial factor to achieve high methanol selectivity. On the other hand, the urea–nitrate combustion method with glycine is proposed to easily produce Cu/ZnO/ZrO2 catalysts with small grain size, large surface area and low reduction temperature, which exhibit excellent activity for CO2 hydrogeneration.146 By changing the content of glycine, phase transformation between t-ZrO2 and m-ZrO2 can be controlled, which tunes the selectivity of methanol.85 The solid-state reaction between hydrated metal salts and citric acid ligand also provides a simple, rapid and solvent-free method for Cu/ZnO/ZrO2 generation, and the catalytic activity can be optimized by varying the calcination temperatures.147 Ultrasonic spray precipitation also shows possibility of improving the selectivity and yield of methanol via reducing the Cu crystallites and improving particle uniformity and surface basicity of the catalysts.103 Similarly, the polymeric precursor method with a suitable calcination temperature results in the formation of m-ZrO2 and increases the Cu surface area, leading to high CO2 conversion and methanol selectivity.160

On the other hand, the presence of NaBH4 in the precipitation process can tune the Cu particle size, the Cu/Cu+ ratio and the number of basic sites on th Cu/ZnO/ZrO2 catalysts.153 A suitable Cu/Cu+ ratio is helpful for obtaining high CO2 conversion and 26

Page 29

the selectivity of methanol is enhanced by increasing the concentration of basic sites that is related to the state of ZrO2. A two-nozzle flame spray pyrolysis technique is demonstrated to be promising for controlling the particle size of Cu and consequently affecting the catalytic performance.125 A co-precipitation method followed by vaporphase-treatment increases the particle size of CuO, enriches Zn/Zr oxides on the surface and promotes the formation of oxygen vacancies.161 As a result, the larger Cu particles suppress the conversion of CO2 to CO via RWGS and the modified surface properties are beneficial for the selective formation of methanol.

The morphology and particle uniformity are also closely related to the performance of catalysts. It has been reported that catalysts with fibrous structures show much higher CO2 conversion, methanol selectivity as well as thermal stability than the commercial catalyst due to the relatively high specific surface area.149 Using a surfactant-assisted co-precipitation method, Cu/ZnO/ZrO2 catalysts with narrow mesopores size distributions are prepared and exhibit high methanol selectivity, which can be attributed to the larger interfacial area of the metal-oxide interface resulting from the small catalyst particles.127 The ultrasonic treatment also shows positive effect on the CO2 adsorption and conversion to methanol by tuning the surface morphology, specific surface area, particle size and porosity.162

ZrO2 shows multiple roles in different catalyst systems. In several ZrO2 containing catalysts (e.g., Cu/ZnO/ZrO2, Au/ZnO/ZrO2, Au/CeO2/ZrO2 and Au/CeO2/ZrO2), the high activity can be explained by enhanced metal dispersion101, 107, weak hydrophilic character68, 85, 100 and the medium-strength basic or acid property 69, 102, 111, 117, 152. The modification by ZrO2 on γ-Al2O3 supported Cu catalysts improves the catalytic activity and methanol selectivity due to the higher dispersion of CuO species.57 When adding ZrO2 into the Cu/SiO2 catalysts, an enhancement of activity is obtained for catalysts having the same Cu surface area.163

The modification of Cu/ZrO2 catalysts by adding other oxides also improves the catalytic activity, selectivity and stability. Since amorphous ZrO2 is considered as an active support for Cu-based catalysts, crystallization of amorphous ZrO2 usually reduces the methanol yield. The addition of CrOx and MnOx to Cu/ZrO2 can enhance the thermal stability of the catalyst via stabilizing the amorphous state of ZrO2 in thermal treatment and under reaction conditions.131 In addition, the combination of ZrO2 with CrOx improves the selectivity of methanol. The activity of Cu/ZrO2 is also improved by the presence of CeO2 to form a Ce-Zr-O solid solution that promotes the formation of oxygen vacancy and increases the surface area of Cu.111, 152. It has been suggested that CO2 is captured on the oxygen vacancies and migrates to the adjacent H-Cu site to be reduced into methanol. On the other hand, the methanol selectivity is proposed to be strongly related with the distribution of basic sites on the catalysts. 4. COMPLEX CATALYST SYSTEMS 4.1 ZrO2 as a co-support or additive

It has been reported that the addition of MgO and MnO into the Au/ZnO/ZrO2 system reduces the size of Cu and increase the Cu surface area and the Cu concentration on the surface of catalysts, improving the methanol yield.87, 169 The incorporation of Ga2O3 and Y2O3 into Cu/ZnO/ZrO2 catalysts enhances the Cu dispersion and reducibility, thus giving rise to superior methanol synthesis activity.170 Słoczyǹski et al.114 investigate the modification of B, Ga, In, Gd, Y, Mn and Mg oxides on the activity and stability of Cu/ZnO/ZrO2 (CZZ) catalysts and find that these oxide additives affect the ZrO2 concentration on the surface, the Cu dispersion, and the capability of the surface to adsorb water, which further change the concentration of the active centres and their energetics. Partial replacement of ZrO2 by CeO2 (or doping CeO2 in ZrO2 lattice) leads to a positive effect on the catalytic performance of CZZ catalyst mostly in terms of methanol selectivity, although it depresses both the surface area and the metal surface area of the catalysts.148 This is likely attributed to the formation of Ce3+-O(H) Zr4+ species that reacts with the methoxy species to form methanol, which is similar with the observation in the Cu/Ce0.5Zr0.5O2 catalyst for CO hydrogenation.142

Enhanced CO2 conversion and methanol selectivity have been reported with the addition of ZrO2 into the Cu-ZnO-TiO2 catalyst.116 The addition of ZrO2 or ZrO2–TiO2 into Cu-ZnO catalysts reduces the crystallite sizes of CuO and ZnO, enlarges the Cu surface area, increases the amounts of basic sites and improves the H2 adsorption 32

Page 35

capacity, enhancing both the CO2 conversion and methanol selectivity.92 Gao et al. 171 have found that the addition of ZrO2 into the Cu/Zn/Al catalyst increases the fraction of strongly basic sites over total basic sites, and the CH3OH selectivity is linearly related to the fraction of strongly basic sites.

By comparing Cu/ZnO-based catalysts using ZrO2, Al2O3, Ga2O3 and Cr2O3 as additives, Saito et al.18 report that ZrO2 and Al2O3 improve the dispersion of Cu, while Ga2O3 affects the ratio of Cu+/Cu° on the surface of Cu particles. In this case, the Cu/ZnO/ZrO2/Al2O3/Ga2O3 catalyst shows very high activity for methanol formation due to the multiple interaction among different oxides. 5. GENERAL TRENDS AND OBSERVATIONS 5.1 Active sites

Over the Cu/ZrO2-based catalysts, it has been found by using in situ infrared spectroscopy that methanol synthesis occurs on the surface of both Cu and ZrO2, and on the ZrO2 surface CO2 adsorbs to form b–HCO3–Zr that then undergoes progressive hydrogenation to form methanol.86 CO2 conversion is linearly corelated to the Cu surface area and the dispersion of Cu, while methanol selectivity strongly depends on the distribution of basic sites on the catalyst surface.69, 124 A comparative assessment of the activity-selectivity pattern of Al2O3, ZrO2 and CeO2 supported Cu–ZnO systems indicates that at steady-state CO2 adsorption is not related to the surface area of metal but to that of the oxide, and the catalytic activity therefore depends on a synergy of metal and basic oxide sites89-90 In addition, catalytic performance in methanol synthesis is strongly dependent on the interfacial metal–oxide contact area.176 By quantifying the concentrations of Cu-ZrO2 interfacial sites, the formation of Cu-ZrO2 interfacial sites results in an increase in the turnover frequency by an order of magnitude for the 36

Page 39

methanol generation.177 It is also observed that the hydrogenation reaction occurs on the Cu sites for the Cu/SiO2 catalysts, while, for the Cu/ZrO2/SiO2, all the adsorbed species are associated with ZrO2.94 On the other hand, the physical mixture of Cu/SiO2 and ZrO2/SiO2 catalysts hardly show enhancement on the CO2 conversion to methanol, indicating the importance of the Cu/ZrO2 interface sites.177

Incorporating Zn into the Cu/ZrO2 catalyst or addition of Zr into the Cu/ZnO catalyst strongly enhances the adsorption capacity of CO2/H2 and further increases the yield of methanol, indicating the important role of ZnO-ZrO2 interaction.156 Moreover, similar phenomena are also observed over the oxide (Cr2O3, MoO3, WO3 and MgOAl2O3) modified Cu-ZnO-ZrO2 catalysts, and the methanol yield is closely related to the CO2 adsorption capacity and the concentration of basic sites instead of the Cu surface area.92, 174, 178-179 Both of the two properties are controlled by the ZrO2 induced interactions with other oxides in catalysts.

In 1990, Burch et al. has proposed that since ZnO and ZrO2 can catalyze methanol synthesis under correct conditions, it is possible that in some cases the Cu is promoted by the supports, but in other cases the Cu may be the promoter.163 Taking the recent reports on the high performance of ZnO-ZrO2 solid solution into account121, this inference may be true. All the evidence points to dual-site or multisite (Cu0, Cuδ+ and ZrO2 related species in the interface) reaction pathways on the CO2-hydrogenation functionality, which confirms the fundamental role of the metal/oxide and/or oxideoxide interface in Cu-ZrO2 based catalysts 69, 89-90, 92, 124, 148

Another important feature for CO2 hydrogenation is the important role of water. It is generally observed among different catalysts that, at low CO2 conversion, higher methanol selectivity can be obtained, while higher CO2 conversion is always accompanied by lower methanol selectivity. One possible explanation is that high CO2 conversion to methanol results in the formation of abundant water that may partially oxidize the metallic sites and/or the Cu+ species, inhibiting the formation of CH3OH.117 In this case, the stabilization of the active sites by the oxide supports is very important to enhance methanol formation even in a more oxidative environment, and a suitable metal-to-oxide surface ratio is favorable for balancing the competition between conversion and selectivity.

In summary, for a catalytic CO2 hydrogenation process, H2 is dissociated on the Cu sites and transports from the surface of Cu onto the ZrO2 sites via spillover to react with 41

the adsorbed carbon-containing species to produce methanol.69, 100, 124, 153, 182 The catalytic behavior of CO2 hydrogenation is not exclusively dependent on the availability of metallic sites (mainly responsible for H2 activation) or the surface oxides (mainly responsible for CO2 activation), but rather on the extent of the interfacial surface area, as determined by a proper interaction between metal and oxide. The promotion of ZrO2 together with tuning acidic-basic sites and oxygen vacancy can improve the metal dispersion, stabilize Cu or Ag cations, decrease the capability of the surface to adsorb water, and adjust the adsorption capacity for CO2, leading to a high methanol yield (especially high methanol selectivity) from CO2 hydrogenation.

5.2 Reaction mechanisms

For CO2 hydrogenation to methanol over Cu-based catalysts, formate (*HCOO) produced by the reaction between CO2 and dissociative H and CO generated from the reverse water-gas shift are considered to be two major intermediates.8, 22 For all catalysts, methanol selectivity is governed by the competition of the simultaneously catalyzed methanol synthesis and the reverse water gas shift (RWGS) reactions. In both pathways, methanol is generated from the methoxy (*CH3O) intermediate. In situ diffuse reflectance FTIR studies reveals that CO2 can be transformed into carbonate or bicarbonate, formate and methoxy, upon adsorption and hydrogenation on Cu/ZrO2 particles.24 The formate species is considered to be the most important reaction intermediate and that the Cu/ZrO2 interface is crucial for the conversion of this intermediate to methanol. The rate determining step for methanol synthesis is proposed 42

Page 45

to be the conversion of formate into formaldehyde or methoxy.144 The ZrO2 support is found be to facilitate the conversion of adsorbed CO2 to surface formate or hydroxycarbonyl entities.70,100 DFT and kinetic Monte Carlo (KMC) simulations suggest that, on the oxygen rich Cu/m-ZrO2 interface, both methanol and CO are produced dominantly via the formate pathway, while the RWGS channel has only a minor contribution.115 In addition, the synthesis of methanol from CO2 over Cu/ZrO2 involves the spillover of H atoms formed on Cu to the surface of ZrO2, and the atomic H then participates in the hydrogenation of carbon-containing species (i.e., HCOO and HCO3) to methanol.95 In general, a complete process of hydrogen spillover includes several steps, namely: (1) H2 adsorption and dissociation on metal sites, (2) H atoms transfer from one surface to the other, (3) H atoms diffuse rapidly across the oxide surface, and (4) H atoms react or exchange with other intermediates.97 It should be highlighted that the -OH species on ZrO2 is also involved in the spillover, because the exchange of atomically adsorbed H atoms and OH groups on the catalysts occurs, which would affect the spillover efficiency. H2-D2 isotopic exchange experiment is a typical method to estimate the ability to dissociate H2 on catalysts. Ouyang et al.183 identify that D2 can dissociatively adsorb on the hydroxylated ZrO2 and exchange with the H in -OH. They also compare the isotopic exchange reaction between molecular D2 and OH species on different types of ZrO2 (i.e., bare (b-) ZrO2 and ZrO2 adsorbed with formate (f-)). It is found that the rate-determining step for the exchange reaction of surface OH with D2 is the migration of D atoms on b-ZrO2, while it becomes the activation of D2 on f-ZrO2, 43

Page 46

indicating that the presence of H-containing intermediates (e.g., formate and methoxy) strongly affects the dissociative adsorption of H2. Further investigations by Jung and Bell95 reveal that the presence of Cu on ZrO2 significantly increases the rate of H/D exchange of HO-Zr groups. The presence of formate also reduces the rate of H/D exchange of HO–Zr groups. However, adsorbed water enhances the rate of H/D exchange for Cu/ZrO2 but inhibits it for ZrO2, indicating that the Cu-ZrO2 interaction enhances the H/D exchange under the CO2 hydrogenation condition. It is also found that the addition of Ga2O3 into the Cu/ZrO2 system significantly decreases the H/D isotopic exchange rate, likely because that the presence of Ga2O3 reduces the contact area between Cu and ZrO2.97 Similarly, Witoon et al.,98 by using XANES and TEM techniques, observe that stronger interaction and larger interfacial area between copper species and ZrO2 facilitate the spillover of atomic hydrogen. In addition, in-situ DRIFT spectra of pyridine adsorption on the Cu/ZrO2 catalysts reveal that the concentration of H+ on ZrO2, which is derived from the spillover of H atoms, is proportional to the interaction between Cu and ZrO2.99 In this case, it is resealable to conclude that the hydrogen spillover is closely related to the presence of surface OH species on ZrO2 and the interaction of ZrO2 with metal. It is also noted that controversy still remains about the role of hydrogen spillover in determining the methanol formation from CO2 hydrogenation. On one hand, it is proposed that the rates of hydrogen spillover and H/D exchange on Cu are more than an order of magnitude faster than the rates of methanol formation as well as RWGS, therefore, not the rate determining step.95, 97 On the other hand, it is also observed that 44

6. CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS In general, metal oxides exhibit four typical surface properties: acidic, basic, oxidizing, and reducing. Most metal oxides show one or two dominant properties, but ZrO2 possesses all of the four properties and each of them is relatively weak.52 For CO2 hydrogenation to methanol, which can be produced directly from CO2 or via CO as an intermediate. As a Lewis acidic molecule, CO2 would be expected to interact with basic sites, while, as a weakly basic molecule, CO tends to interact with the acidic sites.113 In this case, an integrated approach with tuning the amphoteric character (acidic and basic properties) of supports helps both CO2 and CO to become the reagents.130 On the other hand, it also appears that the interplay between the hydrogen donating function of the copper and the oxygen uptake by the support is a crucial element for selective methanol synthesis.48 In this case, the modification on the binding of lattice oxygen ions in the supports can also change the activity. The ZrO2 support should thus be considered as an integral part of the “active site” since it plays a determining role in the mechanism. On the other hand, ZrO2 shows four crystal structures: monoclinic, tetragonal, cubic and metastable tetragonal and the transformation of the crystal forms is sensitive to the existence of impurities or additives. The changeable properties both in the bulk and on

Three general strategies are usually used to improve the activity of a catalyst system: (1) increasing the intrinsic activity of each active site that can be implemented by producing active alloys or inducing new active interfacial sites, (2) increasing the number of active sites (e.g., through improving the dispersion of metals and the metalsupport interaction) and (3) modifying the surface property and adsorptive ability via controlling the physicochemical features of supports (e.g., combing different supports or create defects in the support). The role of ZrO2 should be highlighted 49

Page 52

as it can be involved in all the above factors to obtain high performance catalysts. As a support, additive, promoter or doped species, ZrO2 can strongly interact with both the active metals and many oxides (including metal-organic framework175) to tune the activity and selectivity of catalysts for methanol synthesis. ZrO2 even shows good performance for CO2 hydrogenation via coupling with other oxides (e.g., ZnO121 or In2O366), creating new metal free catalysts.

The structure dependence of ZrO2 containing catalysts can be summarized to be correlated to the following four factors: metal properties (metal surface area, dispersion, or size), state of oxide supports (crystallinity, particles size, and ZrO2 crystalline form), surface properties (acidic, basic and surface oxygen vacancy concentration) and the metal-support interaction (i.e., formation of new sites that can bind the active intermediates). The changes on the components and structures, the optimization on the preparation methods and the design on the morphology can be related to the above factors to affect the catalytic performance.

The inhomogeneous nature of supported metal catalysts makes it very difficult to obtain conclusive information regarding the active sites and reaction mechanism under reaction conditions. The structure sensitivity of metals and the synergetic effect between metals and oxides are considered as one of the most important factors in determining the catalytic activity. In the case of ZrO2-containing catalysts, since bigger Cu particles can suppress the conversion of CO2 to CO via RWGS and the smaller or amorphous ZrO2 on Cu particles is beneficial for the formation of methanol, inverse

On the other hand, a growing body of evidence suggests the unique role of oxides (e.g., ZrO2) and the oxide-oxide interaction (e.g., ZrO2-ZnO interface, In2O3-ZrO2 and ZrO2-based solid solution) in determining CO2 conversion and methanol selectivity. The challenges in more fundamental understanding of the mechanisms at oxide-oxide interface need to be addressed. The deeper insights into fundamental features of the ZrO2 induced synergies for methanol formation help develop paradigm for designing next-generation catalyst in an optimal manner, which provides the opportunity to tune the catalyst properties by optimizing the functions of the oxide components.
