Abstract: The solid-electrolyte-interphase (SEI) is probably the least understood component in Li-ion batteries. Considerable effort has been put in the understanding of its formation and electrochemistry under realistic battery conditions, but mechanistic insights have been inferred mostly indirectly. Here we show the formation of the SEI between a graphite anode and a carbonate electrolyte using combined atomic scale microscopy and in-situ and operando techniques. In particular, we weigh the graphitic anode during its initial lithiation process with electrochemical quartz crystal microbalance, which unequivocally identifies lithium fluoride (LiF) and lithium alkylcarbonates as the main chemical components at different potentials. In-situ gas analysis confirmed the preferential

Page 2

reduction of cyclic over acyclic carbonate molecules, making its reduction product the major component in SEI. We find that SEI formation starts at graphite edgesites with dimerization of solvated Li+-intercalation between graphite-layers. We also show that this lithium salt, at least in its nascent form, is re-oxidizable, despite the general belief that an SEI is electrochemically inert and its formation irreversible.

Although Li-ion batteries (LIBs) are ubiquitous in our lives, 1,2 much of the electrochemical processes governing their chemistry remains unclear. In particular, the solid-electrolyte-interphase (SEI) is probably the most elusive component 3,4. In order to maximize energy densities, the electrodes in LIBs are designed to operate at extreme potentials5,6; hence, electrolytes need to work under electrochemical strains far beyond their thermodynamic stability limits at both anode and cathode surfaces. In such hostile environments, the kinetic stability of electrolytes is often achieved through a passivation process, where trace amounts of electrolyte components decompose and form a dense and protective layer on the electrode surfaces6,7. This passivation layer must protect the electrode from electron tunneling but remain conductive to Li+ ions 8. It is the SEI that ensures the reversibility and rate-capability of LIB chemistries 9,10. Given its importance, the SEI has been extensively studied with diversified analytic tools, both ex-situ and in-situ3,11,12. The former, as represented by X-ray photoelectron spectra (XPS)13,14, scanning and transmission electron microscope (SEM and TEM)15,16, or secondary ion mass spectroscopy (SIMS)17, provide critical chemical and morphological information of the SEI, but artifacts might inadvertently be present due to the high energy photon or electron beams used; moreover, sample preparation might alter or contaminate the interphases. The latter, as represented by atomic force microscope (AFM)18-20 and electrochemical impedance spectra (EIS)21, although nondestructive to the surfaces, are often phenomenological tools that cannot provide quantitative insights into the underlying chemistry. The quantitative detection of the SEI components has therefore been challenging. Recent efforts22,23in developing in-situ and operando techniques based on TEM or XPS have enabled the direct visualization of battery components during their electrochemical operation, but either the temporal or spatial resolution is insufficient to characterize the interphases. In most cases, non-

Page 3

standard electrolyte systems (such as ionic liquids) had to be adopted, because vacuum conditions are required for the sample visualization. Finally, the non-crystalline and composite nature of the SEI further complicates the interpretation of the interphasial chemistries. In this work, we describe a concerted approach to quantitatively monitor the interphasial components as they evolve on the electrode surface at various potentials. Integrating the quantitative and in-situ and operando natures of electrochemical quartz crystal microbalance (EQCM) and atomistic-precision of AFM (Supplementary Fig. 14), we study a graphitic electrode while cathodically polarized in a typical electrolyte composition: 1.0 M LiPF6 dissolved in equal molar mixture of ethylene carbonate (EC) and dimethyl carbonate (DMC). As a highly sensitive mass monitor24-26, EQCM allows us to precisely weigh what species are accumulated on or lost from the graphite electrode as function of the applied potential, while AFM images reveal how the highly oriented pyrolytic graphite (HOPG) interacts with the solvated Li+ during the initial lithiation and de-lithiation processes. These quantitative observations enable us to establish an atomistic picture for the formation mechanism of the SEI that is hitherto unavailable.

Weighing the solid-electrolyte interphase When a graphite anode is gradually polarized toward the potential of Li-metal, a series of reactions occur before Li+-intercalation, including the initial formation of SEI. Based on the mass change recorded by EQCM during this process, we can divide the cathodic polarization of graphite into four regions (Fig. 1a). Region I lies between the open-circuit-voltage (OCV, See Methods section) and 2.4 V, where both current and mass change remain essentially in the background. Region II, between 2.4 V and 1.1 V, where the current starts to depart from the baseline and reaches a local maximum at 1.6 V indicating the onset of certain reactions accompanied by a mass change. Region III, between 1.1 and 0.74 V, where a broad current response arises corresponding to the initial reduction of electrolyte components. Region IV, between 0.74 and 0.0 V, where major electrochemical reduction of the intercalated species occurs marking the

Considering that we used an electrolyte based on an EC/DMC mixture, the reduction of both EC and DMC can occur simultaneously28,29, although it has been reported that EC-reduction should be preferred 30,31. Since the m/dq for lithium ethylene dicarbonate (LEDC, C4H4O6Li2 from EC reduction; m/e=80.9, Eq. 3) and lithium methylcarbonate (LMC, C2H3O3Li from DMC reduction; m/e=81.9, Eq. 4) differs by only 1 atomic unit, EQCM alone cannot differentiate which alkylcarbonate is predominately formed, or whether a preference exists at all. It should be further cautioned that, given the accuracy limit of EQCM, other organic species with similar molecular weights are also possible. Precisely identifying these species needs other complemental characterization means.

Differentiating Interphasial Reactions According to Eq. 3 and Eq. 4, what can reliably distinguish the single-electron reductions of EC and DMC lies in their respective gaseous products. Although the molecular weights of ethylene (C2H4, M.W. 28) and ethane (C2H6, M.W. 30) are still rather close, the precision of mass spectrometry should be able to tell such difference.

Thus, we used in-situ DEMS (Supplementary Fig. 7) to a graphite electrode cathodically polarized in the same electrolyte during its initial lithiation/de-lithiation. The gaseous products from the cell were pumped into a mass spectrometer, where they were bombarded, and the resulting fragments were analyzed for structural identification. A cluster of peaks were generated centering on m/e 25~30 (Supplementary Fig. 8 and 9), representing the complicated breaking down of the gaseous products under bombardment. By comparing the fragmentation patterns with the standard mass spectra from National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST)32, which provide fingerprint database for identification of chemical compounds fragmented under mass spectrometry environment, we attributed the species at m/e 29 and 30 to DMC (Supplementary Fig. 10 and 11), which

apparently escapes from the electrochemical

chamber into the DEMS line due to its volatility (b.p. 91 oC). The twin peaks at m/e 29 and 30 thus should not be attributed to ethane that is electrochemically produced by DMC reduction (Supplementary Fig. 8). The peak at m/e 28 can be due to either the molecular peak of ethylene (C2H4) produced by EC-reduction or the dehydrogenated product of ethane (C2H6) produced by DMC-reduction. However, the relative ratios among the peaks at m/e 26, 27 and 28 (Supplementary Fig. 8) against NIST standards (Supplementary Fig. 10, 12 and 13), conclusively points to the sole origin of ECreduction. Based on both EQCM and DEMS, the alkylcarbonate produced at a potential below 0.3 V, as represented by m/dq of 78.2 in EQCM, should be primarily generated from EC reduction (Eq. 3). In addition, the increasing intensities of ethylene signature peaks (Supplementary Fig. 8 and 9) starting from 2 hr (~0.67 V), also indicate that this process between 1.05-0.60 V should be attributed to solvent reduction.

Dynamic Formation of the Interphase at the Nanoscale At the low potential of the initial lithiation near 0.10 V (Fig. 1d), the mass change nearly corresponds to neat LEDC deposition, suggesting that solvent reduction dominates the SEI formation process, with negligible Li+-intercalation. The intercalation of neat Li+ without solvent molecules can only occur in the presence of a fully-formed interphase that seals off the edge-sites of graphitic anodes. As expected,

Page 7

in the cycles following the initial lithiation, the mass change gradually approaches a constant value of 7.9 (Supplementary Fig. 14), which, within experimental error, matches the naked Li+ value (m/dq 6.9). We used different scan rates (0.1 and 1.0 mV/s) in the EQCM experiments to study how the densification of SEI evolves with kinetics (Supplementary Fig. 15). At a slower rate, lower m/dq values are achieved at the same potential points. Considering that a perfect SEI should only allow naked Li+ to intercalate (m/dq = 6.9), these lower values quantify how effectively the solvent reduction reaction is blocked, or in other words how effective the protection function of the newly-formed SEI is. A slow rate gives more time for solvent molecules to be reduced and assembled into a more densified interphase. Morphological observation on the HOPG surface via AFM (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 16) reveals that tiny island-like features with irregular distributions start appearing below 2.0 V and continue to grow down to 0.91 V (Fig. 3a), corresponding to LiF generation as identified by EQCM (Fig. 1c) and XPS (Fig. 1e) in Region II. Initial SEI formation occurs between 0.91 and 0.36 V (Fig. 3a). These main interphase components grow along the edge-sites rather than on basal planes of HOPG, consistent with the established belief that SEI formation is preceded, or even guided, by the co-intercalation of solvated Li+, which can only occur at the edge-sites30,33. These edge-site depositions continue to grow in thickness accompanied by an increase in stepheights as the potential decreases towards 0.0 V. This growth is induced by further SEIgeneration from the reduction of those solvent molecules in the primary solvation sheath of Li+ that co-intercalate into the graphitic structure in this potential region (Fig. 3a). At 0.74 V numerous particles appear (Fig. 3b, c) which gradually develop into broader and higher blisters near the edges at 0.62 V. During this process the height increases by 8.8 nm (d1). With the potential of HOPG going below 0.62 V, the step height increases by an additional ~20 nm (d2). The former height increase corresponds to the reduction peak observed in Region III (Fig. 1a), and should be attributed to the onset reduction of the already co-intercalated species (solvated Li+) between the graphene layers. In other words, it is between 0.74 and 0.62 V that SEI experiences its onset formation. The increased height below 0.62 V is caused by the simultaneous co-

Page 8

intercalation of more solvated Li+ into graphene layers along with their reduction, as the nascent SEI is still permeable at this stage. It is widely accepted that the lithiation of graphite occurs by stages34, with stage 1 being the formation of the fully lithiated state of graphite (LiC6). To investigate whether this step-wise mechanism occurs also during the SEI formation, we select a few spots on HOPG of known number of stacked graphene layers (Supplementary Fig. 17 and Supplementary Table 1). We then monitor how these monolayer, bi-layer and quadruple-layer steps respond to the potential during the initial formation of SEI (from 1.13 V to 0.51 V). We observe that around 0.88 V, where the solvated Li+ starts to cointercalate but it is not yet subject to major reductive decomposition, the step-height increases by ~1.3 nm for a monolayer-step, ~6.1 nm for a bi-layer step, and ~20.0 nm for a quadruple-layer step. Of particular interest is the height increase of 1.3 nm for the monolayer step, which is close to what would be expected for the expanded interlayer distance of a single-layer graphene structure caused by a Li+ solvated by four solvent molecules (1.56 nm)35. On average, the step height change in bilayer-step is 3 to 4 times more than that of the monolayer, while that in quadruple-layer step is 1.5~3 times more than that of bilayer step. These ratios are not proportional to the number of steps, and suggest that the expansion of the graphene interlayer distance happens at the edge-sites of every graphene layer without obvious sign of staging. When the electrode potential goes below 0.79 V, further height increases are induced due to the initial reduction and decomposition of co-intercalated solvated Li+ (Supplementary Table 1). In the range of 0.79~0.67 V, the step-heights become constant and independent from the number of layers involved (Supplementary Table 1). This is because the decomposed solvent molecules arising from the already co-intercalated species (solvated Li+) start to serve as nascent interphases at the edge-sites of graphite, hence additional solvated Li+ can no longer be intercalated at this potential range, which is also consistent with the reduced reduction current we record around 0.6 V.

Reoxidation of the Nascent Interphase Fig. 4 summarizes the CV, the mass change in EQCM (Fig. 4a) and surface morphology evolution on HOPG electrode (Fig. 4b) during the anodic scan after the

Page 9

first lithiation process. The current remains cathodic between 0~0.30 V (Region V in Fig. 4a), indicating that the SEI formation continues while the step height keeps increasing (Fig. 1b and 4b). In this region, the interphase continues to form through ECreduction; the mass accumulation m/dq = 78.2. Although this may appear to be counter-intuitive, we must recall that what determines the nature of the reaction (reduction or oxidation) is the direction of the current flow, not the scan direction. At ~0.30 V the current changes from cathodic to anodic (Region VI in Fig. 4a), the step-height of HOPG starts to decrease and the formed interphase disappears (Fig. 4b). It has been reported that part of the surface deposits observed under AFM would completely disappear before certain threshold value when the electrode potential is reversed; our observations confirm this interphasial reoxidation mechanism 36. In order to further study this peculiar process, we selected three spots on HOPG (Supplementary Fig. 18 and Supplementary Table 2): one with mostly basal surface of HOPG, and two with edge-sites of four and two steps. The heights at these three spots are monitored during both lithiation (cathodic scan) and delithiation (anodic scan) processes (Supplementary Fig. 19 and Supplementary Table 3). While the step heights at both edge sites experience dramatic changes during the scan, the step height of the basal surface remains almost constant, because of its remote location from the intercalation reactions, that is, the edge-sites. Although all the heights never revert back to their pristine state, they clearly decrease in this voltage range. As a confirmation, the mass (Fig. 4a) also decrease in this range (m/dq = -67.1; Fig. 1d) revealing loss of materials from the graphite surface. Such a mass loss is much larger than that of naked Li+ (m/dq = 6.9), thus indicates that at least part of the interphasial components are also lost. We posit that it is semi-carbonate resulting from EC reduction that generates this mass loss. Therefore, in the very first de-lithiation cycle, the nascent interphase appeared to be partially re-oxidizable, leading to both gaseous and solid products. These observations could explain the reported meta-stability of the SEI ingredients in their nascent form. We further scrutinize the above proposed mechanism with DEMS. As already shown in Fig. 2b, C2H4 is detected as the main gas generated in the first lithiation process of graphite, as expected from Eq. 3, with CO2 and O2 being around one eighth of C2H4. During the charge (delithiation) process, however, the amount of C2H4 is significantly reduced, while CO2 and O2 slightly increase. Moreover, the proportion of CO2, C2H4 and O2 is rather close to a 4:2:1 ratio, consistent with the mechanism proposed in Eq. 5. Further proofs (Supplementary Fig. 20-24) from transmission electron microscope (TEM), chemical components analysis (Supplementary Fig. 25) from X-ray photoelectron spectra (XPS), as well as a high-precision current measurement (Supplementary Fig. 26) all provide consistent support for the interphasial process as observed above.

Conclusion We have characterized the formation process of the solid-electrolyte interphase on graphitic electrode at the nanoscale in hope that this level of detail can provide useful guideline for the designing and tailoring of better interphases for new battery chemistries. We summarize the SEI formation in Fig. 5. There are five distinct chemical/electrochemical processes: (I) LiF formation at 1.5 V; (II) Co-intercalation of Li+(solvent)x at 0.88 V; (III) Initial EC-reduction at 0.74 V; (IV-V) Major EC-reduction at lower potentials; and (VI) semi-carbonate produced by EC-reduction partially reoxidization during anodic scan above 0.3 V. The capability of the SEI being re-oxidized seems to depend on its age: while the nascent SEI readily disappears upon recharging, it becomes more and more difficult to oxidize on well-cycled electrodes. Further investigation is needed to explain the origin of this aging effect.

Methods Atomic force microscope (AFM). In-situ/operando AFM measurements were performed using a Nanoscope V, MultiMode instrument (Bruker AXS) placed in the glovebox. The tapping mode was employed to collect all images with a single ScanAsyst-Fluid silicon probe (force constant of 0.7 N m-1, Bruker). The resonance frequency of this cantilever was approximately 150 kHz. The images were captured in the retrace direction at a scan rate of 0.5 Hz. Scan size was 2,000 × 2,000 nm with an aspect ratio of 1:1 and an image resolution of 512 samples/line. The liquid cell with electrochemistry setup is designed by Bruker AXS (illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 2). Highly Ordered Pyrolytic Graphite (HOPG) with an extremely smooth surface is used as the working electrode to probe dynamic reactions and morphology changes, and lithium metal is used as the reference electrode and the counter electrode. The simultaneous electrochemical tests were conducted using CHI 660E (Shanghai Chenhua) at a scan rate of 1 mV/s in the voltage range of 0 to 3.0 V (OCV is 2.55 V for HOPG in the electrochemistry setup of the in-situ AFM). Before analysis of the images,

Page 20

second-order ‘flattening’ was applied to each image. The roughness was determined by means of Nanoscope Analysis Software. Electrochemical Quartz Crystal Microbalance (EQCM). EQCM were performed using CHI 420C (Shanghai Chenhua) placed in a glovebox and a special electrolytic cell as shown in Supplementary Fig. 3 (Supplementary Section 2). The AT-cut quartz crystals coated by a gold electrode with the frequency of 8 MHz, platinum electrode, and Ag/AgCl electrode were used as the working electrode, the counter electrode, and the reference electrode, respectively. The surface area of the gold electrode on quartz crystals available for loading the samples was 0.196 cm2. The EQCM experiments were performed at a scan rate of 1 mV/s in the voltage range of 0 to 3.0 V (OCV is ∼3.0 V for graphite electrode in EQCM), using a standard three electrode conﬁguration. In this text, we convert all the potentials (vs Ag/AgCl) to potentials (vs Li/Li+) for comparison more conveniently. The preparation of the graphite deposited on gold electrode of quartz crystals (as a working electrode) is described in the Supplementary Information. Environment. The in-situ AFM and EQCM were operated in a glovebox, and the oxygen partial-pressure and moisture level remain constantly below 0.1 ppm during the entire experimental process. Differential electrochemical mass spectrometry (DEMS). DEMS was employed to analyze and identify gas evolution during SEI formation process on the graphite electrodes. The homemade cell setup is shown in Supplementary Fig. 7. Using this DEMS cell, we have studied the gas evolution (H2, C2H4, CO2 and O2) during the formation process of the SEI on graphite anode materials in 1 M LiPF6 EC/DMC electrolytes. In order to avoid interference from a Li-metal anode, which is known to be able to spontaneously reduce electrolyte and produce gaseous products upon cycling, a LiFePO4 electrode was prepared as both reference and counter electrode, which remains thermodynamically-stable within the electrochemical stability window of the non-aqueous electrolyte. The graphite electrodes and LiFePO4 electrodes (as a reference electrode) are prepared as described in the Supplementary Information. The morphology measurement. The morphologies and thickness of the samples were analyzed by transmission electron microscope (TEM, FEI Tecnai G2 F30).
