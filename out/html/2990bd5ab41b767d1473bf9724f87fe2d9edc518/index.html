Although non-aqueous Li-ion batteries possess significantly higher energy density than their aqueous counterparts, the latter can be more feasible for grid-scale applications when cost, safety and cycle life are taken into consideration. Moreover, aqueous Zn-ion batteries have energy storage advantage over alkali based batteries as they can employ Zn metal as the negative electrode, dramatically increasing energy density. However, their development is plagued by a limited choice of positive electrodes that often show poor rate capability and inadequate cycle life. Here we report a vanadium oxide bronze pillared by interlayer Zn2+ ion and water (Zn0.25V2O5.nH2O), as the positive electrode for a Zn cell. A reversible Zn2+ ion (de)intercalation storage process at fast rates, with more than one Zn2+ per formula unit (a capacity up to 300 mAh g-1) is characterised. The Zn cell offers an energy density of ~ 450 Wh/L and exhibits a capacity retention of more than 80% over 1,000 cycles, with no dendrite formation at the Zn electrode.

Given the looming concerns of climate change, sustainable energy resources such as solar and wind have entered the global spotlight, triggering the search for reliable, low cost electrochemical energy storage.1,2 Among the various options, lithium ion batteries are currently the most attractive candidates due to their high energy density, and foothold in the marketplace.3,4 However, many factors (cost,5 safety,6,7 lifetime) will likely limit their large scale applications, and dictate against their use in stationary grid storage where low cost and durability are more of a concern than weight. Batteries based on Al3+,8,9 Mg2+,10,11,12 and especially aqueous rechargeable batteries (ARBs), which utilize low-cost and safe water-based electrolytes are promising alternatives.13 The high ionic conductivities of aqueous electrolytes (up to 1 S cm-1) compared to non-aqueous (~1-10 mS cm-1) favour high rate capabilities suitable for emerging applications.2

Page 3

This is particularly important for grid storage where fast response balancing systems for frequency regulation are crucial.14 Research effort in ARBs has spanned Li+ intercalation materials,15,16,17,18 naturally abundant Na+ and K+ systems,19,20,21,22 and more recently, the intercalation chemistry of divalent cations that engage in multiple electron transfer.8,23,24,25,26,27 Among the latter, Zn2+ based batteries are particularly advantageous13 owing to the following properties of Zn that include: high abundance and large production which makes it inexpensive; 28 non-toxicity; 28 low redox potential (-0.76 V vs. SHE) compared to other negative electrode materials used in aqueous batteries;23 and stability in water due to a high overpotential for hydrogen evolution.28 This stability renders a large voltage window (~2 V) for aqueous zinc-ion batteries (AZIBs) employing a metallic Zn negative electrode (“anode”). We report the properties of a vanadium bronze, Zn0.25V2O5.nH2O that possesses two-electron redox centres (vanadium) and a

layered structure containing intercalated divalent cations and water. Layered VnOm oxides were the subject of much past investigation for non-aqueous and aqueous alkali (Li and Na) ion batteries.15,16,33 Today, the low cost of vanadium (USD ~ $2.5/lb)34 has sparked renewed interest in its many electrochemical technologies. To capitalize on this, we developed a microwave hydrothermal technique for the rapid synthesis of single crystal Zn0.25V2O5.nH2O nanobelts and a versatile approach to fabricate freestanding film electrodes. Interlayer metal ions (MxVnOm, M = metal ion) and/or structural water in this layered oxide act as pillars, providing structural stability during cycling and mobilizing cation migration (Fig. 1). Favourable structural features, nanoscale morphology and a compact electrode structure act in concert to enable high specific capacities (250-300 mAh g-1) and long term cyclability (>1000 cycles) at ≥ 99% coulombic efficiency at high current rates up to 15C (1C = 300 mA g-1). Operando X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis performed during electrochemical Zn2+ (de)intercalation confirms excellent structural reversibility over long term cycling. Synthesis and characterization of Zn0.25V2O5.nH2O nanobelts

Our scalable microwave approach circumvents the prolonged heat treatment required for traditional hydrothermal synthesis of nanostructured vanadium oxide materials,35,36 resulting in the synthesis of ultralong Zn0.25V2O5.nH2O nanobelts in hours. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images (Fig. 2a and 2b) shows the unique nanobelt morphology, and high aspect ratio, purity and homogeneity of the synthesized material that enables construction of free-standing electrode membranes. The nanobelts exhibit a flat ribbon like morphology, being tens of micrometres in length and 100-150 nm in width (Fig. 2b). Their thin rectangular cross section of ~10-20 nm gives rise to the texturing observed in the XRD pattern (see below). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images (Fig. 2c, 2d and Supplementary Fig. 2) confirm their anisotropic and flat

Page 5

morphology, and single crystalline nature. The lattice resolved, representative HRTEM image of an individual nanobelt (Fig. 2d) shows a spacing of 0.537 nm that corresponds to the d-spacing of the (200) planes. This set of planes runs parallel to the length of the nanobelt, confirming the b axis to be the long growth direction, and the nanobelt width of ~150 nm to correspond to the a direction, the intercalation-path length. These morphological features favour the excellent rate behaviour of the material as discussed below. The XRD pattern of the pristine (Supplementary Fig. 3a) and ground nanobelts (Fig. 3a) is dominated by (00l) reflections, revealing a high degree of preferred orientation owing to their high width/thickness ratio. The absence of any impurity peaks proves the single phase nature of the material. The data are in accord with that of large Zn0.25V2O5.nH2O platelets,36 whose single crystal structure solution36 served as the structural model for our refinement of the XRD data (Fig. 3a). The refinement converged in the targeted composition of Zn0.25V2O5.H2O with similar lattice parameters (𝑃1̅ triclinic symmetry; see Supplementary Discussion 2 for details). The structure, shown in Fig. 3b (& 3c), consists of a double-sheet type 2D V2O5 framework, analogous to the V2O5 layers in the δ phase,37 extending infinitely in the ab plane. The ordered interlayer ZnO6 octahedra act as pillars. They pin the 2D V2O5 layers into a framework enclosing intersecting paths in the ab plane along the [110] and [1-10] directions which accommodate additional Zn2+ on discharge (accompanied by reduction of V5+ for charge balance). Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy analysis (ICP-OES) and a thermogravimetric study (Supplementary Fig. 3b) confirmed a chemical composition of Zn0.25V2O5.nH2O (n ≈ 0.85); i.e., V4.75+. The spacious layered framework also permits insertion of water molecules from the electrolyte into the interlayer space, further expanding the galleries for facile Zn2+ intercalation; this occurs

synergistically with the topotactic deintercalation of absorbed H2O (Fig. 1; Fig. 3c) as revealed by operando diffraction (see below). Electrochemistry

The operational voltage window of the system was evaluated by voltammetry (see Methods). In 1 M aqueous ZnSO4 as the electrolyte, using a Zn negative electrode and stainless steel as the positive current collector, a working potential window of ~2.4 V was very effective within the H2 and O2 evolution potentials (Supplementary Fig. 4). No gas evolution was observed, as confirmed by operando mass spectrometry analysis (Supplementary Fig. 5 and Supplementary Methods 1). For assembling the Zn battery, the ultralong morphology of the nanofibres enables the easy formation of freestanding dense mat-like film electrodes (Supplementary Fig. 6). The use of water as the solvent with low-cost natural binders makes the process very cost effective38 and environmentally friendly compared to solvents and fluorinated binders used for Li-ion electrode fabrication.39 Although cycling in a wide window resulted in higher specific capacities, structural stress generated from insertion of a very zinc fraction caused capacity fading. However, in the 0.5 - 1.4 V window vs. Zn, cycling was highly reversible and durable (Fig. 1b). ICP-OES analysis of the electrolyte extracted from a pouch cell after >100 cycles showed only trace vanadium, indicating no dissolution (< 0.01% of the active material; see Supplementary Discussion 3). This robust behaviour likely owes to strong bonding of the vanadium oxide layers to the interlayer Zn which remains on charging the cell (i.e., to Zn0.25V2O5). Fig. 4a shows the galvanostatic charge-discharge profiles at a current density of 300 mA g-1. A very high capacity of 282 mAh g-1 is initially obtained, corresponding to a ~2.2 electron redox process, of which 278 mAh g-1 is recovered upon charging.

Zn0.25V2O5•nH2O + 1.1 Zn2+ + 2.2 e- ↔ Zn1.35V2O5•nH2O

The 1C rate was defined as 300 mA g-1. This is based on the maximum capacity achieved at a moderate current density of 50 mA g-1 (C/6; see Methods and Supplementary Fig. 7) which yields intercalation of 1.2 Zn2+ (i.e., Zn1.45V2O5•nH2O). In the subsequent 8-10 cycles, minor changes in the voltage profile suggest a slight structural modification, reflected in the corresponding differential capacity curves which show a subtle evolution in this region (Supplementary Fig 8a). Subsequently Zn2+ (de)intercalation is highly reversible at an average voltage of ~0.81 V (vs. Zn/Zn2+) with a perfectly reproducible voltage profile and differential capacity curves (Supplementary Fig 8b). The predominantly sloping discharge-charge profiles suggest that a solidsolution type process is associated with Zn2+ (de)intercalation. Increasing the current density has a very small effect on the discharge capacity (Fig. 4b), pointing to a flexible, highly Zn-ion conducting structural framework. From 1C to 4C/8C the highest delivered capacity decreases only slightly (7 % of 1C capacity) to ~260 mAh g-1 (Supplementary Fig. 7). Even at 15C and 20C (Fig 4b), significantly high discharge capacities of 223 mAh g-1 and 183 mAh g-1, respectively, are delivered, demonstrating a AZIB system with not only impressive energy density (see Supplementary Discussion 4) but also superior power capability. At 15C and 20C, the highest obtainable capacities are delivered in 3 and 2 minutes of charge/discharge time, respectively. Despite its double valence, the Zn2+ ion diffusion coefficient (~10-9-10-10 cm2 S-1) obtained by applying galvanostatic intermittent titration technique measurements (GITT; Supplementary Fig. 9 and see Supplementary Discussion 5) is 103-104 higher than the 1D Li+ diffuser LiFePO4 (in the single phase region)40 and 102 fold higher than the promising high rate Li-ion anode Li4Ti5O1241.

Page 8

While diffusion measurements are difficult to compare owing to variation in protocol amongst researchers, the value is comparable to the commercial Li-ion cathode LiCoO2.42 However, considering the oxide exhibits an electronic conductivity of 3.5 x 10-5 S cm-1 (measured on a film by a two probe method), the rate behaviour and extent of material utilization would be limited for micron-sized particles. In this regard, the nanobelt morphology is vital to enable accelerated charge transfer at the electrode/electrolyte interface and a shorter path length for Zn2+ and electronic transport. The high rate performance is evidenced in the Ragone plot (Fig. 4c), showing the tradeoff between energy and power density of the present AZIB system in comparison to α-MnO223 and Zn3[Fe(CN)6]2.23 Zn0.25V2O5.nH2O exhibits the highest energy density of the three at high power and delivers a steady and high energy density over a wide range of power. The performance is further reflected in the rate data (Fig. 4d). Upon continuous cycling under variable current densities, the specific capacities dropped only marginally with doubling/quadrupling the rates, and immediately recovered with their reversal. The cycle life performance at a representative 8C rate is shown in Fig. 4e. An impressive 93% of the highest achievable capacity (260 mAh g-1) is available after 500 cycles and more than 81% is retained even after 1000 cycles. Consistent with this performance, no dendritic growth on the Zn surface was evidence in post-mortem analysis of the cells even at the highest rates. The coulombic efficiency, (≥ 99% for the duration, and above, for all rates), points to quantitative utilization of the electrical charge. Studies at higher rates of 10C and 15C (Supplementary Fig. 10) show equally good capacity retention - 80% and 82%, respectively, after 1000 cycles demonstrating excellent structural stability and facile Zn ion kinetics. Proof of Zn2+ Intercalation and Structural Evolution

Page 9

Both ICP-OES (Supplementary Table 1) and quantitative energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) analysis (Supplementary Fig. 11) of the discharged and charged electrode indicate that the amount of Zn2+ inserted/extracted (1.1 per formula unit at a 1C rate) correlates closely to the coulombic capacity delivered during discharge/charge, indicating a highly reversible Zn2+ storage process. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis (Fig. 5) provides further proof. The pristine and charged electrode show only one Zn 2p component (2p3/2: 1021.59 eV) due to the native Zn2+ site in Zn0.25V2O5. Upon discharge, a second Zn 2p feature appears at a higher binding energy (2p3/2: 1023.91 eV) corresponding to the intercalated Zn. The difference in the binding energies of the two Zn sites can be correlated to their distinctly different bonding interactions43 with the oxygen atoms in Zn0.25V2O5•nH2O. Although the precise nature of the bonding of the intercalated Zn2+ is not yet elucidated, the XPS data indicates weaker interactions between intercalated Zn and the oxide layer. These interactions lead to a modest bonding rearrangement and less structural stress accompanying (de)intercalation, rendering good rate performance and long term cyclability. Reversible electrochemical reduction of the V2O5 framework as a consequence of Zn2+/2e- intercalation is evident in the V 2p XPS region (Fig. 5). The V4+ signal (2p3/2: 515.8 eV) intensifies strongly upon discharge, and a V3+ component also appears, in accord with the reduction to an average valence of V3.65+ based on the electrochemistry. This is fully reversed upon charge (see Supplementary Discussion 6), where the pristine V 2p spectrum is regenerated. In spite of almost exclusive presence of the (001) set of reflections - which can only unveil the evolution of the interlayer spacing (equal to the c parameter) - significant insights into the Zn2+ (de)intercalation and dynamic structural evolution were elucidated from an operando XRD investigation. Structural modification of pristine Zn0.25V2O5.nH2O upon immersion in the

Page 10

electrolyte was observed, corresponding to an increase in the interlayer distance from 10.8 Å to 12.9 Å. The process is reversible: upon drying, the pristine diffraction pattern is regenerated (Supplementary Fig. 12). The increase in the interlayer distance by 2.1 Å upon immersion is ascribed to water intercalation. The value is in excellent accord with values of 2 - 2.5 Å reported for intercalation of two layers of water in layered oxides44,45 and layered double hydroxide46. The charged material ZnxV2O5 also undergoes structural evolution in the electrolyte solution for x ≤ 0.3 (pristine composition), exemplified by the growth of new diffraction peaks during the rest period after the 1st and 2nd charge (Fig. 6a). The good structural reversibility of the system is shown by the seamless overlap of the diffraction patterns of the charged and discharged states during the first and second cycles. Even after 50 cycles (and 100 cycles, not shown here) at a high rate (2,400 mA g-1), the cell parameters of the charged and discharged electrode correspond well with the electrode at similar zinc content during the first two cycles at a slow rate. The only difference is the slight broadening of the reflections after prolonged high rate cycling, induced because of slight structural stress. The insertion mechanism in ZnxV2O5 was investigated during the second cycle (Fig. 6b). The structural changes accompanying Zn2+ (de)intercalation are complex. Two solid solution regimes (0.25 < x < 0.55 and 0.55 < x < 1.4), separated by a phase transition (at x ≈ 0.55) exist. Up to x ~ 0.55 (insertion of 0.3 Zn2+) only a small contraction of the interlayer distance is observed, consistent with an increased screening of the interlayer electrostatic repulsion as the Zn2+ content increases. For zinc concentrations between 0.55 and 0.6, the interlayer distance sharply contracts from 12.3 Å to 11.0 Å, similar to the 10.8 Å interlayer spacing observed in the pristine material prior to water intercalation from the electrolyte. This results from the expulsion of water from the interlayer, as shown schematically in Fig. 1, and Fig. 2c, with additional effect from intercalated

Page 11

zinc that further pins the layers together. The new set of (00l) reflections, with l = 1 to 7, show a constant d-spacing in the 0.55 < x < 1.4 composition range, exhibiting only an increase in intensity with zinc content. The intensity and position evolution of the other peaks, i.e., at 26, 32 and 40 °, clearly shows that the structure continues to evolve upon Zn insertion. This can be attributed to different Zn2+/vacancy ordering patterns as the zinc content increases. The charge process follows the reverse evolution of discharge. The charged phase ultimately achieves a 13.4 Å interlayer spacing, but it transforms during relaxation to yield a diffraction pattern similar to that at the beginning of the cycle (see Fig. 6a). Strongly preferred orientation of the fibres together with water loss ex-situ preclude a more detailed structural analysis. Conclusions The performance of intercalation-based rechargeable batteries inevitably depends on the combination of host electrodes and guest ionic species, and the charge of the guest ionic species dictates the electrochemical properties to a large extent.

We, and others, have found that

multivalent ion-intercalation batteries function significantly better with aqueous electrolytes than non-aqueous.12,47,48 Co-intercalated water molecules buffer the high charge density of the multivalent ions and lower the activation energy for charge transfer at the electrode interface. This requires a flexible framework structure, however. As shown by the operando XRD analysis, the water molecules play a crucial role by reversibly expanding and contracting the layered galleries of Zn0.25V2O5 to allow Zn2+ ingress/egress, leading to good kinetics and high rate performance. The indigenous Zn ions stabilize the layered structure, thereby ensuring long term cycling stability. Furthermore, the (a) ability of the nanostructure to accommodate the strain generated during electrochemical Zn2+ cycling, (b) short Zn2+ diffusion length and large electrode/electrolyte interface area enabled by the thin nanobelt morphology, (c) good interparticle contacts, conductive

Page 12

wiring with the carbon additives and facile release of strain allowed by the compact, flexible electrode architecture, underpins the superior electrochemical performance presented in this study. Our AZIB system based on Zn2+ intercalation in bilayered Zn0.25V2O5.nH2O displays a specific energy of ~250 Wh kg-1 (cathode only), a capacity of 220 mAh g-1 at a 15 C rate, and a capacity retention of more than 80% in 1,000 cycles. The estimated volumetric energy density is 450 Wh/L, with costs below $65/KWh (see Supplementary Discussion 4). The combination of low-cost aqueous based electrode fabrication, an aqueous electrolyte, a metallic Zn negative electrode, scalable processing of the Zn2+ host material, and ease of overall battery assembly help in meeting the requirements essential for large scale storage applications.

Methods Synthesis of Zn0.25V2O5.nH2O nanobelts. We have adopted a rapid and scalable microwave hydrothermal method for the synthesis of highly homogeneous Zn0.25V2O5.nH2O nanobelts. Water is known to interact strongly with the microwave radiation via a dipolar-microwave interaction, leading to rapidly superheated local regions in the reaction media. In contrast to typical hydrothermal methods where slow heating occurs mainly via a convection mechanism, heating of the entire reaction media through penetration of microwaves triggers rapid intercalation-exfoliation and cleavage of V2O5 into nanosheets and finally into ZnxV2O5 nanobelts. In a typical procedure, 2 mmol V2O5 was dispersed in 50 ml of 15:1 water/acetone (volume) mixture with 1.3 mmol of zinc acetate and transferred to a sealed TeflonTM vessel. The vessels were fitted to a rotor equipped with temperature and pressure sensors and placed in an Anton Parr microwave synthesis system (Synthos 3000). The system temperature was raised to 180°C in 10 minutes and maintained for 90 minutes. The as-synthesized product was thoroughly washed with distilled water followed by a small amount of isopropanol and dried at 60°C for 24 h. 12

Characterization methods. X-ray diffraction was performed on a Bruker D8-Advance powder diffractometer equipped with a Vantec-1 detector, using Cu-Kα radiation (λ= 1.5418Å) in the range from 5° to 80° (2θ) at a step size of 0.025° using Bragg-Brentano geometry. X-ray data refinement was carried out by conventional Rietveld refinement method using Bruker-AXS TOPAS 4.2 software (Bruker-AXS, 2008). The background, scale factor, zero point, lattice parameters, and coefficients for the peak shape function were iteratively refined until convergence was achieved. A correction factor using a special harmonics series was also applied due to the high degree of preferred orientation in the diffraction pattern. Due to the high degree of preferred orientation in the diffraction data, atomic positions and occupancies were not refined. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed in alumina crucible using a TA Instruments SDT Q600 in air. Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) analysis was carried out using Prodigy High Dispersion ICP, Teledyne Leeman Laboratories. The morphologies of the samples were examined by field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM, LEO 1530) equipped with an energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) attachment. For the analysis using EDX, the intensities were corrected for the host matrix effect (ZAF). For high resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM), samples were dispersed in iso-propanol by ultrasonication and loaded onto a carbon coated copper grid for imaging on a FEI Titan 80-300. XPS analysis was performed on a Thermo ESCALAB 250 instrument configured with a monochromatic Al Kα (1486.6 eV). For Ex-situ studies, the cycled electrodes were washed with copious amount of water and extensively dried in ambient atmosphere. Battery cycling. For the electrochemical performance evaluation, freestanding membrane-type electrodes were fabricated by a facile method. In a typical process, nanofibres were mixed with conducting nanocarbon Super P® and a water based composite binder (carboxymethylcellulose,

(CMC)) and styrene-butadiene rubber(SBR) (CMC/SBR= 2:1) in a 70:27:3 weight ratio. The mixture was dispersed in small amount of water using an ultrasonic mixer to obtain a stable homogeneous ink which was filtered through a Durapore® DVPP 0.65 µm filtration membrane. After drying at 60°C the composite film was punched into 1 cm2 electrode coins. The electrochemical properties were investigated in PFA based Swagelok® type cells using 1 M ZnSO4 in water as the electrolyte and titanium rods as the current collector. Galvanostatic cycling studies were performed using a multichannel Biologic VMP3 potentiostat/galvanostat. As the highest capacity of ~300 mAh g-1 was achieved at a moderate current density of 50 mA g-1, a current density of 300 mA g-1 (~1.5-2.1 mA cm-2 for 5-7 mg cm-2 active material loading) was defined as the 1C rate and the remainder of the current rates were defined relative to this value. Operational voltage window of the aqueous Zn-ion battery. Unlike electrode materials used in non-aqueous electrolyte systems, the working potentials of electroactive materials in aqueous electrolytes must operate within the potential window of the water splitting reactions, i.e., between the H2 (HER) and O2 (OER) evolution reactions. These reactions are catalytic in nature, depending on the pH of the electrolyte, and hence are sensitive to electrolyte composition and electrode materials. In-depth linear sweep voltammetry and cyclic voltammetry analysis showed reversible zinc deposition and stripping occurs at a potential higher than the HER and the intercalation of Zn2+ into Zn0.25V2O5 is well below the OER in the 1 M ZnSO4/H2O electrolyte used in this study. The practical working voltage window of ~2.4 V in 1 M ZnSO4 is shown in Supplementary Fig. 4. Details of this electrochemical study will be presented elsewhere. EDX analysis of the electrodes. For the ex-situ EDX investigation, the electrodes were electrochemically cycled at a 1C rate. After 2nd discharge/charge the electrodes were thoroughly washed with a copious amount of water and dried in air. For quantification, intensities were

corrected for the host matrix effect (ZAF). The Zn/V ratio increased after discharge, corresponding to an intercalation of 1.1 Zn2+ per formula unit which is removed upon charge. The morphology of the nanobelts remained unchanged during the electrochemical process. Operando XRD. Operando diffraction experiments were conducted in a homemade cell, mounted on a PANalytical Empyrean diffractometer. Diffraction patterns were collected in Bragg–Brentano geometry using Cu-Kα radiation and a PIXcel detector with a Ni Kβ filter.
