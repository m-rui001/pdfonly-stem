Linear Combination Fitting: Linear combination fitting (LCF) is a standard analytic tool in XAS software Athena. The analysis is based on the principle that XANES spectrum of a mixture containing multicomponents is composed by the linear superposition of the XANES spectra of individual component. This method is often used to quantify the fraction of the same metal element distributed in the different phases of the mixture.7 The method involves changing the weighing factors of the individual component XANES spectra to generate a composite spectrum simulating that of the unknown mixture. Through computational least-square fitting, the simulated XANES with the best match to an unknown sample is obtained and the weight fractions of the element in each component are derived. The LCF is calculated using normalized μ(E) spectra of the compounds in the mixture at narrow energy range around the absorption edge. Our analysis was performed within an energy range of -20 eV below to +20 eV above the copper k-edge. LCF reconstructs the sample spectrum using a combination of normalized model spectra of CuO, Cu(AcAc) 2 and Cu foil since both cupric oxide and copper metal cluster were found by XRD and EXAFS in the higher Cu loading samples. It is worth noting that there is no standard XANES for Cu SA. We selected Cu(AcAc)2 since it contains the single Cu+2 ligated by four oxygen from two acetylacetonates. The hydrocarbons in Cu(AcAc)2 has little interference to the electronic structure of Cu, therefore it serves as a good reference. Our EXAFS analysis on Cu/C-0.1, the sample with the lowest copper loading and highest dispersion, found that Cu is nearly all coordinated by ca. four oxygen. To verify the selection of this model compound, we also applied Cu/C-0.1 in place of Cu(AcAc)2 with as SA reference for LCF analysis assuming that the copper in this sample is completely atomically dispersed. LCF fitting plots in both methods are provided in Supplementary Figure 7. The weight fraction of Cu in each phase and the quality of the fitting (R-factor and reduced χ2) are provided in Supplementary Table 4 and Supplementary Table 5, respectively. Operando XAS characterization of Cu SA catalyst To investigate the electronic configuration and coordination structure of the Cu SA catalysts under operating conditions, we employed an operando electrochemical setup for XAS measurements.9 In a homemade electrochemical cell, the electrochemistry was performed over the catalyst coated on graphene sheet as a working electrode with an Ag/AgCl reference electrode and a platinum wire as the counter electrode. The electrochemical cell contains 0.1 M KHCO3 solution as the electrolyte constantly purged by a flowing CO2 (Supplementary Fig. 22). All XAS data were collected at Cu K-edge in fluorescence mode. Cu/C-0.4 catalyst was used as the representative sample. Cu foil and CuO were used as the references. Postmortem XAS characterization of Cu SA catalyst To better understand the oxidation state and coordination structural changes in the Cu SA samples, we conducted the x-ray absorption spectroscopic (XAS) studies on the Cu/C-0.4 sample before (Pre Cu/C) and after (Post Cu/C) the chronoamperometric measurement under -0.7 V vs RHE for 16 hours. Their extended x-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) and x-ray absorption near edge structure (XANES) spectra, together with Cu(AcAc)2 as a reference, are shown in Fig. 4 (a) and (b). Radial distribution functions (RDFs) derived from the Fourier transform of k2-weighted R-space χ function from EXAFS showed mainly a one Cu-O shell for Pre and Post Cu/C catalysts at both loadings. The coordination numbers and shell radii are similar to that in Cu(AcAc)2, suggesting copper remains to be as atomically dispersed Cu2+ ligated by about four oxygen before and after the extended period of the electrocatalytic reaction. Cu SA is mostly likely bonded on the carbon substrate through surface oxygen. The XANES of both pre and post Cu/C-0.4 sample was found to be similar to that of Cu(AcAc)2, indicating that the copper oxidation states of the samples are almost in +2. The XANES result further confirmed the EXAFS analysis. XANES and EXAFS analysis We first analyzed the XANES spectra of Cu/C-0.4 at Cu K-edge prior to applying any potential on the cell (Fig. 4c). We found that the catalyst at this stage (pre Cu/C-0.4) has the XANES feature similar to that of Cu(AcAc)2, in which copper is in Cu+2 state. As soon as the cathodic potential was turned on to -0.7 V vs RHE, the Cu edge energy shifted to lower (green line). The XANES feature resembles to the superposition of metallic Cu and Cu2O, suggesting Cu2+ ions were reduced to the mixture of Cu0 and Cu+1. Further decreasing the cathodic potential to -1.0 V vs RHE led to little change in the copper XANES spectrum. This observation indicates Cu SA in our catalysts is highly sensitive to the reducing potential and can be readily converted to lower oxidation state. Such observation is also consistent with our computational results of free energy change upon reduction of Cu+2 to Cu+1 and Cu0 by (H+ + e-) (Supplementary Fig. 24). Interestingly, once the potentiostat for the electrochemical cell was turned off, the copper in the catalyst 7

Computational Details Structures of the Pre-catalysts: As described in the experimental section, the formation of the single-site Cu catalyst results from a sequence of reactions: the dissociation of bulk Cu to Li-solvated Cu atoms, the conversion of Li to LiOH, the oxygenation of carbon black surface, and the conversion of single Cu to Cu(OH)2 under humidified air and alkaline solution at ambient temperature. Explicitly, the interaction between LiOH and carbon leads to hydroxyl decorated graphene structures (Supplementary Figure 23 a). 8

Page 9

The interaction between Cu(OH)2 and hydroxyl decorated graphene result in the single-site Cu pre-catalyst (Supplementary Figure 23 b). Our calculations show that this reaction is energetically favorable (Supplementary Figure 23 b). The resulting pre-catalyst has a four-coordinate Cu+2 site coordinated with two oxyl groups on graphene and two H2O ligands. The calculated pre-catalyst Cu site agrees well with the experimental XRD and ex-situ XAS measurements, in which the coordination number of Copper is 3.22 (± 0.66). Potential Catalytic Sites under Reaction Condition: We hypothesize that under electrochemical reaction condition, the pre-catalyst could interact with (H+ + e-) and form reduced Cu sites (i.e., Cu(I) and Cu(0)). Although the Cu pre-catalyst prefers to have a four-coordinate site with two H2O ligands, the H2O ligands could be replaced by the adsorbate species during CO2 reduction, and vice versa. Thus, to focus on the intrinsic interactions between the adsorbate species and the catalyst, the two-coordinate Cu sites with no H2O ligands were considered as the catalytic sites (Supplementary Figure 24 a). Our calculations show that the reduction of the Cu(II) site by (H+ + e-) to Cu(I) and Cu(0) is thermodynamically favorable (Supplementary Figure 24). In addition, our operando x-ray absorption spectroscopy identified a dynamic and reversible transformation from copper single atoms to Cu trimers and tetramers Cun (n = 3 and 4) under the electrochemical potential reaction. Therefore, upon applying the potential, the Cu single atoms aggregate into small Cu clusters as shown in Supplementary Figure 24 b. These supported three/four-atom Cu clusters provide active reaction centers in the electrochemical reduction of CO2. We have adopted hydroxylated graphene supported Cu3 as a model system to investigate the electrochemical reaction pathways. Reaction Mechanism of Electrocatalytic CO2 Reduction on Cu Catalysts: The reaction mechanisms of CO2 electrochemical reduction to CO, HCOOH, CH4 and CH3CH2OH were investigated thoroughly for the rGO supported Cu3 cluster. Reaction free energies of each elementary step in a proposed reaction network (Supplementary Figure 25) were calculated and the most thermodynamically favorable pathway for each product was identified. Supplementary Table 12 shows the calculated free energies for all elementary steps in the reaction network. Supplementary Table 13 shows the calculated rate limiting potentials (UL) for CO, HCOOH, CH4 and CH3CH2OH formation. Overall, the calculations show that the production of CH3CH2OH goes through the formate (HCOO*) pathway of CO2 reduction, where the formation of HCOO* is 0.83 eV lower than the formation of COOH*. It is followed by the reduction of another CO 2 to form a co-adsorbed state (HCOO* + HCOO*). This continues with the reduction to (HCOOH* + HCOOH*). Then one HCOOH* reduces to form CH3* in the presence of the co-adsorbed HCOOH*. Similarly, the second HCOOH* gets reduced to form H2CO*, co-adsorbed with CH3*. These two fragments undergo a C-C coupling to form CH3CH2O*. And the final reduction by (H+ + e-) generates ethanol (Supplementary Figure 9

25 and Supplementary Table 12). It is worth mentioning that this co-adsorption pathway lowers the rate limiting step of HCOO* + (H+ + e-) → HCOOH* by 0.33 eV in comparison with the single adsorbent pathway. On the other hand, the formation of HCOOH and CH4 also follow the formate pathway, and CH4 formation shares a similar pathway with CH3CH2OH formation from the first hydrogenation HCOO* to the formation of CH3OH*. We found that the calculated mechanisms of these products tend to agree well with the general trend in the experimental Faradaic efficiencies (FE) (Fig. 2 b). For instance, the FE of CH3CH2OH tends to follow a similar trend with that of CH4 (i.e., the FE of CH3CH2OH decreases as that of CH4 decreases from -0.8 to -1.2 V), but follows an opposite trend with that of CO. This indicates that CH3CH2OH could undergo a similar reaction mechanism with CH4, but a competitive mechanism with CO. For CH3CH2OH formation, the calculated overpotential (the difference between the equilibrium potential and the rate limiting potential, 0.49 V) agrees well with the experimental observation (0.39 ~ 0.49 V). In addition to CH3CH2OH formation, the calculated limiting potentials for CO, HCOOH, CH4 formation agree qualitatively well with the experimental observation of the onset potentials. For example, CO and HCOOH formation were calculated to have low limiting potentials, while CH4 formation requires a high potential due to the strong binding of OH*. This trend agrees well with the experimental FE (Fig. 2 b), where CO, HCOOH and CH3CH2OH products were observed starting from low applied potentials (-0.4 V), while CH4 was only obtained at -0.8V or higher potentials. Computationally, we have also investigated the CO* pathway, which is commonly seen for electrocatalytic

CO2RR on Cu nanoparticles and surfaces. The comparison of these two pathways are shown in Supplementary Figure 26. Our calculations showed that HCOO* pathway is energetically more favorable than the CO* pathway for supported Cu3. First of all, for the first (H++e-) transfer step, the formation of HCOO* is 0.83 eV lower than the formation of COOH*. Thereby, from the thermodynamic point of view, the formation of HCOO* is dominant. Secondly, in the following steps, the rate determining step CO*HCO* demands a higher potential UL=-0.76 V, compared to the value of -0.41 V for the HCOO* pathway. Methodology: Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed to investigate the active site structure of the Cu catalyst and the reaction mechanism of the electrocatalytic CO 2 reduction. All the structures and total energies (E) were calculated using the PBE functional10 with a plane wave basis set implemented in the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP), version 5.3.3)11-14. The energy cutoff of 400 eV was used, and the Γ-point and a 3 × 3 × 1 k-point mash were used to sample the Brillouin zones for the molecules and graphene supported systems, respectively. For the reduced graphene oxide model (rGO) (Supplementary Figure 23 a), a unit cell with 72 carbon atoms was built, and a 15 Å vacuum space above 10

the surface was created to separate the surface slab and its periodic image. All the molecules were placed in a 20 × 20 × 20 Å box. All calculations were conducted within the framework of spin-polarized DFT and the spin states were fully relaxed. The computed results of magnetic moments of all surface species are listed in Supplementary Table 14. Each electrochemical reaction step during catalysis considered in the present work involves a (H+ + e-) pair transfer from the solution to the adsorbed species on the catalyst. The free energy change of each (H+ + e-) pair transfer reaction was calculated using the computational hydrogen electrode (CHE) method suggested by Norskov et al.15-18 The reaction free energy of each elementary reaction can be calculated using Eqn. 1: ∆𝑮𝒆𝒍𝒆 = 𝝁[𝐏𝐫𝐨𝐝𝐮𝐜𝐭] − 𝝁[𝐑𝐞𝐚𝐜𝐭𝐚𝐧𝐭] − 𝟎. 𝟓𝝁[𝐇𝟐(𝐠) ] + 𝒆𝑼 (1) where ΔGele represents the free energy change of an elementary electrochemical step, μ is chemical potential and U is applied electrical potential. When U = 0 V, ΔGele determine the limiting potential (UL) of elementary hydrogenation reaction. The CHE method circumvents the explicit quantum mechanical calculations for solvated protons and all the solvent effect on geometries and free energies were neglected. The free energy of each system is calculated by adding a Gibbs free energy correction (including contributions from zeropoint energy, enthalpy and entropy) to the total energy calculated in VASP. All the Gibbs free energy corrections were calculated in gas phase at 25°C using the standard statistical mechanical model employed by Gaussian09 program package19. The PBE functional with 6-31G** basis set and a scaling factor of 0.986 were used to calculate the frequencies. For graphene supported systems, only the frequencies of the molecular adsorbates that are relevant to CO2 reduction were calculated, and the Cu atom and the graphene support were kept frozen during frequency calculations, assuming that changes in the vibrations of the catalyst surface were minimal. All the gaseous molecules (CO2, H2, CO and CH4) and graphene supported systems were calculated under the standard pressure, 101325Pa, while the liquid phase molecules (H2O, HCOOH, CH3OH, and CH3CH2OH) were calculated under their corresponding vapor pressure. The solvation effect at the water-solid interface was taken into account by adding an energy correction to the calculated total energy of certain adsorbates17. For instance, OH* was found to be stabilized in liquid water by approximately 0.5 eV, and hydroxyl group that is indirectly bound to the surface/cluster through other atoms, *ROH and *ROOH, was found to be stabilized by 0.25 eV. Also, CO* was stablized by 0.1 eV, which was applied to both CO* and CHO*20.
