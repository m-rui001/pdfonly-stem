Abstract: Discovery of earth-abundant electrocatalysts to replace iridium for the oxygen evolution reaction (OER) in a proton exchange membrane water electrolyzer (PEMWE) represents a critical step in reducing the cost for green hydrogen production. We report here a nanofibrous cobalt spinel 5

catalyst co-doped with lanthanum and manganese prepared from a zeolitic imidazolate framework embedded in electrospun polymer fiber. The catalyst demonstrated a low overpotential of 353 millivolts at 10 milliamperes per square centimeter and a low degradation for OER over 360 hours in acidic electrolyte. A PEMWE containing this catalyst at the anode demonstrated a current density of 2000 milliamperes per square centimeter at 2.47 volts (Nafion® 115 membrane) or 4000

milliamperes per square centimeter at 3.00 volt (Nafion® 212 membrane), and low degradation in an accelerated-stress-test. One-Sentence Summary: PGM-free catalyst showed promising performance as the replacement for Ir in a proton exchange membrane water electrolyzer

Main Text: Low-temperature water electrolysis can rapidly produce environmentally sustainable, or “green” hydrogen and is a prospective means of storing energy from renewable but intermittent 20

power sources such as wind and solar in future clean energy infrastructure (1-4). Commercial systems use either liquid alkaline electrolyte or proton exchange membrane electrolyte (1). Compared to the alkaline counterpart, PEMWE offers the advantages of higher current density, higher H2 purity, lower resistance losses, and more compact design, rendering it a preferred technology where high efficiency and small footprint are essential (1-2). This is particularly the case for the anode catalyst due to the high overpotential for OER (3). At present, OER catalysts for PEMWE are primarily restricted to the platinum group metal (PGM) materials, such as IrOx (1). Their high cost and limited reserve, however, pose 5

Low-cost transition metals and their oxides are known to be active toward OER in alkaline electrolyte (4-6), but their demonstration in acidic electrolyte is very limited (1, 7, 8). Cobalt molecular and oxide compounds emerged as promising OER catalysts for water splitting in recent years (9). Nocera’s group systematically investigated water oxidation using a

Stahl and co-workers performed a comprehensive mechanistic study of Co-catalyzed water oxidation from homogenous to heterogenous phases in electrolyte over a broad pH range of 0-14 (11). Those studies provided profound understanding of electrocatalytic oxygen evolution by cobalt. More recently, thin film spinel-type Co3O4 was found to be active toward OER and stable at low overpotential in acid (12).

The activity and stability of Co oxide were improved significantly when modified with iron, manganese, antimonite, and PbOx (13-15). In addition to activity and stability, the inherent conductivity represents an essential requirement for efficient OER electrocatalysis to overcome the insulating properties of most transition metal oxides in their crystalline form. Conventional carbon supports, used to facilitate the electron conductivity, are not stable against oxidation to CO2

Herein, we present a cobalt spinel-based OER catalyst derived from a zeolitic methylimidazolate framework (Co-ZIF) and processed by electrospinning. The catalyst demonstrated excellent OER activity benefitting from its high specific surface area, porous interconnected nanonetwork structure, and high conductivity. Design of an acid-stable cobalt OER catalyst. Our design concept of an efficient cobalt

15

spinel-based OER catalyst for PEMWE anodes is based on the following rationale: to enhance OER activity in acid, an oversized and more stable 2nd element can be selectively introduced to the cobalt oxide surface to generate strain, oxygen vacancy (Vo) and acid tolerance (17); to improve the oxide electronic conductivity, a 3rd element with similar charge and dimension to cobalt may be incorporated inside the lattice to bridge the Fermi bandgap through d orbital partial occupation

20

of the 3rd element induced by its d-electron delocalization. Advancing further from the RDE/halfcell study to MEA demonstration, the catalyst should have a high porosity and surface area easily accessible to the reactant (H2O). Meanwhile, the electrode layer should be effective in transporting H2O and releasing O2 without blocking the water-catalyst interface. Furthermore, the oxide 4

We selected Co-ZIF as the precursor for the catalyst preparation due to its high intrinsic porosity and reticular structure. It was recently used to prepare a nano-plate oxide with excellent OER activity tested in strong alkaline electrolyte (1M KOH) using the RDE method (18). Our catalyst preparation through low-temperature oxidation partially retained the porosity and morphology of Co-ZIFs after their conversion into interconnected hollow metal oxide particles,

10

providing an excellent platform for enhanced charge and mass transfer. Among different elements we screened, we selected lanthanum (La3+) as the second element due to its much larger radius than Co2+ (1.06 Å vs. 0.72 Å) along with its strong affinity to bind -OH group at the surface of cobalt oxide. We also added manganese ions (Mn2+) of similar radius to Co2+ (0.8 Å vs. 0.72 Å) during the Co-ZIF synthesis, which would be oxidized to Mn3+ (0.72 Å) during the oxidative

Another critical issue in electrocatalysis is the inherent electron conductivity of the oxide itself. Our calculation of LMCF shows that substituting a low concentration of Co3+ ions with uniformly distributed Mn3+ ions in the Co3O4 lattice induces two partially occupied defect states in the mid-bandgap (Fig. 4, C and D). The Mn induced electron wave function overlaps 5

significantly with neighboring Co ions, causing obvious dispersion and hence good electron mobility. This provides a direct enhancement of bulk-based electron conductivity, which combined with the connectivity of the nanofibrous oxide network, offers an overall high conductivity value for LMCF. The improved electronic conductivity was further confirmed experimentally by the 4probe van der Pauw method (fig. S9).

Outlook. This study offers prospective directions and design insight for the future development of PGM-free OER catalysts for hydrogen production using PEMWE technology. For example, the catalytic activity enhancement can be further explored by increasing the surface functional group density through elemental doping, primary size control and morphology innovation. The PEMWE durability can be improved by removing the electrochemically

15

Fundamental understanding of the OER mechanism with respect to mononuclear versus binuclear reaction intermediates and catalytic pathways will help to guide the precursor and catalyst designs for lower overpotential and better acid tolerance (33, 34). These improvements offer paths to the next-generation PGM-free OER catalysts as viable replacements for precious

Materials and Methods

Catalyst synthesis Materials: All the chemicals including Co(NO3)2.6H2O (≥ 99.9 %), 2-methylimidazole (≥ 98 %), Polyacrylonitrile (PAN), 5% Nafion solution (Nafion® 117 solution), La(NO3)3.xH2O (≥ 99 %), Mn(NO3)2.4H2O (≥ 99 %), isopropyl alcohol (IPA, ≥ 99.7 %), methanol (≥ 99.9 %), Ir black (99.9%, powder) and Co3O4 (99.5%, nanopowder, < 50 nm) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and used directly without further purification. DI water was supplied by a Millipore system (Millipore Q). Synthesis of Co(mIm)2 (Co-ZIF) and LaMn@Co-ZIF: Co-ZIF was synthesized according to the reported method (36). Typically, 2-methylimidazole (2.25 g) was dissolved in 80 ml of methanol, followed by addition of methanol solution (50 ml) containing Co(NO3)2·6H2O (1.00 g). The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 20 min to form a homogeneous violet solution, which was then sealed and kept for 12 hours at room temperature. The violet precipitate was collected by centrifugation, washed with methanol thrice and dried under vacuum at 60 °C overnight. For the synthesis of LaMn@Co-ZIF, the process was essentially the same as that of CoZIF, except that the lanthanum (La) and manganese (Mn) were introduced during the ZIF synthesis. Typically, 2-methylimidazole (2.25 g) was dissolved in 80 ml of methanol, followed by addition of a mixture methanol solution (50 ml) containing Co(NO3)2·6H2O (1.00 g), La(NO3)3·6H2O (0.53 g) and Mn(NO3)2·xH2O (0.33 g). Lanthanum doped Co-ZIF without addition of the second metal, La@Co-ZIF, was also synthesized by using the same method. Synthesis of porous cobalt oxide nanofiber (CF) and La/Mn co-doped porous cobalt oxide nanofiber (LMCF): ZIF-derived CF and LMCF were prepared via electrospinning (19), followed

2

Page 30

by post-activation under air. As-synthesized LaMn@Co-ZIF powder was added into DMF at a concentration of 50 mg ml-1, followed by a planetary ball-milling at 600 rpm for 6 h to produce ZIF suspended slurry. A DMF solution containing PAN at a concentration of 100 mg ml-1 was added into the ZIF slurry at the volume ratio of 1:1, followed by ball-milling at 400 rpm for 0.5 hour to make the final precursor slurry, which was subsequently electrospun to polymeric nanofiber at the electrostatic voltage of 9 ~ 10 kV with the distance between the needle and the collection plate at ~15 cm. The LaMn@Co-ZIF/PAN composite nanofiber was transferred into a ceramic boat and placed in a temperature-programmed tube furnace. The sample was heated to 360 °C at a ramp rate of 3 °C min-1 under flowing air and was maintained at that temperature for 6 h. The elemental analysis showed atomic ratio of Co:Mn:La = 80:8:12 in LMCF. The CF and LCF nanofibrous oxides were synthesized by the same method. Electrocatalytic measurement Catalyst ink was prepared to apply over RDE as the OER working electrode for testing. Briefly, 4 mg of catalyst was dispersed in a mixture solution containing 0.2 ml water, 0.44 ml 2propanol and 4 ul 5% Nafion solution, followed by sonicating for 1 h to form the catalyst ink, of which 10 ul was applied to the pre-polished RDE with a gold disk (PINE, 5 mm diameter, 0.196 cm2) resulting in a catalyst loading of ~ 0.26 ± 0.03 mg cm-2 after air dry. For Ir black coated RDE, the catalyst loading was ~ 0.23 ± 0.03 mg cm-2. Electrolysis experiments were performed using a CHI electrochemical station (model 760C) in a standard three electrode cell, which was composed of working electrode, reference electrode (Hg/HgSO4), and counter electrode (gold wire). Cell potential was calibrated to the reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) reference. All potentials in this study referred to RHE calibrated against saturated hydrogen reference electrode (HydroFlex®/gaskatel GmbH). Cyclic voltammetry (CV) 3

Page 31

scans for the initial activity were measured in oxygen saturated 0.1 M HClO 4 or 0.5 M H2SO4 solution after the electrode was activated at a sweep rate of 50 mV s-1 for 20 cycles from 1.0 to 2.0 V until a stable voltammogram curve was obtained. The OER catalytic activity was measured by LSV at room temperature at 1600 rpm and a scan rate of 10 mV s-1. LSV curves were corrected with 95 % iR-compensation. The reported data are based on at least three independent replicates. For catalyst stability study, the catalyst was first subject to an initial break-in in N2 saturated 0.1 M HClO4 with potential cycling from 1.0 V – 2.0 V at room temperature until the electrode is stabilized.
