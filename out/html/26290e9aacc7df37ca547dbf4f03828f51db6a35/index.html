Opportunities and challenges for tandem solar cells using metal halide perovskite semiconductors Tomas Leijtens, Kevin A. Bush, Rohit Prasanna, Michael D. McGehee Materials Science and Engineering, Stanford University, CA, USA

Abstract Metal halide perovskite semiconductors possess excellent optoelectronic properties allowing them to reach high solar cell performances, have tunable bandgaps, and can be rapidly and cheaply deposited from low cost precursors. This makes them ideal candidate materials for making tandem solar cells, either by using perovskites as the wide bandgap top cell along with low band gap silicon or copper indium diselenide or by using both wide and small bandgap perovskite semiconductors to make all-perovskite tandem solar cells. This review highlights the unique potential of perovskite tandem solar cells to reach solar to electricity conversion efficiencies far above those of single-junction solar cells at low costs. We discuss the recent developments in perovskite-based tandem fabrication, and detail directions for future research to take this technology beyond the proof-ofconcept stage. Rapid cost reductions in photovoltaic module manufacturing have made balance-ofsystem costs and installation costs the major contributors to the price of installed solar in both residential and utility settings. These costs scale with the solar panel area required, making the efficiency of photovoltaic panels one of the most important and promising directions for technical developments. The efficiencies of c-Si and thin-film, such as CdTe and CIGS, technologies have started to saturate at ~ 26 and 23%1 respectively, and are limited to the fundamental Shockley-Queisser (SQ) limit of 31-33 % for single junction (SJ) solar cells. Multijunction solar cells, comprising multiple absorber layers with complementary bandgaps, offer a proven approach to reach significantly higher performances. III-V compound semiconductors have been employed, sometimes in conjunction with Ge, to make efficient (39 % under 1 sun) multi-junction solar cells with up to 5 different absorber layers1,2. Unfortunately, these require epitaxial growth and lattice matching across multiple single crystal semiconductor layers, which is generally achieved by slow and cost-prohibitive processes such as metal organic chemical vapor deposition (MOCVD) and molecular beam epitaxy (MBE) that require the use of expensive single crystal substrates. Other than III-V compounds, established semiconductor

Page 2

systems do not offer a wide range of bandgaps capable of high performances 3,4, leaving no cost effective solution for high efficiency tandem solar cells. Solar cells comprising polycrystalline films of metal halide perovskite semiconductors have recently attracted attention for their potential to be processed at low cost from commonly available precursors5–7 via both solution8 and vapor deposition routes9. Crucially for tandem applications, their bandgaps can be tuned by simple substitution of chemical elements on the A, B, and X sites of the ABX3 crystal structure yielding functioning solar cells with a range of bandgaps between 1.18 and 2.3 eV10–12. As a result, perovskite solar cells are attractive candidates for use in tandem solar cells, both in hybrid configurations where they are paired with more established bottom cells such as c-Si and CIGS or in all-perovskite tandem solar cells. This review will explore the potential for perovskite-based tandem cells. We will discuss the unique promise of metal halide perovskites for multijunction solar cell applications, highlight current developments of perovskite tandems, and discuss the work that remains to be done to bridge the gap to commercial readiness. As laboratory scale devices continue to high efficiencies, proving the potential of perovskite tandem solar cells, it will become increasingly important to consider the design of the tandem devices holistically to ensure that they are translatable to commercial manufacturing and operation in real world conditions.

I.

Tandem solar cells and the promise of perovskites Multijunction (tandem) solar cells mitigate losses that come from carrier thermalization

by using several semiconductor layers with different bandgaps. In a double junction tandem, a “top cell” with a large bandgap absorbs high energy photons but permits lower energy photons to pass through to be absorbed in the “bottom cell,” which has a small bandgap. In this way, the higher energy photons generate a high voltage in the top cell while the low energy photons are absorbed in the bottom cell, raising the obtainable efficiency of the combined tandem cell over that of either a single high or low bandgap solar cell. Currently, perovskite-perovskite tandems have obtained 18.5 %13 and 21.0%14 in 2T and 4T configurations with matched bandgaps. The black dotted line in Figures 1a and b represents the ~1.1 eV bandgap of c-Si and the best performing CIGS solar cells. These could be ideally paired with perovskites in the 1.6-1.75 eV range. This type of “hybrid” tandem approach where perovskites provide a boost in efficiency for little additional cost presents an exciting path to delivering higher efficiency panels without significant additional costs or changes to existing c-Si manufacturing lines and supply chains. This may be the most direct pathway to commercializing perovskite based photovoltaics, and also one of the fastest routes to realizing a step improvement in commercially available c-Si module efficiencies. As a result, the perovskite-Si tandem has been the focus of a great deal of research and has recently reached efficiencies of 23.6 %15 and 26.4 %16 in 2T and 4T configurations, respectively. In both cases, the tandem solar cell has >25% greater efficiency (relative) than the SJ silicon solar cell used as the bottom cell. Figure 1c also depicts the SJ SQ limit as a function of bandgap and demonstrates that almost the entire range of available perovskite bandgaps can attain over 50 % (but less than 80 %) of the SQ limit, putting them in a comparable position to materials such as c-Si, CdTe, and GaInP1,17, again making perovskites highly attractive for use in tandem solar cells.

Energy Yield While tandems undoubtedly offer the highest performances under AM1.5 irradiation, real world conditions are often different, and there has been a question over whether varying temperature and irradiation spectra will negatively impact the overall energy yield of tandem solar cells. A recent study 28 combined optical modeling with device modeling, using 5

Page 6

experimentally derived parameters, to determine that both perovskite-Si and perovskiteperovskite 2T tandems present real promise to exceed 30% and approach 34% under AM1.5 conditions. Importantly, both Duck et al and Horantner et al28,29 also demonstrated that the energy yield for 2T and 4T tandems can both be expected to achieve the efficiency enhancement of the devices designed for standard testing conditions in the real world as well, and that neither thermal or optical mismatch presents any considerable additional losses in a series connected tandem. This suggests that there is no clear advantage in voltage vs current matching in 4T architectures. Additionally, the higher practical efficiency, fewer processing steps, and simpler module design lets us argue that monolithically integrated 2T tandems offer greater promise than the mechanically stacked 4T tandem designs. These results would bode very well for perovskite tandems lowering the levelized cost of electricity of PV systems. On the other hand, Futscher et al30 found that tandems (2T and 4T) offer little benefit over SJs in terms of energy yield, but made different assumptions in their modeling. Both studies extracted the device parameters by fitting published current-voltage characteristics of perovskite solar cells, but Futscher chose a device with a high ideality factor and relatively low shunt resistance for the perovskite top cell, whereas Horantner et al chose a device with low ideality factor and high shunt resistance. While there is little difference at 1 sun with AM1.5 spectrum, there is a big difference in behavior at low light intensities, where the top cell chosen by Futsher suffers much more in V oc and FF and hence limits tandem performance under low light conditions. Hence, differences in total energy yield were primarily dictated by the predicted efficiencies at low light intensities rather than in the mismatched photocurrent generation in the different subcells due to variations in irradiation spectrum throughout the course of a year. As a result, the energy yield of perovskite tandems will depend heavily on the quality of the perovskite sub cells in question - high shunt resistances and ideality factors approaching 1 will be critical to enable high performances at low light intensities and resultant high energy yields. Costs Metal halide perovskites are unique from an industrial standpoint because they possess optoelectronic properties reminiscent of high quality single crystal semiconductors, but can be manufactured using low cost raw materials and simple thin film deposition processes. There exist several published technoeconomic analyses31–33 using different assumptions in manufacturing processes and materials selection leading to ranges in expected perovskite SJ module costs of

Page 7

~30 – 120 $ m-2. Differences in manufacturing process such as the use of a thermal evaporator vs a sputter tool for depositing metal electrodes affects not only capital costs but also throughput. Despite these differences and differences in materials selection and assumptions of the facility depreciation rate, all three studies agree that the materials costs generally dominate the module cost (>90% of total costs). The difference in estimated module costs of ~30 31 and ~12032 $ m-2 between the studies lies predominantly in the choice of materials used: using custom designed organic materials such as P3HT and using expensive metal electrodes such as gold will of course yield expensive modules. While the costs of custom designed and expensive organic materials such as P3HT, fullerenes, and spiro-OMeTAD will inevitably reduce as production volumes increase, it is prudent to minimize their use to enable low cost perovskite modules. Replacing impractical materials from the perovskite solar cell stack with lower cost materials such as SnO 2, NiOx, and commonly used electrode materials like ITO, IZO, and Al yields a module cost structure that is only weakly dependent on the perovskite and contact layer costs (Figure 2)31,33. While the device layers used in these studies may not have been identical to those in the best tandem architectures, some broad conclusions can be drawn (Figure 2). The substrate (glass) is the limiting factor in cost of any perovskite solar cell, contributing ~$8/m2, followed by the junction box and TCO deposition (including the materials cost of Indium) at a similar $/m2, and module lamination and packaging. These numbers agree with cost predictions by Woodhouse et al for CIGS modules34,35. The perovskite active layer is generally expected to contribute very little, at ~$1/m2, while the estimated costs for depositing the selective contacts has varied from ~$1/m2 31 to ~$70/m2 32 depending on the choice in materials as discussed above. The total costs discussed above vary only slightly (20 % reduction with an almost 3 fold increase in throughput from 0.5 to 1.4 m2 min-1) with throughput time and deposition method, since they are predominantly dependent on the materials costs. Since 2T tandems do not require additional substrates, packaging, or junction boxes, the additional costs relative to SJ perovskite or silicon solar cells are associated with the perovskite top cell in a tandem and should be dictated by the perovskite active layer, the selective contacts, and the TCOs costs. These are estimated to be on the order of ~$10-20/m2, compared to the already present costs of ~$70-80/m2 for a commercial c-Si module as the bottom cell. This implies that the tandems should provide a ~25% relative boost in efficiency to make sure they do not raise the $/WP of the modules. This could be complicated by the need for protective layers

(such as SnO2) in a tandem stack to enable sputter coating of transparent electrodes (see Section IV); it is imperative that these additional layers do not add considerable cost to the modules. Fortunately, such layers are currently deposited as nanoparticles from solution or as conformal dense layers through atomic layer deposition and CVD type depositions, which have been demonstrated to be cost effective at scale depending on precursors used36. There is added value to a higher efficiency module as a 4% absolute increase in module efficiency can reduce the area related balance of systems costs by ~0.03 and 0.06 $/WP for utility scale and rooftop solar installations, respectively, as recently quantified by Werner et al37. Werner et al also performed a more rough cost analysis of perovskite-Silicon tandems. They assumed a constant cost for each additional step that was based on existing manufacturing processes used in c-Si manufacturing lines (similar in cost to ITO deposition) and found that the BOS related savings outweighed the additional manufacturing costs for rooftop installations for 4 or less additional steps. We must point out that that study assumed each step to cost the same as an ITO deposition, which is expected to be the most expensive step in the perovskite solar cell manufacturing process flow, so that the added costs per layer were most likely an upper bound. Taking these preliminary costing studies together with the realistically obtainable cell level efficiencies near 30% using existing device structures and materials and high annual energy yield 28, the perovskite-Si tandem seems poised to be a success provided that the perovskite top cell does not limit the tandem stability. All-perovskite tandems offer the promise of even lower manufacturing costs associated with roll to roll thin film manufacturing, as well as the allure of efficient but lightweight and flexible photovoltaic modules, but require substantially more materials development particularly in terms of flexible encapsulation. Assuming a similar cost structure to those published for SJ perovskite solar cells, but incorporating additional contact layers, a small bandgap perovskite, and an extra TCO (for the recombination layer) costs below ~$40/m2 should be achievable for a device architecture capable of nearing 30% power conversion efficiency at the cell level and >25% at module level, translating ultimately to <$0.16/WP. If TCO-free (or more specifically, indiumfree) recombination layers are developed, this cost would drop further. Unfortunately, cost effective flexible encapsulation is still under development, raising costs of flexible all-perovskite tandems over those of rigid, glass packaged modules.

Figure 1 demonstrates that the exact bandgaps of the bottom and top cells must be carefully selected to achieve the highest efficiency monolithically integrated tandems, as the current in the tandem will be limited by the lowest performer. Yu, Leilaeioun, and Holman argue that top and bottom cell partners should be chosen based on each cell’s spectral efficiency 38. This valuable metric highlights that a top cell will only improve the efficiency of a bottom cell if it has a higher efficiency resolved at a given wavelength, which should be a natural result of a higher open circuit voltage. Spectral efficiency is a convolution of the material’s fill factor (FF), open circuit voltage, and external quantum efficiency (EQE) at a given wavelength. With long carrier diffusion lengths and strong light absorption, perovskites readily attain high EQEs, 10

making it critical to minimize voltage loss and increase FF to maximize efficiency at a given wavelength. Figure 3 shows the spectral efficiencies of the most promising perovskite top cell candidates and the best perovskite and c-Si bottom cells. Perovskite solar cells have already achieved losses in potential comparable to the best semiconductors such as GaAs and silicon:0.33 V for a X eV bandgap39, 0.36V for a 1.57 eV bandgap40, and 0.39 V with a 1.63 eV bandgap 41 rival the record voltage loss of 0.37 V for a silicon solar cell with a 1.12 eV bandgap42. Ideally, these low voltage losses would be universal to halide perovskites of any composition and bandgap. However, voltage has thus far not tracked linearly with increasing bandgap in bromide rich perovskite compounds (Figure 1d). Particularly striking is that 1.75eV cells developed with FA and Cs on the A site by Wang et al.43 still do not surpass the spectral efficiency achieved by the 1.63 eV cells developed by Saliba et al.41 or 1.55eV cells by Yang et al44. There may be several reasons for the fact that the VOC and spectral efficiency do not follow bandgap in perovskite-based solar cells, but the most well studied is that upon illumination, most mixed halide perovskites suffer from halide segregation into I-rich and Brrich regions (a process termed the Hoke effect)45. This means that they may reach initial high efficiencies but degrade rapidly under illumination. In addition, carrier lifetimes for bromide containing high bandgap materials have remained lower than those of the more heavily studied iodide materials even when the Hoke effect does not occur. It is unclear whether the fast nonradiative recombination is due to unique bromide related defects, wider bandgaps exposing trap levels previously hidden within the bands for smaller bandgap materials, or simply the result of the bromide containing wide bandgap materials and associated selective contacts being less studied and optimized. Significant progress has been made in understanding the nature of light-induced phase segregation (Hoke effect)45. We refer the reader to two thorough reviews on the subject, which highlight current understanding (Slotcavage et al)46 and demonstrate (Unger et al47) that while compositional tuning of the A and X site in 3D perovskite structures offers some promise, no definite solution has been found for obtaining photo-stable 3D perovskite compositions approaching the 1.8 eV bandgaps that are most desirable for all-perovskite tandems. Strategies include stabilizing Br-free CsPbI3 (~1.7 eV) in its black phase by making nanocrystals48, slowing

migration of the halide ions by incorporating sheets of large organic cations to make quasi 2D/3D structures43,49, and by compositional engineering19. Any top cell with a bandgap lower than ~1.7-1.75 eV can result in a tandem with a matched current density when paired with Si (1.1 eV) or perovskite (1.25 eV) bottom cells. At present, the compounds with ~1.63 eV deliver some of the highest voltages (1.24 V) and spectral efficiencies better than those obtained for the less stable, higher bandgap materials, in addition to showing the best stability due to a low (17-18%) Br content15,41. This makes them promising top cell candidates. Figure 1a shows that for a 1.1eV bottom cell, a top cell bandgap of 1.63eV can still achieve a limiting efficiency of 43%, vs 44% with a 1.7eV bandgap. For all-perovskite tandems, with a bottom cell bandgap ~ 1.25 eV, a 1.63eV bandgap top cell can still reach a theoretical maximum efficiency of ~39%. Horantner et al28 show, using a realistic, coupled optical and device model to determine ideal bandgap combinations for all-perovskite tandems, that reducing the top cell bandgap for the sake of improved stability does not necessarily introduce large losses in the practically obtainable efficiency. Indeed, the present generation of 1.63 eV top cells in a perovskite-silicon tandem can still yield a practical tandem performance over 31%50 and 29.5 % in an all-perovskite tandem. III.

Narrow bandgap perovskites for all-perovskite tandems All-perovskite tandems offer a route to high efficiency while maintaining the benefits of

low-cost and low-temperature fabrication as well as the possibility of lightweight, flexible form factors for both sub-cells in the stack. So far, all low band gap perovskites12,14,51–55 useful for tandem applications use tin and lead on the B-site. This introduces advantages and challenges unique to all-perovskite tandem solar cells. The best performing single junction cell with a narrow gap perovskite reported so far uses FA0.6MA0.4Sn0.6Pb0.4I3, achieving 17.8% PCE with a band gap of 1.25 eV14,55. This perovskite has been used to fabricate a (21%) mechanically stacked all-perovskite tandem14. While perovskites with still smaller band gaps have been reported, such as MASn0.8Pb0.2I3 with a band gap of 1.19 eV56, these have not yet been demonstrated to yield solar cells with >10 % PCE. Using perovskites with smaller band gaps is desirable to get closer to the ideal bottom cell band gap for a 2T tandem (Figure 1a) and because it allows the use of smaller band gap perovskites for a current-matched top cell, which are less susceptible to photo-instability due to light induced trap formation, as described above. Tin-lead perovskites have lower absorption cross-sections than pure lead perovskites (Figure 4a). Monolithic perovskite tandems and single junction low bandgap perovskite solar cells based on Sn-Pb perovskites so far have been limited by low short circuit currents due to low external quantum efficiency in the near infrared (Figure 4b, c). To obtain an EQE of higher than 80% across the spectrum, which will be essential for optimal current matching in a tandem, it has been necessary to use tin-lead perovskite layers that are over 1-um thick for the bottom cell14. This in turn requires carrier diffusion lengths to be at least 3 um to allow efficient extraction of photogenerated charge. Early reports of tin perovskites showed extremely short carrier lifetimes of well below a nanosecond10,57, but lifetimes of hundreds of nanoseconds have been achieved by compositional tuning on the A-site using methylammonium (MA) and formamidinium (FA) and optimizing processing conditions with antisolvent techniques to produce large grains14,54. Still, EQEs of all-perovskite tandems have been limited to below 80% (Figure 4b,c). On the other hand, many Sn-Pb perovskites display high open circuit voltages relative to their band gap56. The loss-in-potential (Eg – qVoc) reported for these perovskites is as low as 0.33 eV39 (Figure 1d), which is the smallest reported for any perovskite solar cell41 and is even better than the loss-in-potential of the best crystalline silicon solar cells 58. Horantner et al recently demonstrated through optical and device modeling that all-perovskite tandems based on the best reported small and wide bandgap perovskite solar cells can exceed 30% in performance with EQEs over 90% and high VOCs when thick (700-1000 nm) absorber layers are used50.

IV.

Tandem design

Fabrication challenges in perovskite tandem solar cells The device architectures and layers used within them that are described in the sections below are depicted in Figure 5, where Fig 5a-f are perovskite-silicon tandems and 5g-i are all-perovskite tandems. 1. TCO Unlike in a single junction perovskite solar cell, the top TCO in many tandem architectures (both 2T and 4T) must be deposited on top of the perovskite cell to allow light to enter into the device. To prevent sputter damage to the soft layers comprising perovskite solar cells, research groups have had to develop protective “sputter buffer” layers 10,15,23,24. These are typically thin (530 nm) so as not to negatively impact electronic transport properties, and this has spurred a shift from solution processed layers59 to conformally deposited vapor phase layers to enable deposition on even rough perovskite surfaces10,15,23,24. These deposition processes must be at a low enough temperature (<100 °C) and use mild enough precursors (perovskites are sensitive to oxidation and reactions with bases) that they do not damage the underlying layers. Additionally, they should be transparent and have appropriate energy levels. Ballif et al23 used evaporated MoOx as a buffer on top of commonly used HTMs such as Spiro-OMeTAD, and Duong et al16 used this approach in developing a 26.4 % 4T perovskite-Si tandem. Other suitable protective 14

Page 15

materials could be NiOx and VOx. Tandem architectures with p-i-n polarity (such as those made on commercial p-type c-Si wafers) require protective electron conducting layers. Our group first used spin coated AZO nanoparticles59 with success and then developed an atomic layer deposited SnO2 layer to yield 23.6 % 2T tandems with promising stability15. Alternative materials could be ZnO (though this is prone to reacting with perovskite semiconductors), and TiO 2 (which is UV sensitive). The recent record all-perovskite tandems developed by Jen et al54 do not use any special sputter buffer layer; position of the sample in relation to the plasma, gas flow, accelerating voltage, and temperature in the sputter tool may all be important parameters that enabled this result. ALD SnO2 and sputtered TCOs also improve the stability of perovskite solar cells by preventing the release of organic cations and the diffusion and subsequent reaction of metal and iodide15,59–61. Given the volatile nature of the organic cations used on the A site in perovskite semiconductors, the low activation energy for halide vacancy formation and migration, and the propensity for metals to react with perovskite layers, it is essential to continue developing sputter buffer layers and TCOs with improved barrier properties62–64.

2. Perovskite Layer Tandem processing requires perovskites that are thermally and chemically robust. ALD and CVD processes often involve temperatures around 100 °C for the precursors to react. The precursors themselves also tend to contain ligands and counter-reagents, like water, ozone, or hydrogen peroxide, that can degrade the perovskite65. In addition, common manufacturing processes used in silicon PV lines such as gridline printing and module lamination between glass sheets with adhesive polymers, such as EVA, often require annealing temperatures of up to 150 °C. For these reasons, the volatile MA cation62,66 may be somewhat less suitable than FA and Cs on the A site, though further work needs to be done to understand the stability of mixed A-site cation materials41,67. If solution processing is desired in 2T perovskite-perovskite tandems, then the morphology of the first deposited cell can strongly influence the coating and crystallization

of the second layer. As a result, smooth, homogenous perovskite films are critical to enable solution processed perovskite-perovskite tandems. 3. Recombination Layer A 2T tandem requires a recombination layer in order to allow current to flow across the two subcells by allowing opposite carriers from the subcells to recombine in a highly doped layer. In traditional III-V multijunction solar cells, tunnel junctions must be lattice matched to the subcells68,69, limiting the available options. This restriction does not apply to perovskite tandems, since polycrystalline films of metal halide perovskites with good optoelectronic properties can be successfully grown without lattice matching on a wide variety substrates. Traditional recombination layers used in III-V tandems use a tunnel junction made of two semiconductors with high n- and p-type doping; these require extremely steep band bending across the junction so that the depletion regions are narrow enough to enable tunneling. A similar approach resulted in the first 2T perovskite-Si tandem, where a narrow n++ / p++ junction on top of an n-type Si cell was used to allow tunneling of electrons from the p++ to the n++ layer on top of which the perovskite solar cell was built starting with the electron transporting TiO 2 layer25. A similar approach has yet to be demonstrated for the more commercially relevant p-type c-Si solar cells. Solution processed tandems made from organic molecules employ thin layers of highly doped organic (such as PEDOT:PSS) or inorganic layers (such as ZnO) as recombination layers, and this approach was adapted for perovskite-perovskite tandems with some success27,70. Bolink et al26 also demonstrated the use of thermally evaporated layers of doped n and p type organic molecules as an effective recombination layer in an evaporated 2T tandem. We recently demonstrated the use of a sputtered ITO layer (deposited as described above) as a conductive recombination layer10,13. We and others also employ a thin ITO recombination layer in 2T perovskite-Si architectures10,15,21. The disadvantage of the highly conductive recombination contact is that carriers will be more prone to being funneled to any existent shunt pathways along the recombination layer22, a problem that could limit scaling to larger areas. Sahli et al22 developed a nanocrystalline hydrogenated Si (nc-Si:H) recombination layer using n+ and p+ doped layers deposited via low temperature plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition compatible with SHJ solar cells and used it to make 22 % efficient small area (0.25 cm 2) and 18% efficient large (12.96 cm2).

4. Compatible Processing Since the first cell must remain intact during processing of the second cell, it dictates the processing window for the second subcell to be deposited. In the case of perovskite-Si tandems, the Si is always the substrate for the perovskite top cell. Most Si cells can withstand any temperature (up to 500 °C) and solvent that a perovskite deposition would require. The doped aSi layer used to passivate the most efficient Si solar cells (SHJ), however, requires that the cell temperature remain below 200 °C, and makes it impossible to use the record (22.1%)71 perovskite solar cell architecture comprising mesoporous TiO2 layers (which require 500 °C sintering). As a result, the record 2T tandems on HIT cells have been made by using only low temperature processes for the perovskite cell deposition15,23,24. Wu et al21 recently developed an effective n-type homo junction Si solar cell using SiN passivation layers and an ITO recombination layer, allowing the most efficient top cells based on mesoporous TiO 2 to be used in efficient tandem solar cells. Processing a perovskite solar cell on top of another perovskite solar cell is more difficult because the layers are more sensitive to temperature and solvents. To enable solution-processed perovskite layers, it is essential to use a protective layer between the two cells and use only low temperature processes. The protective layer should be conductive and serve as the recombination contact. We and others have used a sputtered ITO layer, which enabled the proof of principle demonstration of a 17% 2T perovskite tandem and more recently an 18.5% 2T perovskite tandem10,13. A completely solution processed 2T tandem27 (including solution processed recombination layer) would likely have to make use of cross linked and doped organic layers, or uniform and impermeable metal oxide layers deposited by sol-gel processes. To date, such approaches have proven difficult. Making a fully dry, vapor deposited tandem would provide a simpler path to a structure in which none of the layers damage one another. Bolink et al9,26 have recently proven thermal evaporation to be an effective route to depositing perovskites and recombination layers, but were not able to evaporate perovskite layers with the ideal bandgap combination, limiting their tandem performance. The ability to co-evaporate the most promising bottom and top cell perovskites highlighted in Sections II and III is complicated by the recent trend to use increasingly complex compositions using multiple A, B, and X site species 10,71,72. This makes developing vapor deposition routes for perovskites with ideal bandgaps a challenging though exciting and scalable route to enable high yield and efficient tandems.

5. Maximizing Light Harvesting Effective light management is a key challenge in making efficient tandem solar cells. It is important to mitigate parasitic absorption by avoiding thick, absorbing contact layers such as doped Spiro-OMeTAD (hole transporter) and TCOs with much free-carrier absorption in the NIR23,73–75. While ITO is commonly used in perovskite tandems, higher mobility materials, such as IZO and H-InOx74,76,77, require lower doping concentrations and display lower parasitic absorption in a tandem. There is also significant parasitic absorption at interfaces with the reflective back contacts that can be recovered by ensuring they consist of materials with large differences in refractive index. For instance, it is possible to obtain an extra 1.5 mA cm-2 from the Si bottom cell by placing doped silicon nanoparticles between the a-Si and silver reflector layers15. Effective light management helped enable the development of 23.6% 2T and 26.4% 4T perovskite-Si tandems15,16. Since perovskite solar cells comprise thin films, optical interference effects are very commonly observed in EQEs of perovskite tandem solar cells. This is especially true for the perovskite-perovskite tandems; several mA cm-2 are lost just due to optical interference effects10,13. Optical modeling78,79 should enable rational design to minimize reflection peaks in the spectral response and even direct the IR part of the spectrum into the bottom cell and the visible into the top cell by selectively reflecting the visible light at the interlayer between the top and bottom cells. Complex refractive indexes for most commonly used perovskite materials are available28,50,78–80 and should guide such work. Materials with very low refractive indexes should be avoided in the middle of the tandem stack since these will result in large reflections at interfaces with the high refractive index perovskite and Si subcells. The ITO recombination layer used in perovskite-Si tandems also induces reflectance losses to the Si subcell; the nc-Si:H recombination layer developed by Sahli22 has significantly lower parasitic reflectance than an ITO recombination layer. To fully remove reflection losses by improving light incoupling and enhance the NIR response at the band edge, an ideal tandem solar cell would also benefit from surface texturing. This is commonly realized in the form of several um sized pyramids etched into c-Si wafers. Such texture improves light incoupling by minimizing reflections off the front surface while also scattering the light within the solar cell to enhance light trapping (enhancing path length and preventing escape through the front surface) and reduce optical interference to improve overall

Page 21

light harvesting. This can be easily done to the back surface of a c-Si bottom cell, but there exist no published examples of perovskite-Si tandems using c-Si bottom cells with both sides textured, despite the fact that commercial c-Si wafers are textured on top and bottom for maximum light trapping. Solution processing of perovskites does not enable conformal deposition over sharp features with several um size. This limits the obtainable photocurrent in perovskite-silicon tandems by 1-2 mA cm-2 81 The most logical solution is to deposit the perovskite solar cell layers via a vapor deposition process that will conform to the sharp features of the texture. Alternatively, it is also possible to position an electrically inert, but optically textured layer with appropriate refractive index directly on top of the top contact of the tandem solar cell to improve light harvesting82. Vapor processing would also enable perovskite-perovskite tandems to be deposited on pre-textured substrates. Such texturing may be the key to allowing perovskite-perovskite tandems to reach their full potential, both because of the low band edge absorption in tin-lead perovskite absorber layers and because of the interference effects described above that will limit their light harvesting ability.

Scaling of perovskite tandem solar cells Most of the tandem solar cells reported in the literature have been made at only 1 cm 2. This is in line with most of the perovskite solar cell research in general, and allows for the translation of spin coating recipes optimized for single junctions to the tandem solar cells. Work at IMEC has demonstrated 4-cm2 4-terminal tandem solar modules approaching 24%83, but still used spin coating for the perovskite subcell layers. There has been some progress on scaling single junction perovskite modules by using various solution coating techniques, with a record of close to 16% measured through a 10 cm2 aperture made through scalable blade coating84. With improved scribing, >17% printed modules could be readily realized. This has been enabled by careful solvent engineering to widen the processing window, the approach lends itself to low cost manufacturing of the perovskite subcell. All-perovskite tandem solar cells could be printed in this way. However, the recombination layer would have to be impermeable and the module would be sensitive to any pinholes within the recombination layer: any hole will lead to dissolution of the underlying cell area, potentially causing a short. For this reason, obtaining high yields for solution processed tandems may be a challenge. In addition, for reasons discussed

Page 22

above, commercial c-Si wafers are textured, which would prevent the use of blade coated top cells unless they were planarized by a an additional step. Currently, lab scale tandem devices have planarized top surfaces for ease of perovskite deposition, but it is unclear whether this can easily be translated to a manufacturing context, and the planarization will result in less light trapping and lower photocurrents than could be achieved with texturing. One of the most stable and scalable perovskite architectures uses porous printed carbon electrodes and a dip coated perovskite layer. While this approach yields very stable devices 85,86, it is not suitable for either perovskite-Si or all-perovskite tandems due to the high deposition temperatures of the porous TiO2 and ZrO2 layers as well as the simple fact that the electrode is black (it needs to be transparent for perovskite-Si tandems and highly reflective for allperovskite tandems). Another approach for making uniform large area metal halide perovskites is by vapor deposition87. While work has been done to make efficient solar cells using MA based perovskites, it has proven difficult to make efficient materials with mixed A and B site compositions via direct co-evaporation. Sahli et al22 sidestep this issue by first thermally evaporating the inorganic precursors (PbI2 and CsI ) and then converting them to perovskite by exposure to FAI/FABr precursors in methanol to achieve the desired composition. They used this technique to demonstrate the first large area (12.96 cm2) monolithic perovskite-silicon tandem solar cells. Efficient (15 %) 12 cm2 single junction modules were recently reported88 by vapor conversion of PbI2 to FAPbI3 and subsequent A site cation exchange (Cs replacing FA). Combining such a vapor conversion process with a thermally evaporated PbI 2/PbBr2 layer could allow for a fully conformal and dry deposition technique. Such an approach would also be beneficial for all-perovskite tandems as none of the steps would damage underlying layers. Unfortunately, thermal evaporation at ultrahigh vacuum could be cost prohibitive, though a full cost analysis remains to be done. We suggest that research into rapid throughput vapor deposition of perovskite precursors should be a high priority for those attempting to scale tandem solar cells. Sahli et al22 also found that to make efficient large area tandems, it is critical to use a recombination layer (in their case nanocrystalline hydrogenated Si (nc-Si:H) n+ and p+ doped layers) with high sheet resistance to prevent leakage current funneling through small shunt

Page 23

pathways. For the same reason, less conductive recombination layers should be chosen for scaled all perovskite tandems.

V. Reliability of Tandem solar cells Perovskite – Silicon Tandems For perovskites to be commercially viable and offer low LCOE in tandems on silicon for rooftop and utility scale installations, they must match silicon’s 25-year lifetime. If the perovskite sub cell declines in performance due to a reduction in photocurrent, the full tandem performance will drop proportionally. It is thus critical to examine whether tandem solar cells will require additional encapsulation over that used in conventional c-Si modules; if so, then this will have to be included in updated cost models to evaluate whether the added power output still validates the additional costs of making the tandem module. While the X-site poses concerns of photo-instability45 as discussed above, the A-site cation is often the focus of concerns regarding thermal, moisture, and oxygen instability. The methylammonium cation is volatile but improved encapsulation has been shown to prevent its escape, enabling operation at elevated temperatures for hundreds of hours59,61. Formamidinium substitution shows improved thermal stability over methylammonium11 and has also led to the highest efficiency devices41,44. Further thermal stability improvement has been observed through either partial or full Cs substitution19,89–93. We have previously demonstrated that when such a stable perovskite composition is chosen, the organic A site cation encapsulated by a conformal and dense TCO layer, and the full cell protected using industry standard processes using glass/glass packaged held together with butyl rubber edge seals and polymer encapsulants, perovskite cells can pass the IEC “damp heat” test (1000 hrs at 85 %RH at 85 C)15 and temperature cycling (-40 C– 85 C, 200 cycles)94. However, most commercial PV modules are subjected to 3-5 times these tests, so passing them once may not guarantee a 25 year lifetime for the full tandem. In addition, many c-Si modules currently use a Tedlar backsheet rather than glass on both the front and back; it remains to be seen whether the full tandem modules will require the use of two glass sheets, which could raise costs of the tandem module.

Page 24

The ions in the perovskite top cell have been shown to be extremely mobile 95–97; while silicon surfaces are known to be very sensitive to contamination by impurities. It remains to be seen whether I- can diffuse into the silicon subcell through the TCO recombination layer. This should be subject for future studies. All-perovskite tandems A unique challenge is that all-perovskite tandems require some Sn2+ on the B-site of the small bandgap bottom cell, but that Sn2+, unlike Pb2+, is susceptible to oxidation from the 2+ to the 4+ state upon exposure to oxygen or moisture. Formation of Sn4+ and accompanying defects such as Sn2+ vacancies has been an often cited reason for poor performance of pure tin perovskites and resulted in extremely fast (~minutes) degradation of device performance in solar cells based on pure tin perovsites57,98,99. The mixed tin-lead perovskites relevant to bottom cells in tandems, however, show significantly improved stability compared to pure tin perovskites, where substitution of 50% or more of the B-site with an element other than tin (typically Pb) that is not susceptible to oxidation appears to be a promising strategy to stabilize tin-containing perovskites. Pb enhances the lifetime by a factor of 10-100 because it changes the oxidation mechanism of Sn to one less energetically favorable100. There have been no published studies of full device stability under heat or light in either ambient or inert conditions, so it is difficult to know whether the standard encapsulation used for Sn-free perovskites used in top cells and in perovskite-silicon tandems will suffice for the all perovskite tandem, or whether more sophisticated packaging will be required. Certainly, if flexible form factors are desirable for allperovskite tandems, flexible packaging will be a critical subject of research; currently available flexible packaging such as the 3M ultrabarrier may be cost prohibitive. All-perovskite tandems may also suffer from a unique instability: exchange of ions between the two perovskite layers. The A and X site cations especially have been shown to be mobile, so if the recombination layer does not provide a good ion diffusion barrier, it is possible that the Br ions will diffuse from the top cell to the bottom cell and that the composition of the sub cells will homogenize over time, defeating the purpose of the tandem solar cell. This adds an extra constraint on the choice in recombination layer. The TCO recombination layers currently used is very likely to provide protection, but it must be investigated whether this is enough for many years of operational stability or whether perfectly crack-free, dense, amorphous recombination layers must be developed.

An additional stability concern for tandem solar cells arises from the fact that they are composed of many layers, each of which could delaminate from the other. Rolston et al have demonstrated that the adhesion between metal halide perovskites and the contact layers is extremely weak and that the contact layers themselves (fullerenes in particular) suffer from weak internal adhesion101. This is especially worrying since the thermal expansion coefficients of the layers differs considerably94. Careful encapsulation with rigid glass/glass packages appears to mitigate this problem for single junction devices and allows them to pass temperature cycling tests94, but tandems contain even more layers. Tests will need to be performed on full tandem solar cells. Flexible all-perovskite tandem solar cells especially may be prone to delamination upon bending and may require use of layers with stronger adhesion, perhaps through crosslinking strategies102. VI.

Outlook

Many of the pieces to enable efficient perovskite tandem solar cells at laboratory scale are in place. The present record open-circuit voltage of 1.24V (for a perovskite with 1.63 eV bandgap), coupled with existing contacts and recombination layers should theoretically enable tandems in current architectures to attain PCEs of approximately 31% and 29.5% for perovskite-Si and perovskite-perovskite tandems, respectively. As proof of concept laboratory scale device records exceed those for perovskite single junction solar cells, it will be important to consider the strategies used to reach those records holistically: can these same device structures and deposition processes be implemented on commercially relevant, stable tandems with high yield and low cost? This is an area of research where a simple translation of processes developed for single junction modules may not deliver the desired results in tandem devices; coating on top of textured c-Si wafers remains an unsolved problem, while all-perovskite tandems may suffer from low yield when solution coating is used for the perovskite layers. Perhaps, tandem specific new deposition routes should be explored; ideal routes would be high throughput, dry and conformally deposited, performed at ambient pressure or only mild vacuum, and easily adaptable to a wide range of perovskite compositions. The ease with which high quality metal halide perovskite compounds are formed suggests that it should be possible to use alternative, high throughput routes such as vapor transport deposition or closed space sublimation used by the CdTe industry103. The organic A site cations could be deposited through

Page 26

a spraying process, a blade coating process, or a vapor conversion process in which the PbI 2/CsI film is exposed to FAI/FABr vapor. This type of deposition has not yet been attempted but may be an important area of research for those aiming to scale tandem solar cells. In addition, tandem architectures currently require the use of thin buffer layers to prevent sputter damage during deposition of the TCOs, complicating device designs; the most efficient perovskite-silicon tandems currently employ 7 steps, and more may be necessary if diffusion barriers prove to be essential to prevent metal ingress over long time scales. Again, the device designs need to be considered holistically to minimize the complexity, use scalable and cost effective deposition and materials, and ensure lifetimes compatible with 25 year operation. Tandem module design has also gone largely unexplored by the academic community, despite the fact that tandem modules offer unique challenges. 2T architectures may be the simplest to implement from a module perspective, as they require fewer exterior electronics and will only require one cell size and form factor for the entire module. A 2T perovskite-Si module will look mostly exactly like that of a normal c-Si module, though attention may have to be given to how the cell interconnects are made: perovskite top cells may not be compatible with the soldering connections traditionally used in c-Si modules. A 2T perovskite-perovskite module could look identical to a single junction perovskite module, consisting of monolithically interconnected cells in the form of long strips, but the exact design of the interconnects may have to be different. A highly conductive recombination layer may not allow the use of the traditional scribing patterns since the interconnect could short the entire top cell through the recombination layer. The 4T module designs are more complicated and allow for a greater range in designs. To enable either current or voltage matching between top and bottom modules, and reduce the reliance on exterior electronics, the size and number of series connected cells can be varied. Minimizing “dead” area from shading by the top cell gridlines will also be an interesting engineering challenge. The materials and device designs to make efficient laboratory scale tandem solar cells are largely in place. Future work must focus on translating these proof of concept designs to scalable deposition processes and ensuring that industry standard packaging and encapsulation will suffice for tandem solar cells. Encapsulation of all-perovskite tandem solar cells especially requires significant research. Research on these topics should see large leaps over the next few years and will determine the eventual success of this exciting new technology.
