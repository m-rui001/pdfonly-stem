Photoelectrochemical water splitting represents a promising solar-to-fuel energy conversion technology, in which photogenerated holes (h+) oxidize water to O2 at a semiconductor photoanode and electrons (e−) are extracted through an external circuit to reduce protons to H2 at a cathode1,2. At the photoanode, oxygen evolution catalysts (OECs) are generally needed to increase photocurrent and shift onset potential negatively for water oxidation3, but the OEC deposition must be controlled because excess material can hinder light absorption and decrease performance of photoanodes4. Yet it is unclear on what semiconductor surface sites an OEC should be deposited for optimal improvement, and to what extent the surface active sites for water oxidation also contribute to charge-carrier recombination5. These problems are exacerbated by surface heterogeneity6, especially for nanostructured photoanodes whose small dimensions allow for minimal charge-carrier transport distance1,7,8. Here we use first-of-their-kind charge-carrier-selective superresolution photoelectrocatalysis imaging and sub-particle photocurrent measurements to define the correlation between surface hole and electron activity and absorbed-photon-towater-oxidation-current efficiency on single TiO2 nanorods. Surprisingly, we find that the most active sites for water oxidation are also the most significant sites for recombination. Guided by hole and electron activity maps, site-selective deposition of an OEC on individual nanorods reveals that on average photocurrent efficiency increases and onset potential shifts negatively, but at the sub-nanorod level the magnitudes of these catalystderived changes are anticorrelated. Moreover, the optimal catalyst deposition sites on these nanorods for photocurrent enhancement are the lower activity sites and for onset potential reduction are the more positive onset potential sites, contrary to what is obtainable under typical deposition conditions. We propose an activity-based strategy for rationally engineering photoelectrodes with minimal amounts of catalysts. We studied single-crystalline rutile TiO2 nanorods with defined surface facet orientations9 (widths = 127 ± 27 nm, lengths = 1735 ± 437) for their capability and stability as photoanodes for water oxidation2. We dispersed them on an indium-doped tin oxide (ITO) electrode in a microfluidic photoelectrochemical cell (Fig. 1a) and used single-molecule fluorescence microscopy10-13 of either h+-induced amplex red (AR) oxidation or e−-induced resazurin (Rz) reduction (Extended Data Fig. 1) to map the surface reactions of photogenerated holes and electrons on individual nanorods. Both reactions produce the fluorescent product resorufin (P), as evidenced by ensemble photo(electro)catalysis measurements (Extended Data Figs. 2-3). In a typical experiment, the ITO electrode potential (E, referenced to Ag/AgCl electrode) was fixed while a N2-purged pH 8.3 electrolyte solution containing nanomolar nonfluorescent probe molecules was flowed through. Continuous 375 nm laser illumination generated charge-carriers within TiO2 nanorods, inducing a potential-dependent steady-state anodic photocurrent (iss, Fig. 1n, blue circles) from water oxidation2; the low probe concentration contributes insignificantly to iss. Another 532 nm laser induced the fluorescence of product molecules formed on nanorod surfaces. Anodic photocurrent and significant probe reactions were only observed with 375 nm laser illumination (Extended Data Figs. 4a, 3c, 3e). By imaging and localizing individual P molecules at ~30 nm precision (Methods), we mapped the positions of h+- or e−-induced reactions across a range of potentials on each nanorod relative to its scanning electron microscope (SEM) image (Fig. 1b-g), representing the first single-molecule imaging study of photoelectrocatalysis. At potentials where iss is significant (E ≥ −0.3 V, Fig. 1n), the h+-induced surface reaction rates are high, but strikingly non-uniform along single nanorods (Fig. 1b, d, f), even though the 2

same {100} facets span the length of each nanorod9. For 25 out of 37 nanorods, their h+-induced reactions are predominantly localized to one or more nanoscale hot-spots (Fig. 1b, d), while their remaining surfaces show less reactions. The smallest hot-spots are ~50 nm (Extended Data Fig. 5b-c), much smaller than the diffraction-limited resolution of ~300 nm (i.e., super-optical resolution, as in related catalysis imaging studies10). For the other 12 nanorods, their h+-induced reactions are more delocalized along the nanorods (Fig. 1f). At potentials where the anodic iss is minimal (E ≤ −0.4 V, Fig. 1n), e−-induced reaction rates are significant but are also non-uniform along the nanorods (Fig. 1c, e, g). (Intra-facet heterogeneous activity was also observed for other TiO2 crystals in photocatalysis11,13). These non-uniform reaction rates (i.e., activity) indicate that the distribution of active sites differs among nanorods and the higher activity sites are not {100} facet sites, but likely surface structural defects discernable by electron microscopy or impurity atoms detectable in elemental analysis (Extended Data Fig. 6). The reconstructed rutile {100} surface contains oxygen vacancies and small Ti2O3 units, which could act as catalytic units for water oxidation14. Among the impurities, Fe has been identified as a dopant that increases both photocatalytic reduction and oxidation activity of TiO2 nanoparticles15. Surprisingly, a strong spatial correlation exists between the h+- and e−-induced activities on any nanorod (Fig. 1k): wherever the h+-induced activity is high (or low) at more positive potentials, the e−-induced activity is also high (or low) at more negative potentials (e.g., Fig. 1b vs. c). The average distance between corresponding h+- and e−-reaction hot-spots on the same nanorod is ~40 nm (Extended Data Fig. 5d), comparable to our localization precision. This spatial correlation is likely not due to preferential probe adsorption to the same surface sites, because the structurally similar product (Extended Data Fig. 1a) adsorbs to all surface sites equivalently (Extended Data Fig. 7). While previous studies on semiconductor microcrystals have shown that photogenerated holes and electrons can preferentially react at the same facet in photocatalysis16, here we show that they prefer to reach and react at the same surface sites within the same side-facets of single nanorods at nanometer resolution. At any location on a nanorod, either at a hot-spot (e.g., S1, Fig. 1b) or at a low activity spot (e.g., S3, Fig. 1f), the h+-induced AR oxidation rate (vAR) becomes significant at about −0.5 V (Fig. 1l), commensurate with the steady-state photocurrent, which has an onset potential (Eon,ss) of −0.47 V (Fig. 1n). For E ≥ −0.3 V, vAR scales with (E)¼ (Extended Data Fig. 3b), and with (I0)½ at a fixed positive potential (I0 = 375 nm laser power; Extended Data Fig. 3c), consistent with mechanisms that AR reacts with surface adsorbed OH radicals (OHs) produced from h+-induced oxidation of OH− or H2O17. In contrast, the e−-induced Rz reduction rate (vRz) shows an opposite trend: vRz is high at E < Eon,ss (Fig. 1m) and decreases significantly at E > Eon,ss, where a ~0.02 nA (~108 holes/s) anodic photocurrent is measured per nanorod (Fig. 1n). The measurable vRz at E > Eon,ss indicates that even under highly anodic conditions, singlemolecule fluorescence microscopy is able to detect the photogenerated electrons that participate in surface reactions (i.e., surface recombination) rather than being collected by the ITO electrode. To assess local water oxidation efficiencies, we focused a 375 nm laser (spot-diameter ~390 nm) onto a nanorod spot and measured potential-dependent photocurrent through the ITO electrode (Fig. 1a). i-E responses differ for spots on the same nanorod (e.g., S1 vs. S2, Fig. 2a), supporting that we measure sub-nanorod photoelectrochemical behavior, a first-of-its-kind measurement. For any spot, the anodic photocurrent (i) scales with (E)½ for E ≥ −0.3 V (Extended Data Fig. 8d). For E < −0.3 V, especially near Eon,ss (−0.47 V), the photocurrent 3

deviates from the (E)½ dependence due to photocurrent transient dynamics (Extended Data Fig. 4b)18. At fixed positive potentials, the photocurrent scales linearly with I0 (Extended Data Fig. 4c). Interestingly, spots with higher hole and electron activities (e.g., spot S1) show larger photocurrents, suggesting that higher surface activity is related to higher water oxidation efficiency. To relate local water oxidation efficiency to surface hole and electron activities, we analyzed quantitatively the i-E, vAR-E, and vRz-E data from 78 nanorod spots using models from Gärtner-Butler19,20 and Reichman21 (Eqs. 1-3, Methods). For each spot, these analyses gave η, the absorbed-photon-to-current efficiency within the depletion layer of the nanorod, and Eon,GB, the photocurrent onset potential predicted by the Gärtner-Butler model, as well as two effective rate constants kh and ke, representing the hole and electron surface activities, respectively. Spots with larger η expectedly have larger kh (Fig. 2b), as water oxidation efficiency should be related to surface hole activity; both terms include the efficiencies of hole transport to the surface and interfacial charge transfer22. kh of each spot is also strongly correlated with its ke, further supporting that photogenerated holes and electrons prefer to reach and react at the same sites. Importantly, the strong correlation between η and surface hole and electron activities suggests that the higher water oxidation efficiency sites on these nanorods effectively mediate both oxidation and reduction reactions (the dominant process depends on the applied potential), making them also more significant sites for surface recombination compared with those lower efficiency sites. This dual oxidation-reduction activity is consistent with that both oxidative and reductive photocatalytic reactions preferentially occur on the same {100} facets on SiTiO3 crystals16. We hypothesize that this dual activity might facilitate high efficiency sites to accept photogenerated holes from the semiconductor interior and transfer them to adsorbed OH− or H2O (i.e., the sites are oxidized and reduced sequentially in a redox-cycle, illustrated in Supplementary Scheme 1). Guided by the hole and electron reaction maps of individual nanorods, we photoelectrochemically deposited cobalt-borate OEC site-selectively using a 375 nm laser focused onto the same high or low activity spots where we measured i-E data. The OEC coverage was heterogeneous (Fig. 1h-j), expected for this amorphous catalyst23, and gave expected24 photocurrent enhancements in subsequent i-E measurements (Fig. 2a), where the enhancement saturates25 with increasing catalyst amount under our deposition conditions (Extended Data Fig. 9b). Gärtner-Butler model analysis shows that η increases for nearly all nanorod spots (i.e., Δη > 0, Fig. 2d), and on average by ~60% (Fig. 2c, top), after OEC deposition. Surprisingly, Eon,GB does not always shift negatively — 16% of the spots have positive shifts (i.e., ΔEon,GB > 0; Fig. 2d), even though on average Eon,GB shows a −70 mV shift (Fig. 2c, right). The latter agrees with the reduced overpotential of OEC modified photoanodes26. Moreover, for those spots with ΔEon,GB < 0 V, the magnitude of ΔEon,GB is smaller for spots with larger Δη (Fig. 2d). Therefore, photocurrent enhancements from OEC deposition on these nanorods are not necessarily accompanied by negative shifts in Eon,GB, and the magnitudes of their changes can be anticorrelated. The most striking effects of OEC deposition are changes in η and Eon,GB compared with their initial values. A strong negative correlation exists for the relative change in η (Δη/η, Fig. 3a): spots with smaller initial η, characterized also by lower surface hole and electron activities, 4

Page 5

gain larger relative enhancements, sometimes exceeding 200%. This trend could be partly due to larger gains in interfacial charge transfer efficiency for initially low η spots, as OEC does not typically affect charge transport25. A strong negative correlation also exists between ΔEon,GB/Eon,GB and Eon,GB (Fig. 3b): spots with more positive initial Eon,GB exhibit larger relative negative shifts in Eon,GB. We attribute this trend to the OEC’s ability to relieve excess positive surface charge25,27 and that larger negative shifts in Eon,GB are possible for sites with initially more excess surface charge28. Our findings reveal that the optimal OEC deposition sites on these nanorods for enhancement in photocurrent are those with lowest hole and electron activities, and the optimal sites for reduction in onset potential are those with most positive Eon,GB. Importantly, these two types of sites are not necessarily the same (Fig. 2d). This revelation pinpoints the challenges for engineering photoanodes with minimal amounts of catalysts. First, under typical photoelectrodeposition (or photodeposition) conditions, more catalyst material is deposited onto higher activity sites (Fig. 3c and references29,30), but these sites show less photocurrent enhancement. Second, upon scanning the potential positively, OEC deposition is expected to initiate earlier on more negative Eon,GB sites. Both effects deposit more catalyst where it is needed least. To overcome these challenges we propose a “block-deposit-remove” strategy based on function rather than structure (Extended Data Fig. 10). First, block more negative Eon,GB and higher η sites by photoelectrochemically depositing protecting groups at increasingly positive potentials. Then, deposit the desired catalyst at the remaining more positive Eon,GB or lower η sites. Finally, remove the blocking groups to yield a photoanode with optimally located catalysts. Our carrier-selective reaction-mapping and sub-particle-level photocurrent measurements are applicable to a wide range of photoelectrode materials and thus may reveal alternative optimal catalyst sites depending on the semiconductor and OEC. We envision that further exploration will provide paths toward improved catalyst-modified photoelectrodes for solar energy conversion. METHODS Synthesis and characterization of TiO2 nanorods. Single-crystalline rutile TiO2 nanorods were synthesized via a molten-flux salt method following Liu et al9 and characterized by electron microscopy and x-ray diffraction. They have rectangular cross sections with {100} side facets and {011} end facets. The ensemble-average diameter and length are 62 ± 16 nm and 263 ± 113 nm, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 1). The 37 nanorods on which we performed measurements have diameters of 127 ± 27 nm and lengths of 1735 ± 437 nm. Redox-selective fluorogenic probe reactions. The fluorogenic probe reaction was either oxidation of amplex red (AR), a h+-probe, or reduction of resazurin (Rz), an e−-probe, both generating the fluorescent product resorufin (Extended Data Fig. 1a). AR’s oxidation potential is ~2 V more negative than the valence band edge of TiO2, and Rz’s reduction potential is ~0.5 V more positive than the conduction band edge of TiO2, determined from cyclic voltammetry (Extended Data Fig. 1b-c). Single-molecule super-resolution fluorescence microscopy of h+- and e−-induced surface reactions. A schematic illustration of the single-molecule super-resolution fluorescence microscopy experimental setup is shown in Fig. 1a, based on a wide-field prism-type total internal reflection fluorescence (TIRF) microscope as described previously12. TiO2 nanorods were spin-coated onto an ITO electrode and annealed at 450 °C for 30 min, then assembled into an electrochemical microfluidic cell (~5 mm wide and 100 μm high) using double-sided tape sandwiched between an ITO electrode and a coverslip (Supplementary Fig. 3). A syringe pump drives the continuous supply of the reactant solution at 25 μL min−1, which supplies 1×1010 probe molecules (at 50 nM) per second to the cell whereas the maximum rate of product formation (or reactant consumption) from all of the nanorods in the flow cell was only 5 × 107 molecules per second, estimated from the AR oxidation reaction rate at +0.2 V and that ~ 2% of the 1.27 cm2 ITO surface area was covered with nanorods. In all experiments, the electrolyte solution was deaerated 1 M KCl, 100 mM pH 8.3 sodium borate buffer. The deaeration via N2purging here removes O2 from the cell, preventing both the competition for the photogenerated electrons by O2 and the subsequent formation of O2−, which could oxidize AR31. All experiments were performed at room temperature. A continuous wave circularly polarized 532 nm laser (630 W cm−2) induces the fluorescence of the reaction product resorufin. The TiO2 nanorods were also excited via TIRF illumination using a 375 nm laser (60 W cm−2). The fluorescence was imaged by an EMCCD camera operated at a 15 ms frame rate. In a typical experiment, a sequence of fluorescence images (e.g., “catalytic movie”) was acquired while the electrode potential, light intensity, flow rate and reactant concentration (either 50 nM AR or 50 nM Rz) were fixed. Potential-dependent movies were acquired by stepping the potential in 0.1 V increments from −0.6 to +0.2 V for AR oxidation experiments and from +0.2 to −0.6 V for Rz reduction experiments. The sequence of reactions were alternated (i.e., AR imaging before Rz imaging or vice versa for five different sample areas) and did not affect the results. The total acquisition time at each potential was 30,000 frames (i.e., 7.5 min). As we only studied isolated nanorods, charge transport between adjacent nanorods does not apply to the measured photoelectrochemical processes. The fluorescence images in the movies were analyzed using a home-written MATLAB program, as described32, to localize the positions of individual fluorescent products on single nanorods to ~30 nm precision (Extended Data Fig. 5a), in correlation with the nanorod’s SEM 10

Detailed data analysis procedures can be found in Supplementary Note 2. There are other techniques also capable of measuring electrochemical or catalytic activities of single nano-entities (see review33), including scanning electrochemical (cell) microscopy34-38, directly attaching a single particle to an ultramicroelectrode37,39, electrocatalytic current amplification via single-nanoparticle collisions40, and surface plasmon resonance spectroscopy41-43. Elemental analysis and high resolution electron microscopy for identifying possible high activity sites on bare TiO2 nanorods. The non-uniform hole and electron reaction rates along the single nanorods indicates that the higher activity sites are most likely not the {100} facet sites. Based on the TEM and elemental analysis (Extended Data Fig. 6), we hypothesize that these higher activity sites are likely impurity atoms or surface structural defects. For impurities, we have detected a number of impurity atoms in these nanorods via inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP-AES, Extended Data Fig. 6a), among which Fe has been identified as a transition metal dopant that increases both photocatalytic reduction and oxidation activity of TiO2 nanoparticles15. Yang and co-workers9, whose procedure we followed to synthesize these TiO2 nanorods, have intentionally doped these nanorods with 2% Fe, which resulted in a reduction in the overpotential for oxygen evolution on these nanorods, directly supporting our hypothesis. Elemental mapping of single nanorods further showed that some of these impurity atoms, including Fe, are heterogeneously distributed within a single nanorod (Extended Data Fig. 6b-f). There is also evidence in the literature that single impurity atoms can act as highly active catalytic sites on TiO2 nanomaterials44,45. On the other hand, according to reference46, large impurity concentrations have an adverse effect on charge carrier transport: the mobility due to impurity scattering can theoretically be shown to vary as ~1/NT, where NT is the total impurity concentration. Indeed, we measure smaller photocurrent efficiencies for larger-diameter nanorods (Supplementary Fig. 19b), for which the electrons have to travel longer distances to reach the ITO electrode. For structural defects, high resolution TEM shows that these nanorods have a structurally irregular surface layer (Extended Data Fig. 6g), even though the bulk of a nanorod is a single crystal (Extended Data Fig. 6h). There is also some indication of an irregular surface structure in the study by Liu et al9, whose synthesis procedure we followed. The irregular surface structure could be caused by surface reconstruction of the rutile {100} surface facets, which was previously observed for rutile {100} single crystal surfaces annealed in the presence of O247,48 (our nanorod sample was synthesized and annealed in air). The reconstructed surface is oxygen deficient (i.e., oxygen vacancies are present) and contains small Ti2O3 units, which Salvador reported could behave as catalytic units for water oxidation14. The two structurally similar probe molecules AR and Rz adsorb to all surface sites equivalently. To support that our two structurally similar probe molecules AR and Rz sample all surface sites in an unbiased way, we analyzed the residence time of the reaction product resorufin (which is also structurally similar to AR and Rz; Extended Data Fig. 1a) on the nanorod as a function of position. Using the three nanorods we presented as examples, we separate each nanorod into 150 nm segments (e.g., Extended Data Fig. 7a-b, d-e, g-h), and within each segment we calculated the average residence time of the individual resorufin molecules;

Page 12

this average residence time is a good measure of how strong resorufin, and presumably AR and Rz, adsorb on the surface. Across the different segments within each nanorod, the average residence time of resorufin is essentially equivalent (~15 ms) and is independent of location (Extended Data Fig. 7c, f, i), even though different segments differ significantly in reaction rates. This independence on location for the average residence time indicates that resorufin, and presumably AR and Rz, adsorb equally on all surface sites along the side-facets. We also determined the average residence times of resorufin from the 78 nanoscale spots where vAR-E, vRZ-E and i-E behaviors were analyzed. The average product residence time is independent of the effective rate constants kh and ke (Extended Data Fig. 7j), further confirming that resorufin, and presumably AR and Rz, do not preferentially adsorb onto higher or lower activity sites. Extended Data Figure 10 | Proposed “block-deposit-remove” strategy that has the potential to selectively deposit a desired OEC on low activity and positive onset potential sites. First, a removable protective group can be photoelectrochemically deposited starting from negative potentials to block negative Eon,GB sites. To block high activity sites with similar Eon,GB values, we propose that using short light pulses may cause more protecting group material to be deposited at higher activity sites first. In this way, low η and positive Eon,GB sites will remain unblocked. The potential could then be stepped from negative E to the ensemble-averaged steady state photocurrent onset value, for example, and thus all remaining sites with Eon,GB values more positive than the ensemble-averaged value will also remain unblocked. Second, the potential can be poised to a positive value and a long light pulse can be used to deposit the desired OEC on all remaining sites: those with positive Eon,GB and low η. Finally, the protective group can be subsequently removed to yield a photoanode with optimally located OECs.
