Summary Increasing temperatures in northern high latitudes are causing permafrost to thaw1, making

large amounts of previously frozen organic matter vulnerable to microbial decomposition2. Permafrost thaw also creates a mosaic of drier and wetter soil conditions3 that determine the amount and form (carbon dioxide (CO2), or methane (CH4)) of carbon (C) released to the atmosphere. The rate and form of C release control the magnitude of the permafrost C feedback, however, their relative contribution to climate warming remains unclear4,5. We quantified the effect of increasing temperature and changes from aerobic to anaerobic soil conditions using 26 soil incubation studies from the permafrost zone. Here we show, using two separate meta-analyses, that a 10 °C increase in incubation temperature increased C release by a factor of 2.1 (95% confidence interval (CI), 1.9 to 2.2). In comparison, under aerobic incubation conditions, soils released 3.4 (95% CI, 2.6 to 4.4) times more C than under anaerobic conditions and 2.3 (95% CI, 1.9 to 3.0) times more when accounting for the higher heat trapping capacity of CH4. These results imply that permafrost ecosystems thawing under aerobic conditions and releasing CO2 will strengthen the permafrost C feedback more than water logged systems releasing CO2 and CH4. High latitude ecosystems store almost twice as much C in soils than what is currently contained in the atmosphere6,7. As the global climate warms, northern high latitudes are experiencing rapid increases in temperature8 that have the potential to not only increase C emissions from previously frozen C in permafrost and active layer9 but also to indirectly affect the C cycle through changes in regional and local hydrology. Warmer temperatures increase thawing of ice-rich permafrost and the melting of ground ice which causes the land surface to collapse into the space that was previously filled by ice resulting in thermokarst terrain10. Permafrost thawing can also gradually increase active layer thickness (seasonally thawed ground), causing poorly drained soil conditions in lowlands or drier conditions in uplands where natural drainage can increase as the water table deepens. On the other

Page 3

hand, permafrost thaw and collapse can cause soils to become waterlogged where anaerobic conditions prevail and C is released in the form of CO2 and CH4. One major uncertainty in determining the climate forcing impact of permafrost ecosystems is whether the effect of changes in subsurface hydrology and oxygen availability on greenhouse gas release in permafrost ecosystems are of similar magnitude as the effect of increasing temperatures. In addition to soil temperature and moisture, the chemical composition (e.g. carbon to nitrogen ratio)11, physical protection by soil minerals, microbial community dynamics, and other environmental controls such as pH and nutrient availability also impact the amount of C released to the atmosphere12. While temperature and soil moisture (i.e. oxygen availability) are the most important changing environmental factors for future C release from permafrost, their effect size on C release has been poorly quantified. Therefore, we used a meta-analysis approach to quantify the ratio of C release with a) a 10 °C increase in temperature and b) between soils that were incubated aerobically and anaerobically (see Methods). We compiled a database of 26 incubation studies (Table S1), which included soils from across the entire permafrost zone from three broad ecosystem types (boreal forest, peatland and tundra). Soils were collected from both the active layer and the permafrost layer, and all samples included in this synthesis were incubated at temperatures >-0.5 °C (Table S1). We quantified the warming capacity of C release from CO2 and CH4 under aerobic versus anaerobic conditions by accounting for both the amount of CO2 and CH4 produced and the relative global warming potential (GWP) of these two greenhouse gases. When investigating the climate forcing potential of the permafrost zone, such a consideration is necessary as the GWP of CH4 is 34 times higher than that of CO2 on a 100 year timescale13.

For the 22 incubation studies that used at least two incubation temperatures, a 10 °C increase in temperature (5-15 °C) resulted in an increase of net C release by a factor of 2.1 (95% CI, 1.9 to 2.2; Fig.

Page 4

1). This pattern was consistent across boreal forest, peatland and tundra ecosystems as well as for mineral (% C <20) and organic (% C >20) soils and did not vary significantly among soils that originated from the active layer or from the permafrost soil layer. Additionally, incubation duration and oxygen availability did not significantly change the effect of warming on C release. It is a well-known fact that increasing temperatures enhance C release and our overall mean value of 2.1 is within the range of previously published literature within and outside of the permafrost zone14. Carbon release under aerobic incubation conditions was on average 3.4 (95% CI, 2.6 to 4.4; Fig. 2) times higher than under anaerobic incubation conditions. The ratio of aerobic:anaerobic C release was not affected by ecosystem type, soil type, active layer or permafrost layer, incubation temperature or duration (p > 0.5). The contribution of CH4-C to total soil C release in anaerobic incubation studies was low (5.5%, 95% CI 2.8% to 8.2%; Fig. 3) but significantly increased at higher incubation temperatures (Table S5). Methane production rates and emissions are known to be highly sensitive to temperature15, and given that CH4 exerts a greater influence on the climate than CO2, this sensitivity will impact the strength of the permafrost C feedback as global temperatures rise. The contribution of CH4-C to total soil C loss was significantly higher during the incubation of tundra and peatland soils than in boreal forest soils, where CH4 production was <4% of total C released under anaerobic conditions (Fig. 3). This may be related to the fact that tundra and peatland ecosystems experience anaerobic conditions more frequently in nature and thus are more likely to have established methanogenic communities. Overall, the CH4-C contribution to total C release in anaerobic incubations was too small to significantly affect trends in the total soil C release in the incubations. We found that the aerobic incubations not only supported greater total soil C release, but were also associated with higher GWP of greenhouse gas (CO2 and CH4) release. Even when accounting for the 34 times higher GWP of CH4 relative to CO2, the aerobic:anaerobic CO2-C equivalent (sum of CO2-C plus CH4-C expressed as CO2-C equivalent) was 2.3 times higher in fully aerobic soils than under anaerobic conditions (Figure 2). The contribution of CH4-C

calculated as CO2-C equivalent to total CO2-C equivalent increased to 21% (95% CI 12.8% to 29.2%) which shows the relatively higher contribution of CH4 when considering the global warming potential of both gases. The comparison of C release under aerobic and anaerobic incubation conditions provides important variables to constrain models and highlights the significance of anaerobic CO2 production in incubation studies. However, it is important to keep in mind that incubation results do not equal in-situ measurements of CO2 and CH4 emissions. Aerobic decomposition is the dominant C mineralization pathway in unsaturated soils and produces CO2 while anaerobic mineralization generates both CO2 and CH4. Net CH4 emissions are the result of CH4 production (anaerobic process) and consumption through CH4 oxidation (largely aerobic process), while these incubation results reflect CH4 production alone. Changes in CH4 emissions from the permafrost zone have been attributed to changes in soil moisture, thermal conditions and vegetation shifts16, and less to the increased availability of easily decomposable organic matter as the active layer thickens17. Additionally, maximum CH4 production rates in incubation studies are sometimes reached after a lag time of a few days to months depending on incubation temperature, moisture content, ecosystem type, and sample depth18. Lag times for maximum CH4 production in incubation studies occur because alternate electron acceptors (e.g. nitrate, ferric iron, and sulfate) are energetically favorable before methanogenic archaeal communities can establish. Although we did not see any significant change with CH4 contribution to total soil C release under anaerobic conditions with incubation length, under field conditions, the contribution by CH4 might be even higher than what was found in the incubation studies. It follows from this analysis that whether northern landscapes will become wetter or drier under global change is a critical mechanism affecting the forms and amounts of C release. Changing surface hydrology due to permafrost thawing will produce a mosaic of dry and wet ecosystems; and thus large uncertainties are associated with C release under changing hydrological conditions3. Another important

Page 6

factor is whether permafrost C (as it thaws) emerges in aerobic versus anaerobic environments which can happen even if surface hydrology stays constant. In comparison, globally increasing temperatures will affect the entire Arctic to an equal degree and will vary little on the small landscape scale. Projected temperatures in the Arctic are expected to increase by 8.3 °C (±1.9 °C) for 2081-2100 compared to 1986- 2005 when using the highest climate warming trajectory (Representative Concentration Pathway (RCP), 8.5) which is 2.4 times the global average warming19. This increase in air temperature will not immediately be propagated into the soil and so the 10 °C increase is an upper limit. It has been proposed that the response of C release to warming may be transient and weaken over time20,21, which would affect the strength of the permafrost C feedback long-term. Higher C losses under warming can be partially offset by increased plant C uptake during the growing season21,22, feedbacks between living and decaying plant roots, and C stored in soil. Although plant communities in the Arctic are predicted to undergo structural, functional and compositional changes with climate change and boreal forests are expected to expand northwards23, our study shows that the temperature effect on soil C mineralization rates varies little among different ecosystem types. Besides changing plant communities, various processes are involved with warmer temperatures, such as the stimulation of decomposition of older soil organic matter with higher plant activity24, that are not accounted for in laboratory studies. Hence, our results should be viewed as a potential for increased soil C release under warmer temperatures, and not as an indicator of changes in net ecosystem C balance. The existence of permafrost exerts a strong control on wetland distribution25, and large changes in the areal extent of wetlands are expected as permafrost thaws26. Ponds and lakes are decreasing in number and area in the discontinuous and sporadic permafrost zone, while changes in the continuous permafrost zone are less consistent27,28. Besides a more gradual decrease of permafrost extent, abrupt thaw processes will change surface hydrology because of loss of ground ice (by melting and runoff).

These abrupt thaw processes not only expose deep C stores to microbial breakdown but also determine

Page 7

whether decomposition releases CO2 (aerobic mineralization) or CO2 and CH4 under anaerobic mineralization5. Most permafrost landscapes will show a wide range of soil moisture conditions, from completely saturated and underwater to well-drained and mostly aerobic especially at the surface. Therefore, the greater release of CO2-C equivalent from permafrost-affected soils under aerobic conditions emphasizes the importance of monitoring and predicting changes in hydrology in a warming Arctic. In this synthesis, we used two extreme scenarios to compare the effect of changing environmental conditions (a temperature increase of 10 °C and a change from aerobic to anaerobic soil conditions) on C release from permafrost-affected soils and showed that increasing temperatures have a large effect on CO2 and CH4 production but that changes in landscape hydrology caused by permafrost erosion can exert even greater effects. Because 3.4 times more soil C is mineralized under aerobic than anaerobic soil conditions, when permafrost thaws and drains, one unit of soil will have a more than three times higher impact on climate change than when the same unit of soil thaws under undrained (anaerobic) conditions. This implies that the permafrost C feedback to climate change will be stronger when a larger percentage of the permafrost zone undergoes thaw in dry and oxygen-rich environments, even in the conditions where CO2 is the dominant gas released to the atmosphere rather than CH4. 137 138 Methods: Permafrost incubation database. We compiled a database of soil incubation studies that used soils from the northern circumpolar permafrost zone. We searched the Web of Science for published incubation studies using permafrost soils (Supplementary Material) and screened the results for actual incubation studies following these criteria: 1) incubation duration of more than 24 hours; 2) either C flux over time had to be available so we could calculate cumulative C release, or a cumulative C release value for the whole experimental period had to be reported; 3) initial C content for individual samples had to be

Page 8

available; 4) soils had to be incubated at a minimum of two constant incubation temperatures, or soils had to be incubated aerobically and anaerobically; 5) for anaerobic incubations, the anaerobic headspace had to be created using oxygen-free gas (usually N2 or He), and 6) anaerobic samples needed to report CO2 and CH4. We identified a total of 26 studies that met all criteria, of which 22 had a temperature treatment and 10 incubated soils aerobically and anaerobically (Table S1). The 22 incubation studies that used a temperature treatment could be either exclusively aerobic or anaerobic or both, so there is some overlap in the datasets (see Table S1). Samples were considered aerobic when samples were incubated at field capacity and flushed with ambient air, under freely drained conditions, or kept at 50-60% moisture content. Samples were grouped into three different ecosystem types (boreal forest, peatland and tundra), which were assigned to soil samples based on information from study authors. Peatland samples could originate from the boreal or tundra zone and were assigned to a sample if the author had specifically called it a peatland. 157 158 Analysis. When fluxes over time were available, we calculated cumulative C release (for CO2-C and CH4- C) by interpolating between successive measurements using linear interpolation and then summing up daily C release over the entire incubation period. We calculated C release as a percentage of total soil C to account for different initial C concentrations and to compare relative differences between various soil types. For comparison purposes we standardized total cumulative C release at 5 °C and 15 °C using sample specific response to temperature (Q10) but kept the given incubation temperature for the comparison between aerobic and anaerobic C release. Aerobic C release is aerobic CO2-C, and anaerobic C release is the sum of anaerobic CO2-C and CH4-C. To account for the higher GWP of CH4 we converted CH4-C into CO2-C equivalent by multiplying CH4-C by its most recently consensus GWP of 34 (includes climate-carbon feedback13) while also accounting for the different molecular weights of CO2 and CH4 since GWP is calculated on a weight basis for the two gases.
