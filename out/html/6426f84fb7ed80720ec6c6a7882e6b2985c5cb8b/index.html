Keywords strain; thermal expansion; degradation; flexible; reliability

Abstract We report on an overlooked factor affecting stability: the residual stresses in perovskite films, which are tensile and can exceed 50 MPa in magnitude, a value high enough to deform copper. These stresses provide a significant driving force for fracture. In addition, we show that films are more unstable under tensile stress—and conversely more stable under compressive stress—when exposed to heat or humidity. Increasing the formation temperature of perovskite films directly correlates with larger residual stresses, a result of the high thermal expansion coefficient of perovskites. Specifically, this tensile stress forms upon cooling to room temperature, as the substrate constrains the perovskite from shrinking. No evidence of stress relaxation was observed, with the purely elastic film stress attributed to the thermal expansion mismatch between the perovskite and substrate. Additionally, we demonstrate that using a bath conversion method to form the perovskite film at room temperature leads to low stress values that are unaffected by further annealing, indicating complete perovskite formation prior to annealing. We conclude that reducing the film stress is a novel method for improving perovskite stability, which can be accomplished by lower formation temperatures, flexible substrates with high thermal expansion coefficients, and externally applied compressive stress after fabrication. Introduction Metal halide perovskites hold tremendous promise for next-generation solar cells, more than any other recently developed low-cost active PV material[1–5]. To fulfill this promise, perovskites must first overcome the chemical[6–8] and thermomechanical instability[9] that has long been observed in them. Recent efforts have demonstrated progress in improving the chemical stability of perovskite devices intrinsically by tuning cation composition[10,11] and extrinsically by encapsulation[12–15]. However, the perovskite field has demonstrated insouciance toward thermomechanical stability, even though it is essential for the commercialization of photovoltaic cells or light-emitting diodes. While some promising results have recently been achieved in scalability with power conversion efficiencies of 12% for 100 cm2 perovskite devices[16], the thermomechanical instability of perovskites remains a significant challenge to producing modulescale perovskite solar cells[17] with operational lifetimes comparable to c-Si and CdTe[18]. 2

Page 3

In particular, stresses are generated in perovskite films during processing and magnified in service by environmental effects such as thermal cycling, resulting in the formation of defects and propagation of fracture and delamination[19]. Additionally, cracks that develop in the film are a source of accelerated degradation for the transport of gases, moisture, and other environmental species. Unfortunately, perovskite layers are exceptionally fragile and susceptible to delamination as measured by their fracture energy[20,21]—less robust than OPVs by an order of magnitude[22] and c-Si or CIGS solar cells by two orders of magnitude[9]. Despite the significance of film stresses for device stability, the origin and magnitude of stresses in perovskite films have been largely overlooked. In addition to causing fracture, stress accelerates the rate of photochemical degradation in many materials—such as fuel cell membranes[23], laser diodes[24], and encapsulants[25]—and a recent report shows that perovskite are no exception[26]. Perovskites can accumulate residual stresses during processing through several pathways. For example, in solar cell devices, perovskites have much higher coefficients of thermal expansion (CTE) than the other device layers and the glass substrate[27]. When perovskites are annealed after deposition and subsequently cooled back to room temperature, a lower-CTE substrate constrains the perovskite from contracting, an effect that generates lattice strain in the film[26]. Given the extreme mechanical fragility of perovskite films, and the possibility for further stresses developing during fabrication and operation (e.g., exposure to environmental stressors such as thermal cycling[28]), these residual stresses could contribute significantly to light, heat, and moisture-based chemical degradation as well as fracture in devices[29]. In this work, we measured film stresses and examined implications for instability in perovskite solar cells comprising cesium-methylammonium-formamidinium lead iodide-bromide (Cs0.05MA0.16FA0.79Pb(I0.83Br0.17)3, referred to as CsMAFA) which is cubic at room temperature[30]. We also studied perovskite films of methylammonium lead iodide (MAPbI3), which is tetragonal at room temperature, fabricated with antisolvent methods and annealed under different conditions[31]. These antisolvent fabrication methods are commonly used to extract solvent, forcing supersaturation and crystallization during or after spin coating[32]. Furthermore, we examined the effect of the substrate coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) and compared the perovskite film stress values when fabricated on lower-CTE glass as well as flexible substrates with CTEs that more closely match those of the perovskite. We observed that the stability of perovskite films

under heat or humidity could be substantially improved by taking measures to reduce the tensile stress.

Results and Discussion Film stresses in perovskite layers evolve significantly during processing. In the initial deposition step, the precursor solution does not strongly interact with the substrate or have the requisite viscosity to support stress and is therefore free to move without any constraint during the spin coating process, as shown in Figure 1. Subsequent heating of the layers results in removal of residual solvent, conversion of an intermediate phase likely composed of edge-sharing PbI2-based layers coordinated to solvent molecules[33] into a crystalline perovskite structure[34], and enhanced chemical interactions with the substrate that constrain the film from expanding or contracting freely. This constraint is a significant driver of film stresses because perovskites have a thermal expansion coefficient (CTE ~ 5010–6 K–1)[35] that is five times greater than the glass substrate (CTE ~ 1010–6 K–1)[36]. After annealing, the fully converted perovskite layer cools to room temperature, an event which would normally lead to a large contraction in the film relative to the glass if the film was free-standing; however, the constraint of the perovskite by the underlying glass substrate impedes this volume reduction, resulting in a tensile stress in the perovskite layer (Figure 1a). We examined the connection between perovskite film stress and resulting stability by depositing MAPbI3 on flexible polyethylene terephthalate (PET) substrates and then applying an external stress ranging from –130 MPa (compressive) to 130 MPa (tensile) on the perovskite films by bending the substrates in either a concave (i.e., applied compressive stress) or convex (i.e., applied tensile stress) shape. This observation was consistent with a previous report that compressive stress increased perovskite photostability[26], providing further evidence of the correlation between the degradation of perovskite due to moisture, heat, or light, and the tensile stress of the film. Another way to change the film stress is to deposit the film on a substrate with a higher CTE (Figure 2b), which also showed similar, significant differences in degradation, as will be discussed later in more detail. In order to evaluate the effect of annealing temperature on film stress, we first developed a process in which the perovskite could be formed over a range of annealing temperatures with comparable device performance. CsMAFA devices were fabricated in the p-i-n architecture by employing polytriarylamine (PTAA) as the hole transport layer as described previously[39]. The antisolvent method—which has produced perovskite devices with world-record efficiencies[1]— was used featuring a diethyl ether (DEE) wash during spin coating to remove the high-boiling point solvent from the film, force supersaturation, and begin perovskite nucleation. The perovskite was then either left at room temperature (25 °C) for 12 h, or annealed at 60 °C or 100 °C for 1 h. The devices were completed with C60, bathocuproine (BCP), and Ag by thermal evaporation. The device performance, ranging from ~14 to 17% PCE, was comparable for devices across the range of annealing temperatures (Figure S1 and Table S1). The measured bandgap decreased slightly with annealing temperature. (Figure S2). A vacuum was used for comparison to crystallize CsMAFA films at room temperature using a glovebox antechamber at –1 bar, but the device performance was reduced (Table S2). The measured residual film stress values for CsMAFA fabricated with the DEE antisolvent exchange method were plotted as a function of annealing temperature (Figure 3a). Films annealed at 100 °C exhibited a tensile stress of 57.6 ± 4.9 MPa, while those annealed at 60 °C resulted in a tensile stress of 20.7 ± 6.6 MPa. Perovskites formed without any annealing (25 °C) exhibited a stress of –10.8 ± 15.2 MPa. We observed a clear linear relationship between the residual tensile stresses in perovskite layers and the temperature at which the films were annealed, with higher annealing temperatures resulting in larger stress values, which we attributed to the CTE mismatch between perovskite and substrate, as described above. SEM images indicated a dense, compact structure with similar morphology for all annealing temperatures, indicating a minimal impact on film stress from surface morphology (Figure S3). However, we observed that the domain size decreased slightly for the CsMAFA films formed at 25 °C. The film stress is more likely to correlate with a property such as grain boundaries that affects the entire thickness of the film as opposed to a surface property. Additionally, grazing incidence X-ray diffraction measurements were performed on the films annealed at 25, 60, and 100 °C on glass to measure the film strain, and the calculated stress values showed the same trend of increasing with annealing temperature (Figures S4 and S5). The estimated film stress was an excellent predictor of the measured stresses (Figure 3a), indicating that the thermal expansion mismatch was the dominant mechanism for stress formation in perovskite films upon cooling. Interestingly, a negative (i.e., compressive) stress was measured in the perovskite formed at room temperature, suggesting an incompletely converted perovskite and the presence of residual solvent in the film. This hypothesis was supported by the observation that the Jsc for devices with perovskite formed at 25 °C was slightly lower than for devices with perovskite annealed at higher temperatures (Figure S1). The linear correlation between stress and annealing temperature indicated that no stress relaxation occurred in our studied temperature range. The perovskite film was fully formed during annealing and likely did not change significantly in morphology when cooled back to room temperature. This effect was not surprising given that stress relaxation is most commonly observed in viscoelastic materials such as polymers[40], which exhibit vastly different mechanical properties than perovskites[9]. And while perovskite devices comprise a stack of several layers where the perovskite is not in direct contact with the substrate, these associated charge transport materials are extremely thin and do not contribute significantly to the stress experienced by the perovskite. To further support this hypothesis, we compared the perovskite film stress when deposited on nickel oxide (NiO) or PTAA. Figure 3b shows the measured stress was independent of the surface upon which the perovskite is deposited. This observation was expected since the glass 6

substrate was four orders of magnitude thicker than the ~20 nm thick charge transport layers, NiO and PTAA, and thus dominated the stress as predicted by equation (2). It is important to note that film stress is independent of the surface, but not the substrate used. Figure S6 shows that the correlation between stress and annealing temperature is independent of perovskite formulation, and Figure S7 shows that the correlation is independent of deposition method. The observed stress is also independent of the antisolvent—assuming the antisolvent is fully removed from the perovskite film—as the antisolvent method that is used in Figures S3 and S4 was chlorobenzene. Specifically, a tensile stress of 57.6 ±4.9 MPa was measured for DEE compared to 49.3 ±3.2 MPa for chlorobenzene for films formed at 100 °C. Based on the observation that the residual stresses in the perovskite layers could be predicted entirely by equation (2), we believed that film stress would depend only on formation temperature, where the film solidifies and no further relaxation occurs. To confirm this hypothesis, we applied a modified antisolvent method in which films of MAI/PbI2 were spin-coated and then submerged in a DEE bath to convert the film to perovskite at room temperature (Figure 1b)[41]. We used a very low boiling point solvent with the intention of ensuring that no antisolvent remained in the final perovskite film. Previous work has shown that the use of higher boiling point antisolvents, such as chlorobenzene, require annealing to evaporate any residual solvent and to promote full perovskite crystallization[42]. Over the course of two minutes in the DEE bath, the MAI/PbI2 film darkened, indicative of conversion to the perovskite phase. Next, the films were thermally annealed at either 25, 60, or 100 °C. No significant change in the film color or morphology was observed during this annealing step and the measured stresses were largely unchanged (Figure 3a). The stresses of films annealed at 100 °C, 60 °C, and 25 °C (SEM and XRD of no anneal films in Figure S8) were 3.0 ± 14.5 MPa, –3.2 ± 19.0 MPa, and –2.6 ± 14.5 MPa, respectively. These results suggested that for the case of bath-converted perovskite, any further processing requiring elevated temperatures—such as the deposition of subsequent charge transport layers—would not affect the residual stresses in the perovskite films. While lowering the formation temperature of the perovskite was one pathway to reduce the film stresses, another clear approach from equation (2) was to reduce the mismatch in the coefficients of thermal expansion between the perovskite and substrate. Perovskites of varying compositions exhibit relatively similar thermal expansion coefficients[35,43], and thus we 7

Page 8

determined that focusing on increasing the substrate CTE would be the best strategy to lower residual stress. Significantly lowered stresses are expected if polymeric substrates such as polycarbonate (PC) (CTE ~ 6510–6 K–1) or PET (CTE ~ 2010–6 K–1) were used instead of inorganic substrates such as silicon (CTE ~ 2.610–6 K–1). Indeed, as noted before, perovskite films annealed at 100 °C on silicon exhibited significantly higher film stress (54.0 ±8.2 MPa) than those annealed on polycarbonate (PC) (11.8 ± 2.0 MPa) (Figure 2b). Improved thermal stability of perovskite on polymeric substrates is shown in Figures 2b and S9 and discussed with in situ Xray diffraction measurements on a heated stage in Figures 4, S10, S11, and S12. Perovskite devices were also fabricated on glass and PET for CsMAFA films formed at 100 °C that were aged in an N2 glovebox at 85 °C for 1000 h (Figure S13). Although the perovskite on PET experienced a slightly improved average PCE of 59% compared to 46% on glass, the majority of PCE decrease was attributed to metal-halide degradation based on yellowing beneath the Ag contact. The improved perovskite stability that is discussed is inherent to the perovskite itself, as when transport layers and metal contacts are introduced, a number of additional degradation modes are evident. Thus, reducing film stress enhanced the intrinsic stability of only the perovskite, yet additional caution must be exercised in devices to ensure that other degradation modes are minimized based on chemical interactions with adjacent layers. In addition, although multiple perovskite formulations were used in the study, we were deliberate in our decisions regarding choice of composition. In particular, the CsMAFA was selected because it has been widely used to produce high efficiency devices. MAPbI3 was used in the various aging experiments and at the beamline because of the faster degradation rate. Since compressive stress was found to counteract the residual tensile stress in perovskites and be beneficial to their stability, another approach to reduce the film stresses was to externally apply a compressive stress. By bending the substrate to a given radius, the applied stress, 𝜎𝐴 , could be estimated using plane strain mechanics in equation (1) above. The final film stress was simply the difference between the measured stress and 𝜎𝐴 . Reducing the overall film stress through substrate bending was previously shown to dramatically improve perovskite stability by limiting PbI2 formation through reduced ion migration in MAPbI3 films exposed to light[26]. As discussed previously and shown in Figure 2a, 𝜎𝐴 of –65 or –130 MPa significantly improved perovskite resistance to degradation under damp air and dry heat conditions. We propose that the improved stability on a polymeric substrate and with lower annealing temperature could be attributed to 8

Films formed at higher annealing temperatures were more susceptible to degradation since the G values increased and began to approach Gc. Additionally, perovskites formed at elevated temperatures were shown to be more fragile—with measured Gc values of 0.67 ±0.24 J m–2 for perovskite formed at 25 °C, 0.49 ±0.06 J m–2 for 60 °C, and 0.31 ±0.09 J m–2 for 100 °C (Figure 4)—showing that the increased residual stress contributed to mechanical instability in addition to the previously discussed environmental instabilities. The implications of the increased mechanical fragility of perovskite with higher film stresses are significant. As perovskite scale-up becomes increasingly relevant[17], a critical concern in the transition to large area cells is the ability to withstand residual stresses developing within the module that, if sufficiently high, may drive delamination due to thermal mismatch strains. While strains imposed upon the layers due to mismatches in thermal expansion coefficients are unchanged in a large-scale module, the resulting absolute displacements at the edge of the module scales with the module size—i.e., larger modules result in larger displacements. These displacements can provide a driving force for crack propagation due to the large shear stresses that exist at the edge of the module[46,47]. These factors underscore the importance of design for mechanical stability in perovskite solar cells. Indeed, caution must be exercised in design to ensure that internal stresses are not sufficiently large to cause delamination in the perovskite or associated charge transport layers. In addition to reducing residual stress in perovskite films, strategies to 9

Page 10

increase Gc through extrinsic reinforcement and stress distribution, such as device-integrated scaffolds as well as module scale perovskites (70–100 cm2) incorporating a porous triple layer to contain and reinforce the perovskite, have been recently demonstrated to dramatically improve the mechanical integrity of perovskites [15,16,48] This study has also uncovered important new questions to be answered in the fabrication, operation, and even testing of perovskite devices. Our work has shown that, in the case of devices on substrates with CTEs below ~ 5010–6 K–1, degradation does not increase exponentially with temperature because the tensile stress in the perovskite is reduced with temperature. This result has important implications for the measurements and testing protocols that are being followed by the majority of the perovskite field. Based on our study, one avenue for future work to address would be to determine whether testing the stability of devices under exposure to environmental elements is truly representative of the conditions experienced in service. Accelerated aging of modules is typically performed at 85 °C and 85% RH, with 1000 h of exposure considered comparable to 10 years of field use in Miami, Florida. However, given the significantly different residual stresses present in perovskite films at 85 °C when compared to their more likely peak field temperature ~60 °C[49], it raises the question as to whether or not the current testing procedures are appropriate indicators of real-world stability in perovskites.

Conclusion Our work provided the first quantitative measurement of the significant mechanical stresses that develop during the fabrication of perovskite films. A direct relation was determined between film stress and stability in both perovskite films and solar cells, and we demonstrated methods for reducing this stress and improving the inherent moisture and thermal stability of perovskite films. Simple steps can be taken to improve the intrinsic stability of perovskite films by selecting a lowtemperature processing method or a polymeric substrate with higher coefficient of thermal expansion (as long as the device operation is below the annealing temperature). The benefit of applying compressive stress opens up the potential for novel fabrication methods, e.g., prestraining the substrate during perovskite processing. This type of approach is not without precedence in related fields, as strain engineering is used to improve carrier mobility in microelectronic devices. Experimental Section Perovskite materials: Methylammonium iodide, formamidinium iodide, and formamidinium bromide (Dyesol), cesium iodide (Sigma), and lead iodide and lead bromide (TCI) were used as received. All solvents were anhydrous and used as received (Acros). Fabrication methods: The antisolvent exchange methods were performed following procedures that are reported elsewhere [30,41]. The Cs0.05MA0.16FA0.79Pb(I0.83Br0.17)3 perovskite was spin coated in a 4:1 DMF:DMSO v/v solution with a two-step recipe of 1000 rpm for 10 s followed by 6000 rpm for 35 s, and 500 L of DEE antisolvent was applied 10 s before the end of the second step. MAPbI3 was spin coated at 4000 rpm for 30 s in an NMP solution before submerging in a DEE bath. Stress measurements: Perovskite film stress measurements were obtained using the substrate curvature method where a laser scans across the center of the substrate and the deflection is calculated to determine radius of curvature. This measurement can be performed on any substrate as long as the substrate is reasonably thin but still rigid enough to not deform during handling. Additionally, as one side must be mirror-like, which can be achieved by evaporating metal on one side. Most measurements were performed on the back side of double side polished Si wafers in order to ensure the laser could reflect from the surface of the substrate after depositing the perovskite.
