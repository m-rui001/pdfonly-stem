Abstract: Long-term elevated nitrogen (N) input from anthropogenic sources may cause soil acidification and decrease crop yield, yet the response of the belowground microbial community to long-term N input alone or in combination with phosphorus (P) and potassium (K) is poorly understood. We explored the effect of long-term N and NPK fertilization on soil bacterial diversity and community composition using meta-analysis of a global dataset. Nitrogen fertilization decreased soil pH, and increased soil organic carbon (C) and available N contents. Bacterial taxonomic diversity was decreased by N fertilization alone, but was increased by NPK fertilization. The effect of N fertilization on bacterial diversity varied with soil texture and water management, but was independent of crop type or N application rate. Changes in bacterial diversity was positively related to both soil pH and organic C content under N fertilization alone, but only to soil organic C under NPK fertilization. Microbial biomass C decreased with decreasing bacterial diversity under long-term N fertilization. Nitrogen fertilization increased the relative abundance of Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria, but reduced the abundance of Acidobacteria, consistent with the general life history strategy theory for bacteria. The positive correlation between N application rate and the relative abundance of Actinobacteria indicates that increased N availability favored the growth of Actinobacteria. This first global analysis of long-term N and NPK fertilization that differentially affects bacterial diversity and community composition provides a reference for nutrient management strategies for maintaining belowground microbial diversity in agro-ecosystems worldwide.

Introduction Agro-ecosystems occupy more than one third of the global land surface (Smith et al., 2008) and provide sustenance for over seven billion people globally. To maintain soil fertility and productivity in the face of a growing demand, external nutrient sources such as mineral fertilizers are added to agro-ecosystems annually (Galloway et al., 2008). However, inappropriate fertilizer input may lead to soil degradation, groundwater nutrient pollution, and surface water eutrophication (Conley et al., 2009). In particular, intensive nitrogen (N) fertilization has been reported to induce soil acidification (Guo et al., 2010), reduce soil fertility (Divito et al., 2011), and ultimately decrease soil productivity (Toljander et al., 2008). As N fertilizer input is expected to further increase in the future due to the increasing global demand for food (Chen et al., 2016), maintaining a balance among food production, nutrient input, and agro-ecosystem function and stability are critical. In most agro-ecosystems, it has been widely established that nutrient addition changes aboveground plant biomass and plant community composition (Gough et al., 2000; Suding et al. 2005). To date, great impacts of nutrient addition on the belowground microorganisms, especially soil bacteria, the most abundant organisms in agricultural soils, have been reported. Soil microorganisms play critical roles in the

Bacterial diversity that includes genetic variability within species, and the number and relative abundance of taxa and functional traits in communities is critical to ecosystem function (Torsvik & Øvreås, 2002). However, soil bacterial diversity is very sensitive to environmental changes, such as those caused by anthropogenic disturbances, including long-term nutrient input (Allison & Martiny, 2008). Long-term nutrient input, in particular fertilization with mineral nutrients, can alter microbial interactions, soil biogeochemical processes, and maintenance of soil productivity. To date, the effect of long-term fertilization with mineral nutrients such as N, phosphorus (P) and potassium (K) on soil microbial biomass and associated activities has been extensively studied (Geisseler & Scow, 2014; Treseder, 2008), yet studies focusing on relationships between fertilization and soil bacterial diversity remain fragmented and in need of synthesis. For instance, the richness and diversity of soil bacteria may (Ling et al., 2017) or may not (Zhong et al., 2010) be reduced by N fertilization. Long-term fertilization has also been shown to change microbial community composition (Fanin et al., 2015；Zhen et al., 2014), and these changes were either determined by the carbon (C) and/or N availability (Ramirez et al., 2010) or soil pH (Rousk et al., 2010) changed by N input. These inconsistent patterns are attributed to differences in soil type, management regime, crop type, fertilizer application rate, and other biotic and abiotic factors (Hartmann et al., 2015; Lopes et al., 2011; Lupwayi et al., 2011). A synthesis of results from diverse studies is needed using consistent and comprehensive criteria to provide a unified understanding of the impact of long-term fertilization on microbial diversity. Here, we conducted a meta-analysis based on 69 publications with 427 observations on changes in bacterial community under long-term mineral fertilization globally. This meta-analysis includes analyses on soil alpha-diversity, beta-diversity, relative abundance of dominant phylogenetic groups, and microbial biomass, under fertilization with different fertilizer types and rates, and a variety of soil chemical and physical properties. The focus is on the long-term effect of nutrient input on soil microbial community in agro-ecosystems. Our objective was to answer the following questions: (1) do soil bacterial diversity and community composition respond consistently to long-term N fertilization across the globe? (2) what factors dominantly control bacterial community diversity under N fertilization? (3) will the effect of fertilization with N alone on the bacterial community differ with combined N and PK fertilization? We hypothesize that long-term N fertilization would decrease soil bacterial diversity, alter community composition and favor the abundance of obligotrophic over copiotrophic taxa，due to changes in soil pH and nutrient (e.g., C and N) availability. Materials and Methods Data collection To investigate the effect of long-term mineral fertilizer application on soil bacterial diversity and community composition, we collected data from peer-reviewed studies published from 1997 to 2017 for conducting this meta-analysis. The following criteria were used to select appropriate studies: (i) the duration of the field trial is ≥ 2 years; (ii) studies that report bacterial alpha diversity (i.e., richness and diversity) and/or bacterial community composition in both fertilized and non-fertilized (i.e., control) treatments. Soil chemical and biochemical parameters including soil pH, organic C, available N and microbial biomass C were also extracted if reported in the papers collected from above criterion. In total, 69 publications with 427 observations were included in this analysis. Of these, a total of 133 and 120 comparisons reported the effects of N alone and NPK fertilization, respectively, on bacterial diversity and community composition. Among these studies, the annual N application rate ranged from 10 to 800 kg ha-1 with a median of 120 kg ha-1. The duration of field trials ranged from 2 to 110 years with a median of 22 years,

Microbial beta-diversity is usually calculated using distance metrics and presented by two/three-dimension ordination plots, such as non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) and principal components analysis (PCA); thus, it is impossible to extract the original data (i.e. distance metrics) used for beta-diversity data calculation from the publications. Unfortunately the number of observations related to fungal and archaeal diversity and community composition were not sufficient for a robust meta-analysis and therefore we only report the bacterial data in this paper.

Both N and NPK inputs were associated with increased soil organic C content, more so in the NPK (Hedge's d = 1.24) than in the N alone treatment (Hedge's d = 0.68) (Fig. 1b and Table S2). Both N and NPK fertilization significantly increased soil available N content (Fig. 1c and Table S2). Nitrogen input alone was associated with decreases in soil microbial biomass C, but NPK fertilization increased microbial biomass C (Fig. 1d and Table S2).

Response of bacterial diversity to fertilization Nitrogen fertilization alone significantly decreased soil bacterial diversity (Hedge's d = -0.87), while NPK fertilization significantly increased bacterial diversity (Hedge's d = 0.65) (Fig. 2 and Fig. S1). Similarly, Hedge's d for soil bacterial richness was -0.36 in N fertilization alone and 0.40 in NPK fertilization (Fig. S2). There was a significant difference in bacterial diversity between N and NPK fertilization (Fig. 2 and Fig. S1). In contrast, absence of significant difference was observed in bacterial diversity between P and NPK fertilization (Fig. 2 and Fig. S1). Due to the large correlation coefficients between the effect size of bacterial richness and bacterial diversity (Fig. S3), we will only present the bacterial diversity data in this paper. With N fertilization alone, the effect size of bacterial diversity was positively correlated with changes in soil pH (r = 0.43, p < 0.01) and total organic C (r = 0.34, p < 0.01) (Fig. 3). With NPK fertilization, the effect size of bacterial diversity was positively correlated with changes in soil total organic C (r = 0.47, p < 0.01) (Fig. 3).

Nitrogen fertilization alone significantly decreased bacterial diversity in upland soils, but increased it in anaerobic soils (e.g., rice paddy soils) (Fig. 4a).

Discussion Response of bacterial diversity to N and NPK fertilization Microbial diversity is a fundamental property of soil ecosystems as microorganisms play critical roles in nutrient cycling, soil structure maintenance, and crop production (Madigan et al., 2008). Although bacterial diversity is critical for the maintenance of soil health and quality (Garbeva et al., 2004), uncertainties still remain across the literature regarding the long-term effect of N fertilization on bacterial diversity, e.g. negative effects (Ling et al., 2017; Niu et al., 2014; Zhou et al., 2015;) and no/positive effects (Ramirez et al., 2010; Wang et al., 2016; Yuan et al., 2013). These results can probably be attributed to the great heterogeneity in soil properties, rate of fertilization, water management regimes, and other biotic and abiotic factors. For instance, in a semi-arid steppe, bacterial diversity was negatively correlated with N application rate (Ling et al., 2017). In contrast, in rice-wheat cropping systems, N fertilization either significantly increased or had no influence on bacterial diversity (Gu et al., 2009; Tian et al., 2015). Our meta-analysis agreed with those studies showing that under N fertilization alone, soils with relatively aerobic condition favor lower bacterial diversity while relatively anaerobic conditions favor higher bacterial diversity (Fig. 4a,c). Although in the majority of upland soils, consistently negative effects of N input on bacterial diversity were observed (Fig. 4), the bacterial diversity was not sensitive to N fertilization in grassland ecosystems (Fig. 4c). It agrees with findings in Leff et al. (2015) that N addition did not strongly alter bacterial diversity based on a

study on 25 globally distributed grassland sites. We attributed it to the large differences in management regime, soil type and plant community between grasslands and croplands. For instance, Jangid et al. (2008) revealed that pasture soils were richer in organic C and N, and bacterial diversity than cropland soils, leading to high resistance of grassland ecosystems to intensive N inputs. Overall, in addition to soil fertility degradation reported before, e.g., soil acidification (Guo et al., 2010), long-term N fertilization can also alter soil biodiversity, and thus potentially affects ecosystem services such as nutrient cycling and crop growth. Although N application rate did not influence bacterial diversity (Fig. 4d), a reduction in bacterial diversity was still associated with very low N input rates, as only a small part of the confidence interval of N input at <100 kg N ha-1 overlapped zero (Fig. 4d). It indicates that a small amount of N input can possibly cause bacterial diversity loss in agro-ecosystems. We should point out that the negative effect of N fertilization on bacterial diversity disappeared when N is applied with P and K (Fig. 2). Therefore, NPK fertilization has the potential to maintain soil biodiversity and long-term sustainability of soil ecosystems. The fertilization-induced variation of soil chemical and biochemical parameters that are associated with changes in bacterial diversity still remains contradictory. Soil pH, organic C, N availability, and other soil properties affect bacterial diversity (Nakatsu et al., 2000; Shen et al., 2013; Van Horn et al., 2014). The significantly positive relationship between bacterial diversity and soil pH changes under N fertilization (Fig. 3) suggests that soil pH decreases presumably led to decreased

diversity. This is consistent with Cassman et al. (2016) showing that lime application significantly increased bacterial diversity via increasing soil pH as lime material does not contain any organic C or N. In general, soil pH is positively correlated with bacterial diversity in arable soils as most bacterial taxa exhibit relatively narrow growth tolerances, especially within the pH range of 4-7 (Lauber et al., 2009; Rousk et al., 2010). Our result is in agreement with the previous hypotheses that stresses how soil acidification caused by N input resulted in the pH of a variety of soils changing towards the acidic range (most soils with long-term N input have pH <7), which can limit the growth of a wide range of bacterial species. The significantly positive relationship between bacterial diversity and total organic C following N input (Fig. 3) indicates that C availability was an important driver of bacterial diversity (Xun et al., 2015). When N was combined with P and K, soil organic C rather than pH was the dominant factor determining bacterial diversity (Fig. 3). Typically NPK input results in a larger crop yield compared to N fertilization alone (Gu et al., 2009; Zhong et al., 2010). The higher crop productivity would increase organic C input into soil ecosystems via carbon exudates released by plant root (Jones et al., 2009), or by residue retention (Saffigna et al., 1989) that could have cascading effects on belowground bacterial communties. When NPK was applied, the soil pH tended to be less acidic (i.e. neutral or alkaline), so the effects of soil pH on bacterial diversity were also likely to be reduced. Correspondingly, soil organic C, a substrate for heterotrophic bacteria, becomes more important than soil pH to affect bacterial diversity. Response of bacterial taxonomic composition to N and NPK fertilization More than 82% and 93% of the previous literatures reported that long-term N and NPK fertilizations significantly changed bacterial community composition (Table S3), consistent with findings in Allison & Martiny (2008). In addition, a significant alteration in bacterial community composition caused by N input was observed in 25 grassland sites, despite large compositional differences across these sites (Leff et al., 2015), strongly supporting that the bacterial community composition was highly responsive to N input across different ecosystems, e.g., croplands and grasslands. Overall, the soil bacterial community composition in most cases is very sensitive to long-term input of nutrients such as N and NPK. With respect to bacterial taxonomic composition, we only analyzed the soil bacterial phyla whose abundances were dominant in soil ecosystems (Fierer et al., 2007) including three bacterial phyla (i.e., Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria and Acidobacteria) that were highly responsive to N input. Acidobacteria, some of which tended to be more fastidious oligotrophic bacteria (Fierer et al., 2007) and were suppressed after N input, while the Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria, some of which are copiotrophic bacteria, exhibited the opposite pattern (Fig. 5). This might conflict with the other expectation that with acidification caused by N fertilization, the relative abundance of Acidobacteria should be increased in soils with a low pH, as

Acidobacteria is often negatively correlated to soil pH (Lauber et al., 2008; Lauber et al., 2009). Surprisingly, the abundance of Acidobacteria decreased even though soil pH was significantly reduced in the treatment of N fertilization alone. Several studies have supported it by showing that N inputs inhibited the growth of oligotrophic bacteria, such as some Acidobacteria (Leff et al., 2015), which have the potential capability to decompose nutrient poor and recalcitrant substrates, but to promote copiotrophic bacterial growth, including some Alproteobacteria and Actinobacteria, due to their fast-growth with lower C use efficiency (Fierer et al., 2012; Ling et al., 2017; Ramirez et al., 2010; Ramirez et al., 2012; Zhou et al., 2015). Our analysis shows that soil organic C significantly increased with N fertilization even though soil pH significantly decreased (Fig. 1a and b). Leff et al. (2015) similarly revealed that N inputs caused a significant increase in the relative abundance of carbohydrate metabolism genes using shot-gun metagenomics. These results imply that organic C availability and N fertilizer are both likely to preferentially support the growth of copiotrophic bacteria (e.g., some Proteobacteria) over oligotrophic bacteria (e.g., some Acidobacteria) due to their preference for living in nutrient-sufficient or nutrient-limited environments, respectively. In addition, the simultaneous increases in the relative abundance of Proteobacteria and soil organic C when NPK was supplied again supported that the indirectly increased organic C via plantation and directly added nutrients (i.e. NPK) were more important than soil pH to determine the abundance of Acidobacteria and Proteobacteria. For Actinobacteria, although N input alone increased its relative abundance, this did not occur with NPK addition (Fig. 5), arguing against the hypothesis that increased C and N availability induced the growth of Actinobacteria. In addition, its abundance had a significant positive correlation with N application rate (Fig. 5c and Table S4), indicating that the growth of Actinobacteria was closely associated with N input, compared to other bacterial phyla. Actinobacteria are gram-positive bacteria with high GC contents in their genomes, and usually have filamentous growth patterns (Ventura et al., 2007). Possible reasons that can explain why Actinobacteria were positively responsive to N input are as follows (but not limited to): some acidophilic aerobic iron-oxidizers can be found in Actinobacteria, which is expected to thrive in soil ecosystems with high N inputs and low soil pH (Madigan et al., 2008); alternatively Actinobacteria contains taxa that are spore-forming and gram positive, some of which are capable of surviving under extreme conditions (Smith et al., 2013). These traits may confer advantages in those systems where soils are undergoing severe acidification. The specific taxa at finer taxonomic resolution levels within the Actinobacteria that responded strongly to long-term N fertilization remains a clear direction for further investigation.

Linkages between bacterial diversity and biomass Soil microbial biomass is a critical component of soils that regulates many processes associated with nutrient cycling (Horwath, 1994). It plays an important role in ecosystem function as an indicator of soil quality. Diverse communities can produce more biomass than less diverse communities predicted by theory because microorganisms can use nutrient resources more efficiently through resource complementarity (Naeem, 2009). Long-term fertilization supplies sufficient nutrient resources for soil microbial community (Allison & Martiny, 2008). Thus, the correlation between the extent of bacterial diversity and the size of the microbial biomass was calculated so as to examine the relationship between diversity and overall microbial abundance in soil following mineral fertilization. Some previous studies showed that microbial diversity losses generally results in soil biomass reduction (Gamfeldt et al., 2015; Peter et al., 2011; Strickland et al., 2009), while others revealed that the biomass was not affected by decreases in microbial diversity (Griffiths et al., 2000; Roger et al., 2016; Singh et al., 2014). Positive relationships between microbial phylogenetic diversity and abundance have been reported (Roger et al., 2016) in aquatic ecosystems, while how the microbial biomass responds to changes in microbial diversity in soil ecosystems still remains unknown. The significantly positive correlations between bacterial diversity and microbial biomass (Fig. 6) showed that microbial biomass under long-term N fertilization in agro-ecosystems was highly sensitive to diversity reduction, even though the nutrient resources were introduced. This result was supported by the

Implications and perspectives Our meta-analysis revealed that long-term N fertilization decreased soil bacterial diversity and changed the abundance of dominant bacterial phyla, particularly in loam-textured upland soils. The negative effect of N fertilization alone on bacterial diversity disappeared when N fertilization was combined with PK. Thus NPK fertilization would be a better choice to maintain soil microbial diversity in agro-ecosystems rather than N fertilization alone, particularly when P and K are also in short supply for sustaining crop productivity. The reduction in microbial biomass linked with decreasing diversity indicated that N input would potentially affect the agro-ecosystem functioning. Nitrogen fertilization increased the relative abundances of Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria, which could be partially explained by copiotrophic life history strategies.
