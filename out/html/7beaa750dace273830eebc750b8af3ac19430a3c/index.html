Transition metal (oxy)hydroxides are promising electrocatalysts for the oxygen evolution reaction.1–3 The properties of these materials evolve dynamically and heterogeneously4 with applied voltage through ion insertion redox reactions, converting inactive materials at open-circuit into active electrocatalysts during operation.5 Thus, the catalytic state is inherently far-from-equilibrium, complicating its direct observation. Here, we establish the link between the oxygen evolution activity and the local operational chemical, physical, and electronic nanoscale structure of single-crystalline β-Co(OH)2 platelet particles through a suite of newly developed correlative operando scanning probe and X-ray microscopy techniques. At pre-catalytic voltages, the particles swell to form an α-CoO2H1.5·0.5H2O-like structure with associated Co2.5+ oxidation state produced through hydroxide intercalation. With increasing voltage to drive oxygen evolution, interlayer water and protons de-intercalate to form contracted β-CoOOH particles with Co3+ species. While these transformations manifest heterogeneously through the bulk of the particles, the electrochemical current is primarily restricted to the particle edge facets. The observed Tafel behavior is correlated to the local concentration of Co3+ at these reactive edge sites, demonstrating the link between bulk ion-insertion and surface catalytic activity.

The oxygen evolution reaction (OER, 4 OH- → O2 + 2 H2O + 4 e-; E0 = 0.401 V vs. SHE; E0’ = 1.229 V vs. RHE) is the anodic half-reaction in electrolytic H2 generation from water. It is acknowledged as the efficiency bottleneck, requiring significant applied overpotential (ηOER = Eappl – EO2/OH- > 0.3 V).3 Improving the OER efficiency is thus crucial to realize a closed-loop clean energy infrastructure based on the generation and storage of hydrogen gas. Achieving this entails developing improved transition metal based electrocatalysts through directly identifying how evolving material properties influence reactivity during operation. Only the Surface Catalyzes the OER Our work utilizes single-crystalline β-Co(OH)2 platelet particles (crystal structure shown in Figure 1a) with ~1-2 μm wide basal plane {0001} facets and ~50-75 nm thick edge {101̅0} facets, shown in scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images in Figures 1b,c.5,6 These particles exhibit two bulk redox transformations with increasing anodic voltage at 𝐸10′ ≈ 1.20 V and 𝐸20′ ≈ 1.55 V vs. RHE, previously assigned to the conversion of Co(OH)2 to CoOOH and CoOOH to CoO2, respectively.5 The OER is observed to initiate concomitantly with the second bulk redox process at E > 1.55 V vs. RHE (Figure 1d). The voltages of these reactions were previously observed to scale linearly with pH (-59 mV/pH vs. SHE) and we use the formal potential, E0’, to describe the reversible voltage on the pH-independent RHE scale.6 Note that all voltages in this work are referenced versus RHE unless otherwise explicitly stated. To investigate the rate dependence of the OER on active site concentration, the OER current (jOER, Figure 1e) and pre-catalytic redox reactions (Qa, Figure 1f) are first investigated as a function of scan rate at sub-monolayer mass loadings on glassy carbon (GC) rotating disk electrodes (RDE). To ensure that residual material transformations do not obscure the relationship between the extent of Co oxidation and OER activity, we cycle the electrodes 3

between 1.0 – 1.75 V starting at 1000 mV s-1 and moving to progressively slower scan rates in each successive cycle. At 1000 mV s-1 each Co atom in the electrode is oxidized on average by Qa ≈ 0.02 e- in the pre-catalytic region, which increases to ~ 0.5 e-/Co atom when the scan rate is decreased 4 orders of magnitude to 0.25 mV s-1. Thus, this regime of reactivity corresponds to transformations that extend throughout the bulk of the particles. Importantly, the total amount of charge passed at a given scan rate scales linearly with mass loading, confirming the absence of limitations in active mass utilization at these electrode loadings.6 In contrast to the extent of bulk Co oxidation, the OER current is not scan rate dependent. We find that jOER at 1.7 V is not statistically different at 0.25 mV s-1 compared to 1000 mV s-1, but does scale approximately linearly with mass loading, as previously described.6 Thus, the OER is already at steady state at 1000 mV s-1. These results preclude catalysis occurring in the interior of the particles, in contrast to previous hypotheses for metal hydroxide electrocatalysts.7 We note that no change in OER activity was observed with Fe-impurities in the electrolyte, suggesting low defect concentrations for our model single crystal particles.8 The Edges Facets are the Active Surfaces We directly map the OER current using Scanning Electrochemical Cell Microscopy (SECCM), at a spatial resolution determined by the diameter of the micropipette tip (dtip = 440 nm), shown schematically in Figure 2a. A detailed description of the technique can be found in recent reviews on the topic.9–11 We employ two modes of SECCM experiments, which offer complementary information on the connection between local OER activity and bulk oxidation. First, in scanning mode, chronoamperometry is performed at 1.87 V while the pipette is raster scanned linearly and continuously across the particles (lateral translation rate = 30 nm s-1). The results, presented in Figures 2c,d, unambiguously demonstrate the particle edge facets dominate the electrochemical reactivity of the system. For the particles shown in the SEM images in Figure 2d, current is observed only when the pipette encompasses the edge of the particles (lower panel), not when it is atop the basal plane (top panel). In hopping mode SECCM, presented in Figure 2b,e,f and Supplementary Movie S1, the micropipette is raster scanned stepwise across the particles (i.e. electrolyte contact with the surface is broken and re-established during each step) and linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) is performed at a scan rate of 1000 mV s-1 at each step. Simultaneous topographical (Figure 2f, top) and voltammetric (Figure 2f, bottom) mapping of the electrochemical response across the particles again reveal the particle edge facets have high electrochemical activity compared to the low activity of the basal planes. The latter show similar currents to the baseline GC support (Figure 2b). Variations in current at the edges of the two isolated particles on the right-hand side of the images in Figure 2f correlate with particle thickness, where thinner particle generate lower currents at a given voltage. Some variation in observed edge activity may also arise from defects at the edge facets, demonstrated by the scan across the edge of a particle fragment in the bottom of Figure 2d.11 These results directly confirm previous hypotheses that basal planes of the CoOxHy system are inactive.5,6,12 The active site for OER is expected to be an oxidized phase of CoOxHy with y/x ≤ 0.5 (generally x = 2).5 Thus, the different activity of these facets can be rationalized by the ion (de)intercalation characteristics of the system, where mobile charge-compensating ions are restricted to the interlayer gallery between the CoO2 slabs. For progressive oxidation of layered 5

β-Co(OH)2, ion (de)intercalation is facilitated at the edge facets where this region is in contact with the electrolyte. In contrast, ion movement in the <0001> direction is restricted in the absence of extended defects in the CoO2 layers, which prevents the basal planes from serving as the reaction sites to initiate the bulk redox transformation reactions. This also explains the lack of activity for interior Co atoms. We also note that oxidized CoOxHy compositions exhibit higher electronic conductivity,13 which may influence the observed current density at the basal planes where the absence of ion (de)intercalation prevents the formation of more conductive CoOxHy compositions. As the particles are oxidized during the second reaction at 𝐸20′ , a mass decrease is observed in EQCM, suggesting interlayer water and additional protons are removed as the particle contracts to form β-CoOOH (observed in post-mortem X-ray diffraction in Extended Data Figure 3). However, interpretation of this region is complicated by the formation of gas bubbles at the electrode surface due to the OER, yielding lower local viscosity at the electrodeelectrolyte interface that increases the resonant frequency and obscures the total mass change.20 The insertion and removal of OH- ions, as evidenced through the EQCM measurements, explain the observed volume changes in EC-AFM. A schematic summarizing the structural changes is shown in Figure 3e. Upon oxidation, OH- intercalates into β-Co(OH)2 to form the expanded α-CoO2H1.5·0.5H2O structure. CoO2 layers can translate significantly during this process due to the reduced van der Waals and H-bonding forces caused by interlayer water. The layer translations show some preferential directionality, clearly seen in Supplementary Movie S2. This effect is well documented in hydroxide systems, where the simultaneous presence of αand β-phases results in turbostratic disordering during oxidation and particle expansion.21–24 As the voltage increases, the increasing Co oxidation state shortens the Co-O bond length causing layer translation and particle contraction to a stabilized β-CoOOH structure free of the interlayer water present in the intermediate state. The distribution in measured oxidation states was investigated through four separate particles (Extended Data Figure 5). We fit a Nernstian ideal solution model to the data to approximate the solid-state redox reactions of the β-Co(OH)2 particles (Extended Data Figures 6 and 7). The model extracts both the number of electrons transferred per redox reaction and the corresponding reversible voltages from XAS, n1 = 0.54 ± 0.04 electrons at 𝐸10′ = 1.14 ± 0.03 V and n2 = 0.46 ± 0.04 electrons at 𝐸20′ = 1.58 ± 0.03 V. The derived reversible voltages show excellent agreement with the redox peaks in the STXM cell (Figure 4d), RDE experiments (Figure 1d), EC-AFM and EQCM results (Figure 3c),6 and the number of electrons transferred during each reaction corresponds well with our EQCM results. Notably, despite the nanoscale spatial resolution of STXM compared to bulk Co K-edge XANES experiments, no regions of Co4+ (nor Principal Component Analysis (PCA)/NMF components contributing >1% to the data variance, Extended Data Figure 5) are observed, contrasting with previous reports on the Co-H2O system. 26–29 While the lack of extended structure and high defect concentration in amorphous systems may allow for the generation of Co4+,28,29 UV-Vis spectroelectrochemical experiments on our β-Co(OH)2 system (Extended Data Figure 8) yield nearly identical results to those reported by Risch et al. on the amorphous “CoCat” system (a full discussion on UV-vis results and assignments of Co4+ in previous work is included in the Supplementary Information).28 Thus, our observations definitively alter the assignments of the observed redox peaks for the CoOxHy system, with 𝐸10′ resulting in an average oxidation state of Co2.54+, not Co3+, and 𝐸20′ resulting in an average oxidation state of Co3+, not Co4+. 9

Having elucidated the evolution of the average oxidation state of CoOxHy, we plot the nanoscale phasemaps in Figure 4e, which show the local Co oxidation state with 50 nm spatial resolution. During both oxidation processes at 𝐸10′ and 𝐸20′ , significant heterogeneity develops at the subparticle level, with preferential oxidation of the interior of the platelets while the edge regions maintain lower oxidation states. This observation is consistent with the morphological heterogeneity observed in the EC-AFM experiments and may reflect chemomechanical effects caused by a turbostratic intergrowth of α-CoO2H1.5·0.5H2O (with interlayer water) and βCo(OH)2 or β-CoOOH.23 We note that the reduced edge features are unlikely to be a result of photoreduction from radiolysis effects due to the high exchange rate of electrolyte in the cell as discussed in the Supplementary Information and presented in Extended Data Figures 9 and 10. Oxygen Evolution is Catalyzed by Co3+ We find the observed Tafel slope is directly correlated to the local Co oxidation state (Co3+ coverage) at the reactive edge sites, as shown in Figure 5. The voltage-dependent phasemaps, edge region XAS, and line scans, all confirm an increase in the Co oxidation state at the edges is associated with an increase in the Tafel slope from 40 mV dec-1 to 120 mV dec-1 (Figure 5e). In addition, the reversible voltage for the conversion to CoOOH of 𝐸20′ = 1.58 V corresponds reasonably well with our previous microkinetic modeling prediction of E0PhaseChange = 1.593 V.5 Based on the observed operando STXM data and our microkinetic modeling work,5 the low coverage of CoOOH at the edge (oxidation state = 2.5+) at 1.55 V corresponds to the high activity Tafel slope of 40 mV dec-1. As the voltage increases, so does the coverage of CoOOH, approaching saturation (Co oxidation state ~3+) at E > 1.75 V and a Tafel slope of 120 mV dec-1. These operando STXM results confirm that Co3+ (β-CoOOH) is the active site (or reactant state of the rate limiting step) for the OER.30 We note that the chemical reduction of the 10

Conclusions and Outlook Correlative operando microscopy reveals how the local operational chemical, physical, and electronic structure of energy conversion materials govern their electrochemical response. In the CoOxHy system, hydroxide ion (de)intercalation reactions couple into surface catalytic activity by controlling the relationship between OER overpotential and voltage-dependent Co3+ active site concentration at the reactive edge facets. These results suggest that the OER activity of layered oxides can be improved through strategies that tune the thermodynamics of bulk ion insertion in addition to surface adsorption energy approaches.

Methods Synthesis of β-Co(OH)2 platelet particles: β-Co(OH)2 platelet particles were synthesized as previously described.6 In short, a 400 mL aqueous solution of 45 mM hexamethylenetetramine (HMT, ≥ 99.0%, SigmaAldrich) was brought to 85 °C with magnetic stirring under N2 atmosphere. 3.5 mmol of CoCl2·6H2O (98%, SigmaAldrich) dissolved in 20 mL ultrapure de-ionized water (“DI water”, 18.2 MΩ cm resistance, MilliQ) was added dropwise to the HMT solution and allowed to reflux under continuous magnetic stirring and nitrogen protection for 5 hours. The stirring was then stopped and the solution was allowed to cool to room temperature naturally under N2 atmosphere. The precipitate was collected through centrifugation and washed with DI water and anhydrous ethanol, in turn, then dried overnight in an 80 °C oven. Each image consists of pixels corresponding to the number of transmitted photons at that energy. The pixel size is determined by the zone-plate dimensions and the step size of the interferometer controlled piezoelectric stage, in this case 45 nm for the zone plate and a 50 nm step size. By taking images at discrete energies spanning an absorption edge, such as the Co LIII-edge used in this study, the stack of images can be 28

Page 29

constructed into an [x, y, IE] matrix, where x and y correspond to the position of the pixel and IE corresponds to the number of photons transmitted in the pixel at a given energy. The stacks are first aligned (i.e. the [x, y, IE] pixels are shifted in x and y) using the aXis2000 software to account for drift of the piezo and x-ray focus over the time required to take the image stack, on the order of 30 minutes. To convert these raw transmission image stacks to absorbance, a cluster of pixels from a region of the image where there is no sample is averaged to obtain a background I0 spectrum for all energies. This process is repeated for each individual image stack collected for the entire set of operando STXM data. The images are then processed according to -ln(I/I0) for each single pixel absorbance spectrum yielding the full spectro-image. To obtain average absorption spectra, the aligned absorbance image stacks are filtered at an energy of non-zero intensity, in this case 780.2 eV corresponding to the isosbestic point in the Co LIII-edge spectra of Co2+ and Co3+. A threshold is set for the absorbance of single pixels at this energy, generally < 0.1 (corresponding to over 90% of the photons transmitted), and the pixels below this threshold were set to zero. This is necessary due to changes in absorbance by the electrolyte across an image caused by the bowing of the SiNx windows but does not significantly influence the analysis of the absorbance by the particles (Extended Data Figure 9). A description of the window bowing is discussed in the Supplementary Information. The remaining pixels are then summed across the image to yield the average image spectrum. Is Co4+ Present during the OER in the CoOxHy System? CoOxHy is an archetypal member of the family of transition metal (oxy)hydroxide electrocatalysts.5,6,12,26,50,51 Tabulated Co-H2O equilibria in alkaline electrolytes (pH > ~8.5) suggests the bulk phase stability to proceed from Co to Co(OH)2 (E0’ = 0.08 V vs. RHE) through the spinel Co3O4 (E0’ = 0.577 V vs. RHE) to the layered CoOOH (E0’ = 1.15 V vs. RHE) and then to the layered CoO2 (E0’ = 1.76 V vs. RHE) structures as the voltage is increased anodically through the OER regime.12,52 However, it has been noted that the thermodynamic data for the higher oxides (specifically CoOOH and CoO2) may not be reliable53 and there are also proposed metastable CoOxHy phases with variable proton content, interlayer water, and/or charge balancing ions.28,29,54,55 Complicating the matter, a number of fundamental studies on CoOxHy OER catalysts (i.e. “Co-Pi”56) use electrodeposited electrodes with low crystallinity as model systems.8,26,28,54,56–58 Because of this, the active structure and Co oxidation state during the OER remains inconclusive.26,27,50,59–63 Still, regardless of the initial phase of the oxide or its crystallinity, the OER is observed at voltages anodic of ~1.55 V vs. RHE for the CoOxHy (oxy)hydroxide system, demonstrating a common active site required to turnover O2.26,30,50,59,64–66 While some recent bulk-sensitive operando experiments using Co K-edge X-ray absorption near edge structure (XANES) and extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) suggest CoOOHy-like molecular motifs with y < 1 and an active state of Co4+ to prevail during the OER28,29,54, other similar experiments find the conversion of Co3O4 to CoOOH with Co3+ to initiate the OER67. Surface sensitive experiments including ambient pressure X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (AP-XPS)63,68 and surface X-ray diffraction measurements64,68–70 have not resolved this debate;

X-ray radiation has previously been shown to photo-reduce particles with high oxidation state due to the generation of high energy electrons. To investigate this effect on our samples, the oxidation state of two particles was measured over repetitive imaging conditions, in which the aggregate dose increased from 3 x 106 to 18 x 106 Gy pixel-1, as shown in Extended Data Figure 10b. Although there is variation in the relative optical density (O.D.) between successive imaging, the measured oxidation state for both particles was not found to decrease over time. At the highest aggregate dosage, there was no observed photoreduction. UV-Vis Spectroelectrochemistry: It is well known that CoOxHy’s are electrochromic materials that change their optical properties from a pink colored brucite type β-Co(OH)2 to a dark brown/black colored β-CoOOH with increasing voltage and Co oxidation state.28,55,92,93 We perform steady state UV-Vis spectroelectrochemistry, shown in Extended Data Figure 8, as a means of comparing our singleparticle results to ensemble average measurements of the CoOxHy system previously reported.28 A thin film of β-Co(OH)2 on indium-tin oxide (ITO) coated glass was charged at increasing constant applied voltages and the optical absorbance spectrum was recorded under steady state conditions (after 2 hours at a given voltage) and plotted normalized by the initial open-circuit βCo(OH)2 absorbance. With increasing voltage there is an increase in the absorbance at a center of gravity of 402 nm followed by a transition to longer wavelength and increased absorbance at 528 nm, suggesting oxidation of the Co atoms and decreasing bandgap. The derivative of the absorbance at 600 nm indicates two redox transitions with half wave potentials of ~1.2 and ~1.55 V vs. RHE which correspond well with the previously observed redox peaks for the particles.
