Abstract: Discovered in 2005, CAACs are among the most nucleophilic (-donating) and also electrophilic (-accepting) stable carbenes known to date. These properties allow them to activate a variety of small molecules and enthalpically strong bonds, to stabilize highly reactive main group and transition metal diamagnetic and paramagnetic species, and to bind strongly to metal centers, which gives rise to very robust catalysts. The most important results published up to 2013 are briefly summarized, while the majority of this review focuses on findings reported within the last three years.

1. Introduction Thirty years ago, when the first singlet carbenes were isolated,[1,2] they were regarded as laboratory curiosities. Nowadays, stable carbenes are recognized to be among the most powerful tools in organic, inorganic and organometallic chemistry, and they have even found medicinal and material science applications.[3] Many different types of singlet carbenes have been isolated I-XXXVIII[4-38] (Figure 1). All of them, except cyclopropenylidenes XXXIV[34] and mesoionic carbenes XXXVI[36] feature either a phosphorus or a nitrogen directly bonded to the carbene center. For many years, imidazolin-2-ylidenes XII,[13] imidazol-2-ylidenes XIII,[2] and 1,2,4triazol-5-ylidenes XVI,[16] the so-called N-heterocyclic carbenes (NHCs), were by far the most studied.[39] More recently, another type of carbene, namely cyclic (alkyl)(amino)carbenes (CAACs) have attracted considerable attention, and have already been the subject of several review articles.[40,41] Herein, in each section, we first briefly discuss the most important results published from their discovery in 2005[42] up to 2013, while the majority of this review is devoted to findings published within the last three years. 2. Synthesis of CAACs So far, CAACs have always been prepared by deprotonation of the corresponding cyclic iminium salts (Scheme 1). These salts were initially synthesized by reacting readily available aza-allyl anions with epoxides, followed by addition of triflic anhydride. [42] Then, we developed the hydro-iminiumation route,[43] which involves the addition of HCl to readily available alkenyl aldimines, followed by heating; this straightforward protocol allows for kilogram-scale syntheses of CAAC precursors. For both routes, a variety of R1 and R2 substituents are compatible, but only aryl groups are tolerated on the nitrogen, as N-alkyl-substituted aza-allyl anions react via the nitrogen center. Limitations also exist for the deprotonation of the cyclic iminium salts. Due to the weak acidity of the aldiminium proton, a quaternary carbon is required at the -position of nitrogen, otherwise deprotonation leads to azomethine-ylides.[44] Similarly, deprotonation of the aryl substituent on nitrogen can occur, although the carbene is presumably the kinetic product. Indeed, deprotonation of a cyclic iminium salt bearing a 2,6-diethylphenyl group at nitrogen does not allow for the isolation of the free carbene. However, when the same reaction is performed in the presence of a metal fragment, the corresponding CAAC metal complexes are formed. Recently, several methods have been developed to assess the accepting properties of ligands, and when combined with the TEP value, they allow for an estimate of the -donation. One of these methods is based on the 31P NMR chemical shift of readily available phenylphosphinidene-carbene adducts (See Section 6, Scheme 19).[56] Two extreme canonical forms have to be considered. Resonance form A features a genuine P=C -bond, resulting from full -back donation, and accordingly displays a low field 31P NMR chemical shift (Figure 3). By contrast, in form B the two electron lone pairs remain at phosphorus giving rise to a signal in the high field region of the spectrum. Within the set of cyclic carbenes that have been investigated, it can be concluded that the -accepting ability of CAACs is among the strongest, and is only surpassed by diamidocarbenes XVIII and XXIV. 4. CAACs in group-1 and -2 element chemistry A handful of group-1 and -2 CAAC complexes have been isolated.[61] Even beryllium has been explored, which has been described by Dutton and Frenking as “the least investigated chemical element of the first octal row of the periodic table except inert neon.” [62] Braunschweig et al.[63] have shown that the reduction of a (CAAC)BeCl2 complex in the presence of a CAAC afforded the (CAAC)2Be adduct 1, in which the beryllium atom is in the formal zero oxidation state (Scheme 5). This is the first example of a neutral compound containing a zero-valent s-block metal. Interestingly, computational studies predicted that NHCs were potentially the best ligands to stabilize a Be(0) as the tricoordinated derivative (NHC)3Be.[64] However, despite several attempts, all complexes that has been prepared feature a Be in the oxidation state +2.[65] The superiority of CAACs to access Be(0) complexes is due to their stronger -accepting properties. Note that theoretical studies predict that Mg(0) complexes are synthetically unviable[64a] with classical ligands (CO, PR3, NHC); however it remains to be proven that CAACs cannot do it.

CAACs in group-13 element chemistry The chemistry of group-13 elements is dominated by compounds in which the element adopts the +3 oxidation state and act as a Lewis acid. To compensate for its intrinsic electron-deficiency, boron often participates in multicenter bonds, and numerous compounds involving hypervalent boron centers are known. The first uses of CAACs in boron chemistry can appear paradoxical since they consisted of preparing nucleophilic organoborons,[66] including tricoordinated boron anions,[67] and low-valent boron derivatives. Indeed, in 2011, we reported the preparation of the first stable neutral, nucleophilic, tricoordinate boron derivative 2[66,68] (Scheme 6). This compound, which is isoelectronic with amines and phosphines, was prepared by reduction of a CAACBBr3 adduct in the presence of a CAAC. 6. CAACs in group-14 element chemistry 6.1. Stabilization of carbon radicals As early as 1997, Fukuzumi et al.[86] reported the first example of a carbenestabilized carbon radical (Scheme 11). They demonstrated that the electrochemical oxidation of an enolate derived from a thiazolylidene produced the corresponding neutral radical 23, which is persistent for several hours at room temperature. Inspired by this work, in 2013 we prepared, isolated and crystallographically characterized the oxyallyl radical cation 24[87] based on an anti-Bredt NHC.[22] Since, prior to this work, oxyallyl radicals were only observed in inert matrices at low temperatures or in the gas phase,[88] we concluded that a variety of carbene stabilized organic radicals should be isolable.[89] However, in 2004 Clyburne et al.[90] showed that the reduction of protonated imidazol-2-ylidenes is irreversible and produces the free carbene and H2, indicating that the corresponding neutral radical 25 is highly reactive. Thus when comparing the electronic properties of thiazolylidenes, anti-Bredt NHCs, and imidazol-2-ylidenes, the logical conclusion is that -accepting carbenes are the most appropriate to stabilize radicals; therefore CAACs are good candidates. CAACs for the activation of small molecules and enthalpically strong bonds Based on the pioneering studies by Breslow on the thiazolium catalyzed benzoin condensation,[186] carbenes such as diaminocarbenes, and more specifically 1,2,4triazol-5-ylidenes XVI developed by Enders[16] have proven to be excellent organocatalysts.[187] In contrast, the high basicity of CAACs makes them poor leaving groups, precluding their use as organocatalysts.[188] However, because of their stronger electrophilicity and smaller singlet−triplet gap, CAACs have been shown to activate a variety of small molecules and enthalpically strong bonds under mild conditions[41e,189,190] (Scheme 36). Conclusion and Perspectives Starting with the work of Dumas[197] in 1835, and for most of the 20th century, scientists struggled to isolate carbenes under ambient conditions. The lessons learned from the many great pioneers: Curtius,[198] Staudinger,[199] Meerwein,[200] Doering,[201] Breslow,[186] and Wanzlick,[202] to cite a few, ultimately allowed for the preparation of stable carbenes.[1,2] Nowadays, many carbenoid species are readily available and belong to the day-to-day armamentarium of chemists. One of the most recent developments can appear paradoxical since it consists of using carbenes to stabilize highly reactive species. For this task, CAACs appear to be very effective as demonstrated by the isolation of a variety of allegedly unstable organic, main group and transition metal diamagnetic and paramagnetic species. This is due to their lowlying LUMO and small singlet−triplet gap. These electronic properties also allow free CAACs to activate small molecules and enthalpically strong bonds, and therefore to imitate, to some extent, transition metals. To fully compete with the latter, it

39 This article is protected by copyright. All rights reserved

Page 41

remains to be seen if they can operate as catalysts. However, they shine when used in conjunction with metals, to which they bind strongly, giving rise to very robust catalysts capable of working under very harsh conditions. The (CAAC)Au(I)promoted hydroamination reactions with ammonia and hydrazine, and the (CAAC)Ru(II)-catalyzed ethenolysis of the seed-oil derivative methyl oleate,[203] well-illustrate the high impact of CAACs in homogeneous catalysis. One recent development is the use of CAACs to isolate transition metal complexes that were postulated to be key catalytic intermediates, but believed to be only short-lived.[204] The availability of these reactive species allows for a better understanding of known catalytic reactions but, more importantly, paves the way for the rational design of new ones. Looking ahead, CAACs should find practical applications beyond academia. Some premises can be found in recent publications. For example, the group of Radius[205] has prepared a (CAAC)Co(CO)2(NO) complex as a precursor for thin-film deposition, which is a rapidly evolving area in integrated circuit manufacturing. Bochmann et al.[206] have reported that (CAAC)CuX complexes exhibit high photoluminescence, and could be used in LED devices. We recently initiated collaborative projects aiming to explore the use of (CAAC)Au complexes for their antitumor activities on various cancer cell lines, a study whose preliminary results are extremely encouraging. The last few years have witnessed the development of facile and cheap synthetic methods for the preparation of CAACs and their corresponding late transition metal complexes. Combined with the commercial availability of some CAAC precursors, and with the growing number of research groups utilizing CAACs, it is safe to predict that applications in and around the fields of chemistry will only continue to increase.
