ABSTRACT

Atomic level understanding of the active sites and transformation mechanisms under realistic working conditions are pre-requisites for rational design of high performance photocatalysts. Here, by using correlated scanning fluorescence x-ray microscopy and environmental transmission electron microscopy at atmospheric pressure, in operando, we directly observe that the (110) facet of a single Cu2O photocatalyst particle is photocatalytically active for CO 2 reduction to methanol while the (100) facet is inert. The oxidation state of the active sites changes from Cu(I) towards Cu(II) due to CO2 and H2O coadsorption and changes back to Cu(I) after CO2 conversion under visible light illumination. The Cu2O photocatalyst oxidizes water as it reduces CO2. Concomitantly, the crystal lattice expands due to CO2 adsorption then reverts after CO2 conversion. The internal quantum yield for unassisted wireless photocatalytic reduction of CO2 to methanol using Cu2O crystals is ~ 72%.

Efficient catalytic reduction of CO2 into liquid fuels can play an integral role in potentially mitigating and resolving the challenge of climate change. Previous studies have focused on CO 2 reduction by hydrogenation at elevated temperatures1–4 although use of hydrogen raises cost and safety issues. Recently, electroreduction of CO 2 has aroused considerable interest5–10. However, this process requires a large overpotential and electrical energy input8,11, and the problem of the decomposition of generated chemicals at low overpotential by electroreduction12. Additionally, use of electricity as a secondary form of energy is inefficient due to losses associated with conversion from primary sources as well as storage and transmission. Photoreduction of CO2 using sunlight offers an efficient way directly store solar energy in the form of chemical fuels. Of particular interest is its conversion directly into liquid fuels such as methanol13. In theory, photovoltaic-electrolysis approaches have lower “solar to fuel” efficiency than photocatalysis due to the energy losses associated with the additional conversion steps from solar to electricity and subsequent conversion to fuels. Photocatalysis is a promising method of converting solar energy to fuel without secondary energy losses and that can potentially be scaled up for large scale applications14. Unfortunately, despite intensive efforts since 197815, photocatalysts developed for CO2 reduction suffer from poor stability due to use of organics16,17, low efficiency in the visible part of the solar spectrum18, high cost as a result of the use of low-abundance elements 19, and low multielectron transfer necessary for chemical production20. Cu compounds are promising photocatalysts with good multielectron transfer properties 21 because of their loosely bonded d electrons. Although in their relative infancy22, such catalysts show great potential for facilitating CO2 activation and conversion to CO7,8,23,24 or ethylene25–27 by electroreduction, as well as for water splitting28,29, solar cells30 and Li ion batteries31. Furthermore, Cu2O is an inexpensive material based on relatively abundant elements. Cu2O is a direct-gap semiconductor with a bandgap of 2.1 eV enabling the absorption of visible light that constitutes most of the solar spectrum28. Importantly, it shows intrinsic p-type conductivity due to the presence of negative-charged Cu vacancies and possesses one of the lowest electron affinities of the elements, enabling Cu2O as a good candidate for reduction of one of the most resilient compounds, namely CO 232–35. All these studies focused on traditional statistic ex situ ensemble bulk measurements. To date, the best reported solar-to-fuel efficiency for unassisted wireless photocatalytic CO2 reduction is less than 1%36, compared to claimed 64% electrical to chemical 2

fuel efficiency in PEC devices37. In order to design high efficient one step CO2 reduction using unassisted wireless photocatalysis in neutral pH, we need to design photocatalyst by active sites. Determining the active sites is necessary to understand the working mechanisms of photocatalysts and design new materials for CO2 reduction. However, identification of the active sites by traditional ex situ ensemble methods is difficult or even impossible because the physiochemical properties of the active sites of photocatalysts are heterogeneous as well as highly sensitive to their local environment. Here, we report the facet specific active sites in Cu2O capable of binding and reducing CO2. This is unraveled using operando multimodal correlated characterization of single particles, ex situ ensemble measurements, and density functional theory calculations. Through this approach, we exploit our understanding of the facet selective active sites to design nanoparticles with improved activity. We report a photocatalytic conversion of CO2 with liquid fuel methanol as the sole product of photoreduction with internal quantum yield is ~70% and 10% solar to fuel efficiency with both O2 and H2O2 as the major oxidation products.

Illumination of faceted Cu2O nanoparticles in the presence of CO2 and water showed formation of new molecular product. The products of this photocatalytic reaction (Fig. 1f black line, see Methods) showed the same retention time as methanol analytical standard (Fig. 1f red line) by gas chromatography (GC) analysis, while no products (Fig. 1f blue line) were observed in absence of purified Cu2O photocatalyst. This implied that presence of the Cu2O particles was necessary for the photocatalytic reaction and the product was methanol. This result was further confirmed by the gas chromatography mass spectrometry (GC/MS) analysis. The products from the photocatalytic reaction with Cu2O particles showed peaks with mass to charge ratios of 32, 31, 30, 29, 28 and 15 (Fig. 1g inset), which correspond to [CH3OH•]+, [CH3O]+, [CH2O]+, [CHO]+, [CO]+, and [CH3]+, the same species as the ones obtained in a standard methanol mass spectrum (Supplementary Fig. 3). The amount of methanol produced was determined by comparing the integrated area of the GC peaks with that of known amounts of standard pure methanol. The methanol production and its dependence on irradiation time (Fig. 1g) showed an internal quantum yield of 72% (see Methods, Supplementary Fig. 4), which is comparable to the recent reported efficiency of 50% and 63% obtained using electroreduction 10,38. The solar-to-fuel efficiency was measured to be 10% (see Methods), which was smaller than theoretical prediction maximum of 35.2%39. The plateau in the time conversion curve (Fig. 1g) indicated the methanol produced by photoexcited electrons reached a steady state with the same formation and consumption rate beyond 6 min of irradiation time. Concomitantly, we found that the system produced oxygen in synchrony with periodic illumination (Fig. 1h, see Methods), suggesting that this new material can couple H2O oxidation with CO2 reduction, functioning as an artificial leaf9, albeit in sub-stoichiometric ratio (see Methods, Supplementary Fig. 5). Operando multimodal characterization of single particles To understand this high efficiency and the influence of the local crystal structure on the photoreduction of CO2 under real working conditions, and to identify the active sites that participated in the photocatalytic reaction, we studied single Cu2O particles by both environmental transmission electron microscopy (ETEM) and scanning fluorescence x-ray microscopy (SFXM) in a novel nanoreactor 44,45 while exposed to a mixture of gaseous CO2, H2O, and light (see Methods and Supplementary Fig. 9) as shown in Fig. 2a. For example, we first identified particle I (Fig. 2d), with a truncated hexagonal shape (indicated by red circle in Fig. 2c), by TEM. A corresponding single particle electron diffraction (SPED) measurement indicated the edges of particle I were {220} planes in the TEM view (Supplementary Fig. 10). Then, we transferred the nanoreactor containing the Cu2O particles from the ETEM to the SFXM platform. Low-resolution scans (Fig. 2e) using the Cu and Ni K fluorescence signals from the Cu2O particles and grid bars, respectively, enabled us to locate the same particle I (indicated by the white circle in Fig. 2e) for imaging and nano-spectroscopic study by SFXM. In the high resolution Cu K fluorescence image in Fig. 2f we observe particle I as a truncated square rather than a truncated hexagon. This was due to the relative projected views of the particle using the ETEM and SFXM platforms, which differed by nearly 60°, and can be understood by considering particle I to be a cube resting on one of its truncated corners, a (111) facet, on the supporting Si3N4 membrane (Fig. 2b). With the TEM view directions as indicated by the red arrow, the particle appeared as a corner-truncated hexagon (Fig. 2b, Fig. 2d), while it presented as a corner-truncated square when viewed with x-rays (Fig. 2b, Fig. 2f). Hence, the corner of particle I was a (110) facet while edges are exposed as a (100) facet along the x-ray view direction (Fig. 2b, Fig. 2f). Particle I was checked again by TEM and SPED after the x-ray measurements, which showed that the location and structure of particle I were preserved (Supplementary Fig. 11). This finding rules out the possibility that the particle moved or was significantly damaged structurally by the x-ray beam. While we characterized the same Cu2O particles by both ETEM and SFXM, it should be noted that in TEM, the particle could not be tilted to the same view 6

orientation as for the SFXM measurements. We targeted specific facets of particle I by directing the nano-focused x-ray beam parallel to them whilst scanning the incident x-ray energy (Supplementary Fig. 9), enabling us to obtain facet-dependent spectroscopic information of the Cu active sites. The spectroscopic signal, which resulted from a columnar projection of the x-ray beam through the plane of the facet, contains information about local chemical state of the facet as well as the nearby bulk material beneath it. The observed spectra likely reflect an admixture of states resulting from both the surface and interior chemistry. X-ray fluorescence spectra (Fig. 3a) taken near the Cu K-edge on the (110) facet of Cu2O particle I in its initial pristine state revealed a white-line peak at 8981.0 eV, indicating a Cu(I) oxidation state associated with this facet, in agreement with reference x-ray absorption spectra (XAS) taken from pure Cu2O (Supplementary Fig. 12). By contrast, the white-line peak observed on the (100) facet was at 8981.5 eV, indicating the presence of both Cu(I) and Cu(II) oxidation states (Fig. 3a). When measuring spectra on the (110) facet before and after flushing CO2/H2O through the nanoreactor for a few min, the peak shifted between 1.0 and 1.5 eV toward higher energy (Fig. 3b, and Supplementary Fig. 13), indicating the oxidation state of the Cu changed from oxidation state 1+ toward 2+ due to the coadsorption of CO2/H2O. In addition, consecutive spectra measured at intervals of 10 min at another position on the (110) facet of particle I showed a gradual shift of the peak by ~1 eV until reaching saturation in about 1 h (Supplementary Fig. 13). These results suggest that CO2/H2O coadsorbed on the (110) facet reduced the electron density of the exposed Cu atoms, causing the oxidation state to shift from Cu(I) toward Cu(II). Furthermore, when the particle was excited with a green laser (532 nm) while maintaining constant CO2/H2O gas flow, the peak reverted back for ~1 eV toward lower energy (Fig. 3b), indicating that the majority of the Cu oxidation states shifted back to Cu(I). In comparison, the oxidation states of the Cu atoms on the (100) facet did not change upon CO2/H2O adsorption in either the dark or the illuminated states (Fig. 3c), indicating the (100) facet was photocatalytic inactive. In successive measurements on several other, similar Cu2O particles we reproducibly observed similar spectroscopic shifts on (110) facets followed by reversion upon illumination, and statistically insignificant shifts on the (100) facets. We also carried out density functional theory (DFT) calculations to understand the mechanism of CO2 and H2O adsorption on Cu2O on different facets (Fig. 4a and 4b). Ensemble characterization at statistical level In addition to the single particle measurements, we characterized the properties of ensembles of Cu 2O particles by high resolution x-ray powder diffraction (HRXRD) and EPR to obtain complementary information about the photocatalytically active sites. As can be seen from operando high energy x-ray diffraction measurements (Supplementary Fig. 15) there was no noticeable structural change (no line broadening, disappearance/appearance of additional lines or decrease of line intensity) in the Cu2O particles over 8 h during the photocatalytic reaction. The stability also confirmed by the ex situ x-ray photoemission spectroscopy since strong Cu 2p satellite peaks, characteristic of oxidized CuO samples, were not observed in both pristine and reacted Cu2O catalyst (Supplementary Fig. 16). This stability may be due to the mild reaction of Cu2O in moisture air (H2O/CO2) rather than aqueous solution, in which Cu2O is known to be instable without surface coating47,48. Fine structural changes in the Cu2O particles were probed ex situ using HRXRD (Fig. 5a). In the HRXRD data in Figs. 5b and 5c we see that CO2/H2O coadsorption under dark conditions increased the crystal lattice constant, consistent with our spectroscopic observation (Fig. 3b) that the CO2 withdrew the electron density from the Cu active sites, leading to the oxidation state change from Cu(I) to Cu(II). Consistent with these findings, we found substantial charge transfer at the coadsorption sites of CO2 and H2O at the Cu-binding site of (110) surface (Fig. 5f) based on DFT calculations. 9

Relative to bulk crystalline Cu2O with Cu-O bond lengths of 1.86 Å, the Cu-O bonds in the vicinity of the CO2 and H2O coadsorption site were found to be about 1.92-1.95 Å by DFT calculations, very similar to the DFT computation of 1.95 Å in bulk crystalline CuO. Interestingly, after optical illumination (blue line in Figs. 5b and 5c), the lattice constant of pristine Cu2O was recovered (black line in Figs. 5b and 5c). The above results suggest that, upon coadsorption of CO2/H2O on Cu2O particles, intermolecular electron transfer occurs between the Cu active sites at the (110) surfaces and CO 2 molecules. This redistribution of electron density results in partial reduction of adsorbed CO2/H2O molecules lowering the energy barrier for their complete reduction. On the other hand, the electron redistribution reduces the electron density of (110) surface. This in turn facilitates localization of photogenerated electrons on these sites, enhancing the probability of their interaction with adsorbed molecules. Illumination of CO2/H2O coadsorbed on the (110) facet, therefore, leads to efficient electron transfer and removal of the bonding between CO2 molecules and active sites, restoring the electron density of the pristine Cu2O. Coadsorption of CO2/H2O by the particle is consistent with the lattice constant changes seen by x-ray diffraction (Figs. 5b, 5c, and Supplementary Fig. 17), which suggest volumetric expansion, and possible CO2 induced lattice strain. Similar behavior has been observed in Au nanoparticles undergoing CO or thiol gas adsorption49,50. The complementary role of the adsorbed water to that of CO 2 reduction gives strong corroboration of the photocatalysis process. Besides providing protons for the photocatalytic reaction, HRXRD study of water adsorption on Cu2O particles indicate that the H2O molecule donates electrons and pushes the electron density toward the Cu 2O surface (Supplementary Fig. 18). This result showcases the collaborative roles of the CO2 and H2O molecules while interacting with the active sites of the Cu2O particles. The oxidation states change (Fig. 3b) indicates the dominating effect of CO2 over H2O during the experiment. To investigate binding and electron transfer between Cu active sites and CO 2 molecules, we made EPR measurements on pristine Cu2O particles, exposed to CO2/H2O in dark and illuminated conditions. The pristine particles (Fig. 5d black lines) showed the existence of Cu(II) defects with unpaired electrons in 3d orbitals consistent with the p-doping of Cu2O nanoparticles. The spectral shape suggests the loosely packed pristine Cu 2O powder aligned in the magnetic field (Fig. 5d black lines). Conclusions To summarize the photoreduction process, the electron density of the Cu active sites on (110) facet shifted from Cu(I) to Cu(II) upon coadsorption of CO2/H2O. Under 532 nm illumination, the Cu oxidation state changed from Cu(II) back to Cu(I), which indicates bond breakage between the CO2 and Cu active sites. In addition, the crystal lattice constant increased consistent with withdrawal of the electron density away from the (110) facets upon CO2 adsorption, then shifted back upon illumination, indicating a volumetric response (lattice expansion and contraction) of the Cu2O with photocatalytic stimulation. CO2 reduction was not with a cost of irreversible Cu2O oxidation. Instead, CO2 was reduced by accepting photogenerated electrons while the H2O was oxidized by accepting photogenerated holes, which was the major photocatalytic reaction. The disproportionation reaction of Cu2O is part of the photocatalytic process but the yield was seven orders of magnitude lower than the major photocatalytic reaction, which was quantified by EPR. The oxidation state of the (100) facet remained unchanged upon CO2 adsorption under both dark and illumination conditions, indicating the (100) facet is photocatalytically inert. Our materials design strategy, which integrated x-ray and electron microscopy, bulk x-ray diffraction, and EPR methods with DFT calculations, enabled us to correlate electronic structure with atomic structure, both at the single particle and ensemble levels. We anticipate that this facet-dependent single-particle approach can be extended to design other catalytic materials displaying site-specific behavior , for example, two-dimensional nanoplatelets/thin film of Cu2O with large-area (110) surfaces. We further expect that Cu2O, with its site-specific photocatalytic reduction of CO2, small electron affinity and large reduction potential, and a band structure optimized for charge pair formation and adsorption of solar radiation, will play a significant role in a new class of materials offering a path toward CO2 utilization as well as efficient conversion of solar energy into chemical liquid fuels. This generic method combined with synthetic strategies provides new insights for novel materials design strategy and opens a door to study operando light matter interactions in environments, which is not accessible by traditional methods.

Synthesis of Cu2O photocatalyst particles

We synthesized two types of Cu2O samples, one containing a mix of cubic and octahedral nanocrystals (Supplementary Fig. 2a) and the other primarily composed of cubic nanocrystals (Supplementary Fig. 2b) using standard colloidal synthesis methods59.

To synthesize the cubic/octahedral Cu2O sample, deionized water (88.2 ml), Cu(CH3COO)2 aqueous solution (5 ml, 0.1 M), and sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS, 0.87 g) were successively added into a three-neck flask. The three-neck flask was placed in a water bath at 60ºC with vigorous magnetic stirring. After complete dissolution of the SDS powder in 1 h, NaOH solution (1.8 ml, 1.0 M), and D-(+)-glucose aqueous solution (5 ml, 0.1 M) were successively quickly injected into the flask. The total volume of solution was 100 ml. The flask was kept in a water bath at 60ºC after the injection under vigorous magnetic stirring for 1 h. The solution color gradually changed from blue to green, yellow, orange, and finally turned to brick-red (Supplementary Fig. 1). After the reaction, the precipitate was separated from the solution by centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 5 min, and washed several times with deionized water/ethanol solution.

Synthesis of the cubic Cu2O sample was performed by dissolving CuCl2•2H2O (0.171 g) into aqueous solution (100 ml). NaOH aqueous solution (10 ml, 2.0 M) was then added drop-wise into the above transparent light green solution. After vigorous magnetic stirring for 0.5 h, ascorbic acid solution (10 ml, 0.6 M) was added into the above solution. The color of the liquid changed from black to turbid red gradually. The mixture was aged for 3 h. All procedures were carried out under constant vigorous magnetic stirring and heated at 55ºC. The resulting precipitate was separated from solution by centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 5 min and washed several times with deionized water and ethanol. All the particles have been washed and purified to remove ligands after the synthesis. Both samples were dried inside a N2 gas flow glove box under room temperature for 48 h then stored inside the N2 gas flow glove box to avoid any contamination from the organics for later characterization. All chemicals were purchased from Sigma Aldrich.

Operando multimodal single particle microscopy and nanospectroscopy We used a custom gas-flow nanoreactor (Hummingbird Scientific) to examine single Cu2O particles under working conditions by in situ environmental transmission electron microscopy (ETEM) and operando scanning fluorescence x-ray microscopy (SFXM). The Cu2O particles were sandwiched inside the nanoreactor between 50 nm Si3N4 membranes through which CO2 and H2O gas was flowed at atmospheric pressure (Fig. 2a). The optical transparency of the Si 3N4 membranes facilitated exciting the photocatalyst with laser whilst probing the structure and chemical state of the Cu 2O particles (Fig. 2a, and Supplementary Fig. 9). This enabled us to study the same photocatalytic particle under working conditions by both in situ ETEM and operando SFXM (Supplementary Figs 6, 12). Nickel grid bars on the bottom membrane served as fiducial marks to identify the same particle in the nanoreactor with both techniques (Fig. 2c).

Cu2O particles were dispersed in ethanol and drop-casted onto the bottom Si chip with a Si3N4 membrane and nickel grid bars. The bottom Si chip was assembled with a top Si chip, which also has a Si3N4 membrane, and separated by spacers. Samples were maintained in the nanoreactor under stable environmental conditions during transfer between the ETEM and SFXM platforms. For the in situ ETEM and operando SFXM measurements, CO2 gas (Airgas, Inc.) and H2O vapor gas (500 ppm mol percentage balance with N2, Airgas, Inc. 500 mol H2O molecules per 1 million moles of N2 molecules.) was mixed and delivered to the nanoreactor at 0.05 sccm using a gas flow control box (Hummingbird Scientific). The CO2 to H2O (gas) pressure ratio was about 1:1. ETEM imaging and EELS were performed on a JEOL 2100F operated at accelerating voltage of 200 kV and equipped with a Gatan imaging filter system with an energy dispersion of 0.05 eV/pixel at the Center for Nanoscale Materials (CNM) at Argonne National Laboratory. EELS spectra were recorded in energy-filter TEM mode with an energy resolution of 0.7 eV, a collimated beam, and 1 nm probe size. Exposure of the sample to the electron beam was minimized to avoid damage. Gas flow was started 30 min with closed beam valve before taking EELS spectra with the gas mixture. Density functional theory calculations To study the stability of both the pristine surfaces and adsorption of CO 2 and H2O on surfaces of the Cu2O particles, we performed density functional theory (DFT) calculations with plane wave basis sets as implemented in the VASP code67,68. All the calculations were spin-polarized and carried out using the gradient corrected exchange-correlation functional of Perdew, Burke and Ernzerhof (PBE)69 under the projector augmented wave (PAW) method, with plane wave basis set up for a kinetic energy cutoff of 500 eV. The PAW method70 was used to represent the interaction between the core and valence electrons, and the Kohn-Sham valence states (i.e. 1s for H, 2s 2p for C and O, 3d 4s for Cu) are expanded in plane wave basis sets. For both the Cu2O bulk crystal and surfaces calculations, the GGA+U calculations which the Hubbard U value of Cu atoms was chosen to be U = 6.0 eV according to the reported Cu2O compounds in the literature71. For the geometry optimization, the convergence criterion of the total energy was set to be within 1x10-5 eV for the K-point integration, and all the atoms and geometries were optimized until the residual forces became less than 1x10-2 eV/Å. For the Cu2O bulk crystal, the calculation was based on a mesh of 8x8x8 in K-point grid. For both the crystalline Cu2O surfaces (i.e. (110) and (100) surfaces) and adsorption calculations, a K-point grid of 2x2x1 were used. For the surface slab calculations, we adopted a slab/vacuum geometry composed of repeating slabs and vacuum layers. In the supercell of surface slab calculations, the vacuum thickness of ~18 Å and thickness of the surface slab was ~22 Å over which the bottom three layers were fixed in the crystalline bulk position during the geometry optimization. Calculated conformations of linear and bent CO2 (i.e. activated CO2)72 on Cu termination of Cu2O (110) with the coadsorption of H2O are shown in supplementary Fig. 14.
