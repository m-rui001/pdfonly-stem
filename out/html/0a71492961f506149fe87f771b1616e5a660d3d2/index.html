Abstract: Long-term device stability is the most pressing issue impeding perovskite solar cell commercialisation, given the achieved 22.7% efficiency. The perovskite absorber material itself has been heavily scrutinised for being prone to degradation by water, oxygen, and UV-light. Most reports, to date, characterise device stability in the absence of these extrinsic factors. Here we show that, even under the combined stresses of light (including UV-light), oxygen, and moisture, perovskite solar cells can retain 94% of peak efficiency despite 1000 hrs of continual unencapsulated operation in ambient air conditions (relative humidity of 10-20%). Each interface and contact layer throughout the device stack plays an important role in overall stability which, when appropriately modified, yields devices where both the initial rapid decay (often termed burnin) and the gradual slower decay are suppressed. This extensively modified device architecture and the understanding developed will lead toward durable long-term device performance.

Introduction Solution-processed organic-inorganic halide perovskite semiconductors of the chemical formula ABX3 have been rediscovered1,2 for their remarkable optoelectronic properties,3 most notably the ability to produce a large photopotential even with substantial structural defects. The power conversion efficiency (PCE) of perovskite solar cells is now on par with that of commercial photovoltaic modules,4 but long-term stability remains a critical hurdle for commercialization.5,6 Significant attention has been brought toward improving the stability of devices,6–10 but the combined effects of moisture, oxygen, and light remain problematic. Key factors in understanding the fundamental degradation mechanisms of the various perovskite active layers (PALs) are now being discovered. In the case of methylammonium (CH3NH3+, MA) lead triiodide, the thermal degradation,11–13 reactions with oxygen, atmospheric water,14–16 and light- and radiation-induced instability17,18 have been extensively studied. Formamidinium (CH(NH2)2+, FA) lead triiodide and caesium lead triiodide, while more compositionally stable than MAPbI3, have both a photoactive perovskite phase and a wider band gap, undesired hexagonal or orthorhombic phase, respectively.19,20 While phase stabilization of CsPbI3 has been achieved through nanostructuring,21 the highest efficiency single junction solar cells benefit from complexed A- and X-site compositions of FAPbI3 [e.g., FAxMAyCs1-x-yPb(I1-zBrz)3, where x is large and z is small] due to the improved compositional stability compared to MAPbI3 and improved phase stability compared to FAPbI3 and CsPbI3.8,19,22 Improved PAL stability is integral to improved device reliability,23 yet further gains are necessary and can be achieved within the device stack as a whole. Previous studies have indicated the electron transport and hole transport materials (ETM/HTM, serving as charge-selective extraction layers) and the metal electrodes used in the devices are critical to device stability,6,24–26 making it critical to address all of these components in concert to understand their relative importance toward degradation and mitigate the prominent interface-specific degradation mechanisms. Operational stability and burn-in degradation In light of the improved hydrophobicity of EH44 relative to LiTFSI-doped spiroOMeTAD,30 we anticipate improved stability of unencapsulated devices in the presence of atmospheric moisture due to a reduction in the kinetics of moisture-induced degradation mechanisms. To probe the operational stability, ISOS-L-1 testing conditions were used.33 The ISOS protocols were developed by a broad swath of the organic photovoltaic community and represent standardized test protocols which are also directly applicable to perovskite solar cell research. The broad use of such standard procedures will facilitate standardization in degradation testing across the community and enable direct comparison between laboratories. For these stability measurements, unencapsulated devices were fabricated with a common architecture of FTO/TiO2/FAMACs/HTM/Au where the HTM was either a standard LiTFSI-doped spiroOMeTAD or EH44. As outlined in the ISOS-L-1 protocols, during testing, the devices remained in ambient conditions (Fig. 2) under approximately 77% of 1-Sun irradiation (irradiance integrated from 300 nm to 1650 nm) using a lamp with a spectrum closely matched to the standard solar spectrum (Supplementary Fig. 6). The devices were held at a static load resistance of 510 Ω and every 30 min a current-voltage scan was acquired by sweeping the voltage at ~60 mV/s from forward to reverse bias conditions (reverse sweep).

Stable perovskite solar cell architectures While the second portion of the decay seen in Fig. 2 is at least partially the result of moisture ingress and subsequent PAL degradation, as seen from the marked reduction in decay kinetics with EH44, we also observe using ToF-SIMS that the Au electrode diffuses into the rest of the device stack (Supplementary Fig. 9), making it necessary to utilize a different electrode material.40,41 As we have previously demonstrated, although MoOx does not interface well with the PAL,42 MoOx/Al electrodes enable a significant stability improvement over Au electrodes while providing similar power conversion efficiency.25,43 The stability of these electrodes is believed to be a result of the MoOx pinning the work function at the HTM interface and the selfpassivating nature of the aluminium oxide formed at the MoOx/Al interface.25 Importantly, the MoOx layer does not serve primarily as an encapsulant since thicker MoOx layers lead to reduced stability.25 To enable long-term testing with MoOx/Al contacts (15 nm MoOx and 200 nm Al), patterned ITO substrates were used in place of FTO (Supplementary Fig. 12). Conclusions We demonstrate that perovskite solar cells can be made resilient to external stress factors with carefully adjusted contact layers even without encapsulation, and elucidate the major degradation mechanisms associated with each of the contact layers (ETM, HTM, and electrode). For this, we first chose the FAMACs absorber paired with a LiTFSI-free HTM, EH44.30 We developed EH44-based devices that can yield power conversion efficiencies in excess of 18% and were comparable with state-of-the-art spiro-OMeTAD-based perovskite solar cells. We show compositional changes in perovskite films in devices with TiO2 ETMs and demonstrate the mitigation of such effects by replacing this common ETM with SnO2. We find the majority of the degradation is in the device VOC, rather than JSC and FF, suggesting primary instabilities have been moved from the contact interfaces to the PAL and are now dominated by environmental degradation mechanisms such as H2O. This conclusion is reinforced by the stability of these devices when these external degradation mechanisms are eliminated. When stability tests are conducted in inert atmosphere, we find only ~2% degradation over the course of 1500 hrs of continual operation (Supplementary Fig. 18). We have developed a device architecture which can be utilized more broadly in the future to evaluate the stability of any of the specific components or interfaces (e.g., PAL, HTM, electrode, etc.) while minimizing complications arising from instabilities related to the other device layers/interfaces. This work highlights the role of interfaces in perovskite solar cell degradation and demonstrates a path toward the further advances required for their commercial adoption. The ability of these unencapsulated perovskite solar cells remain largely unchanged for 1000 hrs despite exposure to the combined stresses of UV-light, oxygen, and moisture, points to a bright future.

The synthesis of 9-(2-ethylhexyl)-N,N,N,N-tetrakis(4-methoxyphenyl)-9Hcarbazole-2,7-diamine (EH44) and was carried out following the reaction scheme in Fig. S1 as described previously.30 Briefly, 2,7-dibromo-9-(2-ethylhexyl)-9H-carbazole (1.76 g, 4.0 mmol), bis(4-methoxyphenyl)amine (2.0 g, 8.8 mmol), sodium t-butoxide (1.15 g, 12 mmol), and bis(trit-butylphosphine)palladium (50 mg, 100 µmol) were added to an oven-dried Schlenk flask, degassed by evacuation, and refilled with argon three times. Anhydrous 1,4-dioxane was added via syringe and the reaction was heated to 80 °C. After 24 hours the reaction was complete via thin layer chromatography analysis and was cooled to room temperature, poured into excess deionized water, and extracted with ethyl acetate. The organic portions were dried with anhydrous magnesium sulphate, filtered, and concentrated by rotary evaporation. The crude reaction mixture was purified using flash chromatography, and concentrated fractions precipitated in acidic methanol (1-2 drops 5% HCl in 200 mL MeOH) to afford a pale yellow solid (1.55 g, 52%). UVvisible absorption spectroscopy, 1H NMR, 13C NMR, DSC, and MALDI were used to characterize the synthesized product, as published previously.30 Preparation of EH44-ox. Oxidation of EH44 to the oxidized EH44+TFSI- salt (EH44-ox) was carried out as described previously.30 Briefly, EH44 (0.40 g, 54.5 mmol) and silver bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (AgTFSI) (0.25 g, 65.4 mmol) were added to a dry Schlenk flask, degassed by evacuation and refilled with argon. Argon sparged dichloromethane (DCM) was added via syringe and the reaction was stirred at room temperature. After 24 hours, the reaction mixture was filtered through a 0.2μm pore PTFE filter. After concentrating with a rotary evaporator, the remaining solution was dissolved in 200 mL hexanes and cooled at 3 °C overnight. The product, a black powder, was collected by filtration, dissolved in warm ethylene glycol, and cooled at 3 °C overnight. After filtration, the product was dissolved in DCM and centrifuged for 5 min at 5000 rpm to remove any remaining Ag particles. The decanted solution was concentrated and dried under vacuum at 75 °C overnight to afford a green-black solid (0.27g, 50%). EH44 Characterization. Measurements were conducted as films on a platinum disk electrode in propylene carbonate with 0.1 M tetrabutylammonium hexafluorophosphate as a supporting electrode, with ferrocene used as an external standard. Electron Transport Layer Deposition. Patterned fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) glass was cleaned by sonication in acetone and isopropanol, followed by UV-ozone cleaning for 15 min. A 20-40 nm thick compact TiO2 layer was deposited by spin-coating a 0.15M solution of titanium diisopropoxide bis(acetylacetonate) (TAA) in 1-butanol with the following procedure: 700 rpm for 10 s, 1000 rpm for 10 s, and 2000 rpm for 30s. The films were dried at 125 °C for at least 5 min and then a 100-150 nm TiO2 nanoparticle layer was deposited by spin coating an ethanolic TiO2 nanoparticle solution (Dyesol 30NR-D diluted at 60 mg/mL in ethanol) using the same spincoating procedure. The TiO2 films were then annealed at 500 °C for 1 hr, allowed to cool to room temperature, and immersed in a 20 mM aqueous TiCl4 solution at 90 °C for 20 min. The films were then rinsed with deionized H2O and annealed at 500 °C for 30 min. The TiO2 films were cleaned for 15 min by UV-ozone immediately before use. For extended stability testing patterned indium-doped tin oxide (ITO) substrates were used to avoid degradation, dramatically observed with MoOx/Al contacts, induced by layer in homogeneities resulting from the >1.5 μm step edge, introduced during the patterning of typical FTO substrates (Supplementary Fig. 12). The ITO glass was cleaned by the same procedure outlined for FTO glass. TiO2 layers were deposited using a low temperature TiO2 process.44 TiO2 nanoparticles were synthesized by adding 2 mL of TiCl4 dropwise to 8 mL of cold ethanol. 40 mL of benzyl alcohol was added to the cold solution causing the solution to change from pale yellow to dark red. The flask was then sealed and heated at 80 °C for 16 hrs in an oven. Next, 5 mL of the reaction solution was mixed with 40 mL of diethyl ether and the TiO2 nanoparticles were precipitated by centrifugation. The particles were redispersed in ethanol and washed a second time by precipitation with diethyl ether. The resulting TiO2 was then dried under vacuum. The TiO2 nanoparticles were dispersed in ethanol at a concentration of 1.18 wt. % along with 20 mol. % (based on the concentration of TiO2) TAA. This ethanolic TiO2 suspension was spin cast onto the ITO substrates using the same spin-coating procedure used for the high temperature TiO2 films. Tin oxide electron transport layers were deposited on cleaned ITO substrates.38 The aqueous SnO2 colloid solution, obtained from Alfa Aesar, was diluted to a concentration of 2.67 wt.% in water, and spin cast at 3000 rpm for 30 s. Both the TiO2 and SnO2 films were then dried at 150 °C for 30 min and cleaned for 15 min by UV-ozone immediately before use. Perovskite Film Deposition. Deposition of all perovskite layers was carried out in a nitrogen glovebox. MAPbI3 perovskite layers were deposited, as reported in ref 45, from a precursor solution containing 461 mg PbI2 and 159 mg MAI dissolved in 78 mg DMSO (1:1:1 mole ratio) and 600 mg DMF. This precursor solution was deposited by spin-coating at 4000 rpm for 25 s. While the substrate was spinning, 0.5 mL of diethyl ether was rapidly dripped onto the film with approximately 15 s remaining in the spin-coating procedure, forming a transparent colourless film which was then annealed for 3 min at 100 °C to form MAPbI3. Triple cation films of the form, FAxMAyCs1-x-yPb(Iz,Br1-z)3 (FAMACs) were formed following the method reported in ref 8. The precursor solution was made by dissolving 172 mg FAI, 507 mg PbI2, 22.4 mg MABr, and 73.4 mg PbBr2 (1:1.1:0.2:0.2 mole ratio) and 40 μL of CsI stock solution (1.5M in DMSO) in 627 mg DMF and 183 mg DMSO (4:1 v/v). The films were deposited by spin coating this precursor solution with the following procedure: 1000 rpm for 10 s, 6000 rpm for 20 s. While the substrate was spinning, 0.1 mL of chlorobenzene was rapidly dripped onto the film with approximately 6 s remaining in the spin-coating procedure, forming a transparent orange film. Devices were tested using Newport Oriel Sol3A solar simulator with xenon lamp. The intensity of the solar simulator was calibrated to 100 mW/cm2 AM1.5G using a KG2 filtered Si reference solar cell. Current density-voltage (JV) scans were taken from forward bias to reverse bias with the following scan parameters: step size = 10 mV, delay time = 10 ms, number of power line cycles = 0.1. The devices were approximately 0.1 cm2 and were masked with a metal aperture to define an active area of 0.059 cm2. The stabilized power output of the devices was measured by recording the current output of the illuminated device while holding it at a constant voltage near the maximum power point of the JV scan. External quantum efficiency (EQE) measurements were taken using a Newport Oriel IQE200. Scanning electron microscopy was performed on cleaved devices using a FEI Nova NanoSEM 630. X-ray Photoemission Spectroscopy measurements were performed on a Kratos NOVA spectrometer calibrated to the Fermi edge and core level positions of sputter-cleaned metal (Au, Ag, Cu, Mo) surfaces. Spectra were taken using monochromated Al Kα radiation (1486.7 eV) at a resolution of 400 meV (pass energy 10 eV) and fit using Pseudo-Voigt profiles. Secondary Ion Mass Spectrometry (SIMS) is a powerful analytical technique for determining elemental and isotopic distributions in solids, as well as the structure and composition of organic materials.46–48 An ION-TOF TOF-SIMS V Time of Flight SIMS (TOF-SIMS) spectrometer was utilized to depth profile the perovskite materials and completed devices. Analysis was completed utilizing a 3-lens 30 kV BiMn primary ion gun, the Bi+ primary-ion beam (operated in bunched mode; 10 ns pulse width, analysis current 1.0 pA), was scanned over a 25×25 micron area. Depth Profiling was accomplished with a 1 kV oxygen ion sputter beam (10.8 nA sputter current) raster of 150×150 micron area. All spectra during profiling were collected at or below a primary ion dose density of 1×1012 ions/cm2 to remain at the static-sims limit. The data is plotted with the intensity for each signal at each data point normalized to the total ion counts measured at that data point, which diminishes artefacts from a changing ion yield in different layers when profiling through completed devices.
