ABSTRACT

Developing highly conductive, stable, and active non-precious hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) catalysts is a key step for the proposed hydrogen economy. However, few catalysts, except for noble metals, meet the entire requirements. By using state-of-the-art density functional calculations, herein we demonstrate that 2D MXenes, like Ti2C, V2C and Ti3C2, are terminated by a mixture of oxygen atoms and hydroxyl, while Nb2C and Nb4C3O2 are fully terminated by oxygen atoms under standard conditions (pH = 0, p(H2) = 1 bar, U = 0 V SHE), which is in good agreement with experimental observation. Furthermore, all these MXenes are conductive under standard conditions, thus enabling high charge transfer kinetics during the HER. Remarkably, the Gibbs free-energy for the adsorption of atomic 0 hydrogen ( 𝛥𝐺𝐻∗ ) on the terminated O atoms (e.g. Ti2CO2) are close to the ideal value (0 eV). Our results

demonstrate terminated oxygens as catalytic active sites for the HER at these materials and highlight a family of promising 2D catalysts for water splitting. Keyword: Water splitting, surface Pourbaix diagrams, termination, volcano curve, Gibbs free-energy of hydrogen adsorption, functional groups.

Introduction Hydrogen production from water splitting offers an essential solution for current problems in the environment 1-2. So far, the most efficient hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) catalysts are noble metals, such as platinum3, however, the scarcity of noble metals hamper their applications as catalysts for largescale hydrogen generation4. In recent years, some promising noble-metal-free catalysts with high activity toward the HER have been extensively studied including MoS25-7, metal phosphides8, g-C3N49-11 and nanocarbon12. However, one significant drawback of these 2D catalysts is that they are semi-conducting with poor charge transfer performance5. Even though 1T-MoS2 is conductive, it is unstable under ambient conditions7. Another drawback is that the catalytically active sites are confined to the edge or defective atoms, while the majority of the in-plane atoms are catalytically inert6, 12-13. Therefore, the ongoing search for new stable 2D HER catalysts with excellent charge transfer kinetics and many catalytically active sites is of paramount significance. 2D MXenes, early transition metal (M) carbides and nitrides (X), have recently been exfoliated via selective etching of the ‘A’ element in the counterpart MAX phases14. Here, M, A and X represent an early transition metal, a main group IIIA or IVA element and a carbon and/or nitrogen atom, respectively. The chemical formula of 2D MXenes is MnXn-1 (n = 2-4), where the top and bottom layers consist of the transition metals that are exposed. Since the synthesis of the first MXene, i. e. Ti3C2 from the MAX (Ti3AlC2) phase in 201115, this new family of 2D materials has attracted intensive research attention14, 1617

. Although a variety of 2D MXenes have been theoretically predicted18, only a few of them including

Ti2C 19, V2C20, Nb2C21, Ti3C215 and Nb4C322, have been synthesized to date. Experimentally, the exposed M-atoms in 2D MXenes are generally terminated by F*, OH* and O* groups 14, 23, where * represents one adsorption site on the surface. Significantly, the surface O-terminated 2D MXenes have demonstrated promising applications in Li-ion batteries24-25, supercapacitors26-27, fuel cells28-29, hydrogen evolution30, CO oxidation31 and gas sensors32 due to their excellent conductivity and tuneable surface terminations33.

Page 4

The purpose of this study is to explore (i) the stability of 2D MXenes with oxygen/hydroxyl (O*/OH*) termination 14, 34 and (ii) whether the experimentally realized MXenes (Ti2C, V2C, Nb2C, Ti3C2 and Nb4C3) with O* or OH* termination can act as efficient HER catalysts. Firstly, we find that 2D MXenes commonly terminated with O*/OH* group in the experiment are metallic. Then surface Pourbaix diagrams were constructed to identify the most stable surface structures of 2D MXenes under relevant potentials vs the standard hydrogen electrode (USHE) and pH. Under standard conditions, the Ti2C, V2C, Ti3C2 are terminated by a mixture of OH* and O*, while Nb2C and Nb4C3 are fully terminated by O*. The HER activity of oxygen-terminated MXenes are further evaluated by calculating the Gibbs freeenergy of the adsorption of atomic hydrogen ( 𝜟𝑮𝟎𝑯∗ ). Remarkably, surface oxygen atoms are for the first time demonstrated to be active sites for the HER. In addition, the HER performances of MXences with and without oxygen termination are compared by volcano curve. The results show that oxygen termination promotes the hydrogen releasing and increases the exchange current dramatically. Computational Details All calculations were performed by using Density functional theory (DFT) as implemented in the Vienna Ab-initio Simulation Package (VASP) code 35-36. Blöchl’s all-electron, frozen-core projector augmented wave (PAW) method37 was used to represent nuclei and core electrons, and only the electrons in brackets of Ti[3d24s2], V[3d34s2], Nb[4p64d45s1], O[2s22p4], and H[1s1], are treated the valence electrons. The exchange-correlation interactions were described by the generalized gradient approximation 38 in the form of the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof functional39. The van der Waals interaction was described by using the empirical correction in Grimme’s scheme, i.e. DFT+D3 in all the calculation40. The electron wavefunctions were expanded by plane waves with cut-off energies of 500 eV, and the convergence tolerance for residual force and energy on each atom during structure relaxation were set to 0.005 eV/Å and 10 -5 eV, respectively. The vacuum space in Z-direction was more than 20 Å, which was enough to avoid the interaction between periodical images. The Brillouin zone was sampled with the Monkhorst−Pack mesh with a K-point of 9×9×1 for unit cell, and 5 × 5 × 1 for 2 × 2 × 1 supercell respectively in reciprocal space

Results and Discussions Excellent Electrical Conductivity The structure models of MXenes (MnCn-1), oxygen-terminated MXenes (MnCn-1O2) and hydroxylterminated MXenes (MnCn-1(OH)2), are presented in Figure 1. The 2D MXenes investigated in this work are 2D Ti2C, V2C, Nb2C, Ti3C2 and Nb4C3, which have all been experimentally realized15, 19-22. As shown in Figure 1a, 1d, and 1g, the bare MXenes are packed in the face-centred cubic arrangement with two exposed metal layers, displaying P3m1 symmetry. The calculated band structures of bare MXenes are presented in Figure 2a-e, clearly indicating that all the MXenes are metallic. Since the exposed metal atoms are electron donors, they are vulnerable to electronegative functional groups, such as OH* and O* group during their experimental synthesis.

Color code: Metal, cyan; Carbon, grey; Oxygen, red; and Hydrogen, white. The surfaces of MXenes that are fully terminated by O* (Figure 1 b, e, h) or OH* (Figure 1 c, f, i) are also constructed. The most stable adsorption sites for O*/OH* functional groups are first examined and we find the hollow sites which are pointed towards the second layer of metal atoms (face-centred cubic hollow sites) on both sides of MXenes are the most energy-favourable sites for the adsorption of O*/OH* functional groups. As Ti2C is terminated by O* (Ti2CO2), the Ti atoms are in their highest oxidized state (+4) achieved by donating their electrons to the negatively charged oxygen and carbon atoms (-2 and -4 respectively)34. Therefore, the Fermi level shift below the d band (conductive band) resulted in Ti2CO2 being semi-conducting with a gap of 0.78 eV (seen in in Figure 2f). Interestingly, once one hydrogen

atom is adsorbed on the Ti2CO2, the band gap is closed and Ti2CO2 become metallic (See Figure S1 in supporting information). This is mainly attributed to some spare electrons in the d band of Ti atoms after donating an electron partially to the negatively charged OH- (-1). Except for Ti2C, all other 2D MXenes terminated with functional groups are metallic. Therefore O* or OH* terminated 2D MXenes can display excellent charge transfer performance for efficient HER.

High Thermal Stability In the following, the surface Pourbaix diagrams of all five 2D MXenes are constructed by plotting the thermodynamically most stable surface state under the relevant USHE and pH (see Figure 3) [21]. As alluded to above, the bare MXenes are very vulnerable to electron accepting functional groups such as OH*, and O*. In acidic solution (pH = 0), negative USHE values (reducing environment) as low as -2.63 V, -1.91 V, -2.04 V, -2.53 V, and -1.51 V are required to protect Ti2C, V2C, Nb2C, Ti3C2 and Nb4C3 from oxidation by H2O, respectively. A more negative potential is necessary to protect the bare MXenes at high

Page 10

pH with a slope of -0.059 V/pH. When the potential is above the cathodic protection potential of the 2D MXenes, water starts to oxidize and the MXenes are covered by 1/8 ML OH*. As the potential increases, more OH* adsorbs on the 2D MXenes, and it finally reaches to 1 ML OH* (MnXn-1(OH)2). For instance, 2D Ti2C is fully terminated by OH* at USHE = -0.34 V. The hydroxyl is partially oxidized if the potential continues to increase and finally the MXenes are terminated by a mixture of O* and OH*. At higher potentials, all the hydroxyls on MXenes will be oxidized, and the most stable O* terminated MXenes (MnXn-1(O)2) will be formed. The lowest USHE for 2D MXenes with full O* termination are 0.21 V (Ti2C), 0.50 V (V2C), 0.00 V (Nb2C), 0.37 V (T3C2), and -0.07 V (Nb4C3) in acidic solution (pH =0). Therefore, under standard conditions, the Ti2C, V2C, Ti3C2 are terminated by a mixture of OH* and O*, while Nb2C and Nb4C3 are fully terminated by O*. The 2D MXenes must exhibit high stability and excellent HER activity at USHE = 0 V if they are to be used as efficient HER catalysts. Herein, we investigated the relative stability of MXenes with different terminations as a function of pH at USHE=0V (see Figure 4). The bottom line corresponds to the states with the lowest free energy, i.e. the most stable state at a given pH value. The O*/OH* terminated MXenes are much more stable than bare 2D MXenes or partially terminated MXenes (1/8 ML OH*). In acidic solution (pH = 0), the most stable states of Nb2C and Nb3C2 are O* terminated surfaces, while, Ti2C, V2C, and Ti3C2 terminated by the mixture of O* and OH* are the most energetically favourable. As the pH value is increased, the hydrogen atom in the hydroxyl group on the 2D MXenes (Ti2C, V2C, and Ti3C2) can be dissociated. When the pH values are above 3.6, 8.4 and 5.5 for Ti2C, V2C, and Ti3C2 respectively (the black lines cross the green lines in Figure 4 a, b, d), these MXenes are fully terminated by O*. Collectively, these are consistent with the experimental observation that MXenes terminated by a mixture of OH* and O* are highly stable14, 23 and thus it is reasonable to investigate MXenes terminated by a mixture of OH* and O* as the most appropriate catalysts for the HER.

The 𝜟𝑮𝟎𝑯∗ of an ideal catalyst for the HER should be close to 0 (𝜟𝑮𝟎𝑯∗ →0). It is worth noting that there are two surface O* layers in MnCn-1O2

which are functioning as the active sites for the HER. In a 2 × 2 × 1 MnCn-1O2 supercell, there are 8 O* atoms and different H-coverages can be simulated with an increment of 1/8. For Ti2CO2 with hydrogen coverage of 1/8, 𝜟𝑮𝟎𝑯∗ is -0.04 eV, which is nearly perfect for the HER. When an even number of hydrogen atoms are adsorbed in a 2 × 2 × 1 Ti2CO2 supercell, the system can be stabilized by H* binding due to a higher symmetry. Therefore, the hydrogen binding free energy at H-coverages of 2/8 (4/8) is lower than that at H-coverages of 1/8 (3/8). The 𝜟𝑮𝟎𝑯∗ for hydrogen coverage ranging from 1/18 to 4/18 (|𝜟𝑮𝟎𝑯∗ | < 𝟎. 𝟏𝟓) is close to 0, indicating that Ti2CO2 is potentially an excellent catalyst for the HER under a wide range of H coverage.

Conclusions In summary, we have demonstrated that 2D MXenes commonly terminated with a mixture of O* and OH* in experiment exhibit excellent HER performance. Under standard conditions, the surface Pourbaix diagrams clearly indicated that O*/OH* terminated MXenes are the most stable states, and all of them are metallic, thus favouring excellent charge transfer. The surface oxygen atoms on the top and bottom layers of 2D MXenes function as the catalytic active sites for the HER with a suitable interaction strength between H* and 2D MXenes. If coupling with a suitable photocatalyst, metallic 2D MXenes under standard conditions could be an efficient co-catalyst for the photocatalytic HER. Our findings demonstrate that O-terminations act as catalytic active sites for efficient HER and highlight a new family of promising noble metal-free HER catalysts for future experimental verification.
