Electroreduction of nitrogen at almost 100% current-to-ammonia efficiency Hoang-Long Du1,2, Manjunath Chatti1,2, Rebecca Y. Hodgetts1,2, Pavel V. Cherepanov1, Cuong K. Nguyen1,2, Karolina Matuszek1, Douglas R. MacFarlane1,2,*, Alexandr N. Simonov1,2,* 1

School of Chemistry and the 2ARC Centre of Excellence for Electromaterials Science, Monash University, Clayton, Victoria 3800, Australia *Corresponding Authors E-mail Addresses: douglas.macfarlane@monash.edu (DRM); alexandr.simonov@monash.edu (ANS). Beyond its use in the fertiliser and chemical industries1, ammonia is currently seen as a potential replacement for carbon-based fuels and as a carrier for worldwide transportation of renewable energy2. Implementation of this vision requires transformation of the existing fossil fuel based technology for NH3 production3 to a simpler, scale-flexible technology, such as the electrochemical lithium-mediated nitrogen reduction reaction (Li-NRR)3,4. This provides a genuine pathway from N2 to ammonia, yet is hampered by limited yield rates and efficiencies4-12. Here we investigate the role of the electrolyte in this reaction and present a high-efficiency, robust process enabled by compact ionic layering in the electrode-electrolyte interfacial region. The interface is generated by a highconcentration imide-based lithium salt electrolyte, enabling stabilised ammonia yield rates of 150±20 nmol s-1 cm-2 and current-to-ammonia efficiency closely approaching 100%. The ionic assembly formed at the electrode surface suppresses electrolyte decomposition and supports stable N2 reduction. Our study highlights the interrelation between the performance of the Li-NRR and the physicochemical properties of the electrode-electrolyte interface. We anticipate that these findings will guide the development of a robust, high-performance process for sustainable ammonia production.

The only known realistic pathway towards high-performance electrosynthesis of ammonia from nitrogen at ambient temperature13-15 is the lithium-mediated process3,6,14, which harnesses the ability of Li metal to reduce N2 spontaneously under ambient conditions, producing lithium nitride. Reaction of Li3N with a suitable proton carrier (BH) generates ammonia and releases the Li+ to reinitiate the electrocatalytic cycle4:

Reports of this process from the 1990’s demonstrated that N2 can be reduced to ammonia with faradaic efficiencies (FE; the fraction of charge producing the target product) of 50-60% and yield rates of a few nmol s-1 cm-2 (hereinafter, cm-2 indicates per geometric surface area of the electrode) at 50 bar nitrogen pressure, and with 5-6 fold lower performance at ambient pressure, on a timescale limited to a few hours4,5. Recent studies typically report comparable metrics6,8,9,16,17. Our latest work demonstrated improved Li-NRR rates of 53±1 nmol s-1 cm-2 and FE of 69±1 % using a phosphonium salt as the proton carrier10 to enhance the performance. A key aspect of the Li-NRR that remains unexplored is the composition of the active surface of the electrode and the structure of the electrolyte near this surface, collectively known as the solidelectrolyte interface (SEI) layer9,18. The present work aims to explore the role of this layer in controlling the Li-mediated N2 reduction, focusing on the effects of electrolyte concentration19,20 and the chemical nature of the anion18,21,22.

ELECTROLYTE EFFECTS Previous studies on the Li-NRR used perchlorate5,6,8,11,12,17, tetrafluoroborate5,7,16 and triflate5,9 electrolytes; none of these are considered favourable for electrochemical lithium cycling23. Conversely, the bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide (NTf2-) and bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (FSI-) anions are well-known to be stable under the lithium reduction conditions24 and support high rates of Li0 electrodeposition25,26. Here, we investigate LiNTf2 as the Li-NRR electrolyte at a nickel wire electrode under 15 bar dinitrogen pressure, using tetrahydrofuran as solvent and ethanol as proton carrier. Technical aspects of the work are detailed in the Supplementary Information, including experimental procedures (Sections 1-2), ammonia quantification27 (Section 1), effects of pressure (Section 3) and convection (Section 4), and apparent Li0/+ potential (Li0/+ app ) used as a reference for all potentials reported herein (Section 5).

Both potentiodynamic (cyclic voltammetric) and potentiostatic (chronoamperometric) measurements with a 0.15 cm2 electrode demonstrated a pronounced influence of LiNTf2 concentration on the rate of electroreduction at potentials below 0 V (Figure 1a-b). The observed trend in current density with salt concentration, with a maximal cathodic (most negative) current at 1.5 M and a decline at higher concentrations, results from an interplay of three factors: (i) the amount of Li+ available for reduction, (ii) the ionic conductivity (σ), and (iii) the dynamic viscosity of the solutions (Supplementary Figure 15), which collectively determine the Li+, ethanol and N2 transport rates. The presence of N2 and ethanol resulted in the desired suppression of the chemical reversibility of lithium cycling, as observed in the lower oxidation charge in the positive-scanning parts of voltammograms, compared to the reduction charge (Figure 1a). When the potential was fixed at -0.55 V for 6 h (Figure 1b), the current flowing through the system was stable regardless of the LiNTf2 concentration, although some fluctuations were observed under conditions enabling the highest reduction rates. The measured amounts of ammonia produced in these experiments were used to calculate the NH3 yield rates and faradaic efficiencies (Figure 1c). Increasing the LiNTf2 concentration from 0.1 to 0.5 M did not significantly affect the FE within the 10-20% range, but enhanced the yield rate. At 1 M, ammonia was produced at ca 160 nmol s-1 cm-2 with an improved FE of ca 45%. These effects likely arise from the increase in the amount of Li+ available for reduction and significant increase in σ, which peaks at 1 M (Supplementary Figures 15 and 16a). Unprecedented NH3 yield rates of 240±20 and 230±20 nmol s-1 cm-2 were achieved with 1.5 and 2 M LiNTf2, respectively. The productivity was then observed to decrease in the more viscous 2.5 and 3 M solutions where the mass-transport becomes a limiting factor (Figure 1c and Supplementary Figure 15). Even more remarkable is the electrolyte concentration effect on the FE, which approaches 90% at ≥ 2 M. The genuine nature of the Li-NRR was confirmed through a series of control experiments including 15N2 reduction27 (Supplementary Information, Section 7). To demonstrate the unique role of the NTf2- anion, we investigated electrolytes used in previous research, viz. LiOTf5, LiClO45,6,8,17 and LiBF45,7,16, as well as NTf2-’s smaller imide analogue FSI-. Conductivities of their 2 M solutions increased in the order OTf- < ClO4- < BF4- < NTf2- < FSI(Supplementary Figure 15 and 17a). The ammonia yield rate followed the same trend, although the result for 2 M LiNTf2 is a clear standout (Figure 1d). Moreover, the 1 M LiBF4 + 1 M LiNTf2 combination produced much less ammonia than 2 M LiNTf2, or even 1 M LiNTf2 (Figure 1d). Thus, although higher σ is beneficial for the Li-NRR rate, the NTf2--based electrolytes distinctly stand out from the conductivity-rate trend observed for other anions. No correlations between the performance and viscosity were identified (Supplementary Figure 18). Most important are the clear performance 3

advantages, i.e. Li-NRR rates >200 nmol s-1 cm-2, provided by the two imide anions (Figure 1d). We also found that the performance strongly depends on the chemical nature of the electrode (Supplementary Information, Section 8), with nickel being optimum. Taken together, these findings identify the key role of the chemical nature of the electrolyte-electrode interface in the Li-NRR.

ELECTRODE-ELECTROLYTE INTERFACE The use of the progressively more negative constant potentials with 2 M LiNTf2 down to -0.80 V increased the reduction currents (Figure 2a) and ammonia yield rates (Figure 2b). High faradaic efficiency in excess of 80% was maintained in the -0.5 to -0.8 V range, but declined at more negative potentials. Moreover, performance deteriorated significantly after several hours of operation at -0.7, -0.8 and -1.0 V (Figure 2a, Supplementary Figure 19e). The more negative the potential that was employed, the earlier this degradation occurred. The appearance of the working electrodes after tests at different potentials also differed notably (Figure 2c-f). In contrast to the visually clean electrode after the reaction at -0.55 V, experiments at -1 V produced a gross deposit around the nickel wire. Analysis of the latter by X-ray diffraction (XRD) revealed a set of peaks which might be associated with lithium amine, oxide, sulphide and fluoride, the latter two being the products of NTf2- decomposition. Uncontrolled build-up of these compounds is the cause of the progressive deceleration of the Li-NRR (Figure 2a). Thus, continuous stable operation of the system requires the potential to be maintained more positive than -0.8 V. Scanning electron microscopic (SEM) examination of the electrode after tests at -0.55 V revealed minor modification of the surface with a faint coating (Figure 2d), which was undetectable by XRD (Figure 2f) and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) (Supplementary Figure 30). More sensitive X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) indicated the presence of intact LiNTf2 along with LiF and reduced S-O species28 as the only electrochemically generated products (Figure 2e). A welldefined nickel metal signal was also detected by XPS (Supplementary Figure 31), indicating that the electrochemically-generated layer is very thin (<10 nm) and/or non-continuous. Highlighting the unique properties of 2 M LiNTf2, Supplementary Figures 32-34 compare photographs, XRD, XPS and SEM/EDS data collected for the electrodes operated with 0.5 M LiNTf2 and 2 M LiBF4. In both cases, a clearly visible grey deposit with LiF as a prominent component developed on the surface, which was particularly thick for LiBF4. Electrochemical impedance spectra (EIS) – a sensitive indicator of the changes to the electrode-electrolyte interface – confirmed minimal modifications to the electrode surface with a low-resistance SEI layer after tests with 2 M LiNTf2, but not with LiBF4 (Figure 1e, Supplementary Tables 2-7).

High-concentration LiNTf2 electrolytes can displace solvent molecules from the negatively charged electrode surface with layers of strongly coordinated Li+ and NTf2- ions20-22,29. Such ionic assemblies are critical for stable Li0/+ and Na0/+ cycling25,30, and their formation was probed herein by differential capacitance measurements. The results obtained show an order of magnitude higher capacitance provided by the NTf2- electrolyte as compared to BF4- (Figure 1f, Supplementary Figures 21-24). This indicates a much more compact ionic assembly layer is formed near the interface by Li + with the imide-based anion, since differential capacitance increases as the inverse of distance between the layers of charge31. Decrease in the LiNTf2 concentration from 2 to 0.5 M also decreases the capacitance of the electrode-electrolyte interface (Figure 1f). High salt concentration excludes the tetrahydrofuran solvent from the interface, where lithium ions are expected to be predominant in the innermost layer. Coordination requirements of Li+ are therefore exclusively satisfied by tight, chelatetype binding to NTf2-, supporting the formation of compact ionic assemblies. The unique role of the imide anion in this context was demonstrated in additional control experiments (Supplementary Figure 20). Another important parameter is the Li+ transference number (tLi+), which significantly increases with LiNTf2 concentration in tetrahydrofuran32; high tLi+ is beneficial for stable and efficient Li0/+ cycling33. Thus, the advantages provided by the high-concentration LiNTf2 electrolytes for the NRR arise from their capacity to form dense ionic assemblies at the electrode-electrolyte interface and provide high Li+ transference numbers. These factors suppress reductive degradation of the solvent and electrolyte, beyond the initial formation of a thin layer of LiF and S-O species, while facilitating the overall N2 reduction process. We hypothesise from this that the Li-NRR is most effective when the thinnest SEI layer is formed on the surface, as was achieved herein for 2 M LiNTf2 (Figure 2d-f).

IMPROVED FARADAIC EFFICIENCY To further understand the kinetics of the Li-NRR mediated by 2 M LiNTf2, the time dependence of NH3 production was investigated at -0.55 V using 0.15 cm2 electrodes. Notwithstanding variable, yet remarkably reproducible, behaviour of the current (Figure 3a), ammonia was steadily accumulated throughout the 12 h tests (Figure 3b). Faradaic efficiency at 3 h was 65±8 %, while experiments undertaken for 6, 9 and 12 h produced ammonia at progressively increasing FEs of 85±9, 89±9 % and 91±7 %, respectively; these values are based on the charges and NH3 yields over the whole duration of the experiments, including the initial period of lower productivity. Calculation of the FE for the 612 h experiments after subtraction of the charge and NH3 yield data for the 3 h tests produced values very close to 100% (empty triangles in Figure 3b). In other words, essentially all charge passing through the system after 3 h was associated with the conversion of N2 to NH3 and the excess charge 5

Page 6

during the first 3 h is likely associated with the electrolyte-solution decomposition, which stops contributing thereafter. Investigating the origins of this unproductive consumption of charge during the initial period, we noted that a clearly identifiable deposit containing LiF, polysulphides and Li3N was formed on the working electrode in the region at the electrolyte-electrode-gas triple phase boundary (i.e. electrolyte meniscus), contrasting the visually pristine surface of the part that was immersed in the solution (Figure 3c, Supplementary Figures 30 and 35). Formation of this multicomponent deposit is supported by facilitated kinetics of the reaction between gas-phase N2 and electrochemically generated Li0 through a thin liquid-phase meniscus on the electrode34. Ethanol proton carrier in this region is rapidly consumed resulting in uncontrollable accumulation of high-surface area Li3N, which might further promote the decomposition of the electrolyte solution. To determine the contribution of these unproductive processes to the lower than 100 % FE, experiments with isolated nickel wire (0.05 cm2 active area) where no meniscus was present were undertaken (Figures 4a-c). This strategy enabled a reproducible FE of 98±2 % (n = 4 independent experiments, including additional 15N2 +14N2 control experiment) in 24 h tests, with average ammonia yield rate of 530±20 nmol s-1 cm-2 (Supplementary Table 15, Supplementary Figure 38). Longerterm, 96 h continuous (Figure 4a) and interrupted (Supplementary Figure 39) experiments, produced ammonia at FE of 99±2 % (n = 3) (Supplementary Table 15). These results suggest that a cathode for the Li-NRR must be completely flooded with the electrolyte solution to enable close to 100 % selective NH3 electrosynthesis. The behaviour of the current density during the initial 24 h of operation with an isolated wire electrode was dynamic, yet highly reproducible and almost exclusively resulting from the Li-NRR (Figure 4a, Supplementary Figure 38). After ca 30 h, the current density stabilised at an unprecedently high value of 36±12 mA cm-2 (Figure 4b), which is essentially exclusively associated with the Li-NRR producing ammonia at a yield rate of 150±20 nmol s-1 cm-2. Stability of NTf2- in solution was confirmed by Fourier-transform IR and NMR spectroscopy (Supplementary Figures 36-37). The amount of ammonia produced in the 96 h experiments (3.9±0.1 mmol) was ca 4-fold higher than the amount of ethanol present (1 mmol), indicating that it is not a completely sacrificial reactant but can also operate as a proton carrier. It is also noted that the use of our recently reported phosphonium proton shuttle with the concentrated LiNTf2 electrolyte enables significant improvements in both FE (77±2 vs. 69±7 %) and ammonia yield rate (430±40 vs. 58±14 nmol s-1 cm-2) as compared to lowconcentration LiBF410 (Supplementary Figure 40, Supplementary Table 17). Finally, experiments with larger working electrodes having an active geometric surface area of 1.2 cm2 were undertaken as a preliminary demonstration of the scalability of the process (Figure 4d6

f). The electrochemical behaviour observed with the electrodes of different size was qualitatively similar (Figure 4a vs. 4d). Most importantly, the NRR metrics achieved with the 1.2 cm2 electrodes agreed well with those obtained for the smaller ones, with NH3 being produced at 170-190 nmol s-1 cm-2 at 90 % faradaic efficiency (Figure 4e, Supplementary Table 16). The slightly lower FE in these experiments is attributed to the differences in the mass-transport conditions (Supplementary Information, Section 4) and/or oxidation of produced ammonia due to the more positive anode potential required (Figure 4c vs. 4a) to maintain the significantly higher currents. Table 1 compares the Li-NRR metrics presented herein to previous literature reports. Apart from the significant, several-fold, improvements in the ammonia yield rate over the state-of-the-art and the first demonstration of the faradaic efficiency of 99 %, our work also presents a highly stable operation on a timescale of 4 days. While the present study highlights lithium bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide as an optimal electrolyte, it is likely that other anions of the fluorosulfonyl imide family might provide even better performance. Our finding of the pernicious effects of the electrode-electrolyte-gas phase boundary also has important implications for the future design of practical NH3 electrosynthesis cells. Our investigation here has focussed on the fundamental Li-NRR performance at the cathode. Further developments towards a complete ammonia electrosynthesis system will require investigations of appropriate anode reactions while eliminating sacrificial solvent oxidation. A feasible initial strategy is to couple the Li-NRR with the H2 oxidation reaction, already demonstrated7,10 but requiring improvements in stability and activity35. A more desirable anode process is H2O oxidation, which presents more significant challenges due to the potential interference of water with the Li-mediated process and vice versa. This is the subject of our ongoing work, while the aim of the present paper is to demonstrate the unprecedent ammonia yield rates and close to 100% faradaic efficiency that are fundamentally possible for the N2 reduction reaction mediated by the concentrated LiNTf2 electrolyte.
