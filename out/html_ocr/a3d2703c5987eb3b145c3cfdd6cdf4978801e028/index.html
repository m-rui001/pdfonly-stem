Abstract:

Discovery of earth-abundant electrocatalysts to replace iridium for the oxygen evolution reaction (OER) in a proton exchange membrane water electrolyzer (PEMWE) represents a critical step in reducing the cost for green hydrogen production. We report here a nanofibrous cobalt spinel catalyst co-doped with lanthanum and manganese prepared from a zeolitic imidazolate framework embedded in electrospun polymer fiber. The catalyst demonstrated a low overpotential of 353 millivolts at 10 milliamperes per square centimeter and a low degradation for OER over 360 hours in acidic electrolyte. A PEMWE containing this catalyst at the anode demonstrated a current density of 2000 milliamperes per square centimeter at 2.47 volts (Nafion $ ^{ \circ} $ 115 membrane) or 4000 milliamperes per square centimeter at 3.00 volt (Nafion $ ^{ \circ} $ 212 membrane), and low degradation in an accelerated-stress-test.

Main Text:

Low-temperature water electrolysis can rapidly produce environmentally sustainable, or "green" hydrogen and is a prospective means of storing energy from renewable but intermittent power sources such as wind and solar in future clean energy infrastructure (1-4). Commercial systems use either liquid alkaline electrolyte or proton exchange membrane electrolyte (1). Compared to the alkaline counterpart, PEMWE offers the advantages of higher current density, higher $ H_{2} $ purity, lower resistance losses, and more compact design, rendering it a preferred technology where high efficiency and small footprint are essential (1-2). Working under the acidic

and oxidative environment, however, adds substantial challenges to the catalyst activity and stability profile. This is particularly the case for the anode catalyst due to the high overpotential for OER (3). At present, OER catalysts for PEMWE are primarily restricted to the platinum group metal (PGM) materials, such as $ \mathrm{I r O_{x}} $ (1). Their high cost and limited reserve, however, pose substantial barriers to the widespread implementation of PEMWE. Low-cost transition metals and their oxides are known to be active toward OER in alkaline electrolyte (4-6), but their demonstration in acidic electrolyte is very limited (1, 7, 8).

Cobalt molecular and oxide compounds emerged as promising OER catalysts for water splitting in recent years (9). Nocera's group systematically investigated water oxidation using a catalyst deposited from $ \mathrm{C o}^{2+} $ solution in pH 7 phosphate buffer (10). Stahl and co-workers performed a comprehensive mechanistic study of Co-catalyzed water oxidation from homogenous to heterogenous phases in electrolyte over a broad pH range of 0-14 (11). Those studies provided profound understanding of electrocatalytic oxygen evolution by cobalt. More recently, thin film spinel-type $ \mathrm{C o}{3} \mathrm{O}{4} $ was found to be active toward OER and stable at low overpotential in acid (12). The activity and stability of Co oxide were improved significantly when modified with iron, manganese, antimonite, and $ \mathrm{P b O_{x}} $ (13-15). In addition to activity and stability, the inherent conductivity represents an essential requirement for efficient OER electrocatalysis to overcome the insulating properties of most transition metal oxides in their crystalline form. Conventional carbon supports, used to facilitate the electron conductivity, are not stable against oxidation to $ \mathrm{C O}{2} $ at the PEMWE anode under OER operating potential. For example, a Co-polyoxometalate composited with carbon paste achieved a lower OER onset potential than $ \mathrm{I r O{2}} $ in 1 M sulfuric acid but decayed quickly, presumably due to oxidative corrosion of the carbon (16). Self-conductive oxide catalysts can also enhance the active site volumetric density without being diluted by a

secondary non-active support. A recently developed self-healing OER catalyst demonstrated excellent activity and durability in acidic electrolyte (14). The approach, however, requires the presence of metal precursors in the aqueous electrolyte, hindering integration into a PEMWE. Most of the aforementioned studies were carried out either in half-cells or aqueous electrolyzers where the demands for OER catalyst stability and conductivity are different from those in a PEMWE. For example, the dissolved transition metal concentration must be minimized to avoid poisoning the proton exchange membrane in the PEMWE. Effective OER for PEMWE requires optimal interfacial properties, micro-porosity, and surface catalytic activity (1), all of which need to be validated in the operating electrolyzer.

Herein, we present a cobalt spinel-based OER catalyst derived from a zeolitic methylimidazolate framework (Co-ZIF) and processed by electrospinning. The catalyst demonstrated excellent OER activity benefitting from its high specific surface area, porous interconnected nano network structure, and high conductivity.

Design of an acid-stable cobalt OER catalyst. Our design concept of an efficient cobalt spinel-based OER catalyst for PEMWE anodes is based on the following rationale: to enhance OER activity in acid, an oversized and more stable $ 2^{\mathrm{nd}} $ element can be selectively introduced to the cobalt oxide surface to generate strain, oxygen vacancy $ \left( V_{0} \right) $ and acid tolerance (17); to improve the oxide electronic conductivity, a $ 3^{\mathrm{rd}} $ element with similar charge and dimension to cobalt may be incorporated inside the lattice to bridge the Fermi bandgap through d orbital partial occupation of the $ 3^{\mathrm{rd}} $ element induced by its d-electron delocalization. Advancing further from the RDE/halfcell study to MEA demonstration, the catalyst should have a high porosity and surface area easily accessible to the reactant $ \left( \mathrm{H}{2} \mathrm{O} \right) $ . Meanwhile, the electrode layer should be effective in transporting $ \mathrm{H}{2} \mathrm{O} $ and releasing $ \mathrm{O}_{2} $ without blocking the water-catalyst interface. Furthermore, the oxide

catalyst should be self-conductive without the need for another conductive support such as carbon that is unstable under high OER operating potential and current density. Last but not least, the metal oxide should be stable against oxidative and acidic (pH 2~4) corrosion in the PEMWE environment.

We selected Co-ZIF as the precursor for the catalyst preparation due to its high intrinsic porosity and reticular structure. It was recently used to prepare a nano-plate oxide with excellent OER activity tested in strong alkaline electrolyte (1M KOH) using the RDE method (18). Our catalyst preparation through low-temperature oxidation partially retained the porosity and morphology of Co-ZIFs after their conversion into interconnected hollow metal oxide particles, providing an excellent platform for enhanced charge and mass transfer. Among different elements we screened, we selected lanthanum $ \mathrm{(La^{3+})} $ as the second element due to its much larger radius than $ \mathrm{Co^{2+}} $ (1.06 $ \mathrm{\AA} $ vs. 0.72 $ \mathrm{\AA} $ ) along with its strong affinity to bind -OH group at the surface of cobalt oxide. We also added manganese ions $ \mathrm{(Mn^{2+})} $ of similar radius to $ \mathrm{Co^{2+}} $ (0.8 $ \mathrm{\AA} $ vs. 0.72 $ \mathrm{\AA} $ ) during the Co-ZIF synthesis, which would be oxidized to $ \mathrm{Mn^{3+}} $ (0.72 $ \mathrm{\AA} $ ) during the oxidative conversion and uniformly distributed inside cobalt spinel lattice to promote conductivity (via bandgap) and OER activity (via affinity OH/H group). The catalyst synthesis scheme is shown in Fig. 1A. Briefly, La and Mn doped Co-ZIF, LaMn@Co-ZIF, was prepared in solution. X-ray powder diffraction (XRD) combined with scanning electron microscopy (SEM)-energy dispersive x-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping confirmed the successful incorporation of atomic La and Mn into the structure and cavity of the Co-ZIF (fig. S1). The LaMn@Co-ZIF was then suspended in a polyacrylonitrile (PAN) polymer slurry $ \mathrm{(LaMn@Co-ZIF/PAN)} $ (19), which was subsequently electrospun into a fibrous mat. Active site analysis. To understand the nature of the active site and impact of the $ 2^{\mathrm{nd}} $ and $ 3^{\mathrm{rd}} $ metal doping, we performed in situ XAS of LMCF in an $ \mathrm{O_{2}} $ saturated electrolysis cell (0.1 M $ \mathrm{H C l O_{4}} $ ) at the Co, Mn K-edge and La- $ \mathrm{L_{III}} $ edge, respectively. Fig. 2A shows the XANES spectra at the Co K-edge under different OER operating potentials. At the onset potential of 1.23 V when OER has yet to commence, XANES already shows a decrease in WL intensity and an increase in pre-edge 1s $ \rightarrow $ 3d peak intensity compared to the spectrum of as-prepared LMCF, accompanied by a decrease of CN of the first Co-O shell (Fig. 2D, fig. S12, table S5). This signals oxygen loss and structural change as a result of interaction with the acidic electrolyte under the electric field, altering cobalt's centrosymmetric coordination (symmetry breakdown) and electronic structure (27). The CN is significantly lower than that in $ \mathrm{C o_{3} O_{4}} $ , indicating a higher fraction of tetrahedral coordinated $ \mathrm{C o}^{2+} $ combined with higher concentration of $ V_{0} $ , which serves as the nucleophilic site in promoting O-O bond formation (28). As the cell potential increases to initiate OER, the 1s $ \rightarrow $ 3d transition becomes more intense (Fig. 2A, inset). The average valence state of cobalt in LMCF was maintained at a lower value than that of $ \mathrm{C o_{3} O_{4}} $ (+2.67) under all the test potentials from +2.51 at 1.02 V to +2.32 at 1.70 V (29) (fig. S27). Simultaneously, Fourier transform of EXAFS spectra show the reduction of Co-O and Co-Co shell intensities (Fig. 2D). At a potential of 1.7 V when the OER reaction proceeds much more rapidly, XAS analysis shows shortening of the Co-O bond length (Fig. 2F, table S5), indicating a high degree of covalency contraction which positively affects the catalytic activity of the nanoparticles (28). A similar phenomenon was observed in an

MnO $ {2} $ film, where the Mn-O bond was shortened concomitant with accumulation of $ Mn^{3+} $ and $ V{0} $ under applied potential in acid (22). Shortened Co-O bonds during OER were also found in the amorphous cobalt oxide OER catalyst (CoCat) (30), except that Co was in the +4 state, distinct from our catalyst. Accompanying the rapid acceleration of OER at 1.7 V, a clear increase of the Debye-Waller factor (DWF) $ \sigma^{2} $ was also observed. DWF pertains to the motion of the coordinated atoms. In this case, it indicates increased Co-O vibration as the adsorbed $ H_{2}O $ is converted to $ O_{2} $ possibly involving the shuffling lattice oxygen in LMCF, as was recently observed in a perovskite OER catalyst (31). The participation of surface and lattice oxygen creates anisotropic displacement of the ligation to cobalt, causing an increase of 1s $ \rightarrow $ 3d transition intensity. The Co XANES and EXAFS spectra were nearly fully restored to their original intensities at the onset voltage (1.23 V) when the cell potential was returned to 1.02 V, suggesting the reversibility of the active site restructuring in LMCF. In contrast, XANES spectra at the Mn K-edge and La $ L_{III} $ -edges show little changes under different cell potentials (Fig. 2 B and C). Compared to Co, the EXAFS spectrum of Mn only showed a minor reduction of shell peak intensity at 1.23 V after the catalyst was immersed in the electrolyte. No apparent changes in shell structure and DWF were observed for Mn during OER (Fig. 2E, fig. S13). XANES and EXAFS analyses indicate that Mn and La do not participate in the electrocatalysis directly. Rather, their presence modifies the structure and activity of the cobalt site. To this end, we also investigated the in situ XAS at different potentials over the fibrous cobalt oxide catalyst prepared by the same method but in the absence of Mn and La (CF). The changes in XANES and EXAFS are significantly less dominant than that found in LMCF (fig. S28).

For Mn, the surface layer structure is merely 0.1 eV higher than that in the bulk, suggesting that it can displace Co anywhere in the system. For La, the energy is 3.09 eV lower at the surface than in the bulk. This agrees well with the STEM measurement of Mn and La distributions as shown in Fig.1, G and H, affirming their roles as dopants in enhancing oxide conductivity and surface defect/oxygen vacancies for better OER activity according to our design concept (32).

For cobalt spinel based OER catalysts operable in PEMWE, the most imposing challenge is stability in the acidic media. To better understand the enhanced acid tolerance of LMCF under electrolytic condition, we calculated the surface Pourbaix diagram of the La decorated $ \mathrm{C o_{3} O_{4}} $ (111) facet (Fig. 4A), as it represents the dominant facet in the LMCF observed by STEM (Fig. 1, F, G, I; fig. S6, D-E). The Pourbaix diagram consists of five regions, I: $ \mathrm{d i s L a^{0} _ H^{}} $ $ + 2 \mathrm{L a}^{3+} $ , II: $ \mathrm{d i s L a^{0} _ C o^{T} O O H^{}} $ $ + 2 \mathrm{L a}^{3+} $ , III: bri3OH_H $ ^{} $ , IV: bri3OH $ ^{} $ , and V: $ \mathrm{L a^{0} 3 O _ C o^{T} O^{}} $ , with their intermediate state configuration shown in Fig. 4B. Among these five phases, Phases I and II contain ionic $ \mathrm{L a}^{3+} $ and therefore are unstable and soluble, whereas Phases III, IV and V contain the surfaces of $ \mathrm{C o}^{2+} $ $ \mathrm{C o}^{3+} $ and $ \mathrm{L a}^{3+} $ bridged by OH $ ^{} $ , OH_H $ ^{} $ and O $ ^{} $ and exhibit relative stability against Co dissolution. Under low pH and low or negative potential, the surface La in the La doped $ \mathrm{C o_{3} O_{4}} $ (111) facet dissolves to form $ \mathrm{L a}^{3+} $ (Phase I, magenta). At potentials between 0.86 V and 1.1 V (RHE), the cation on the surface is oxidized and stabilized by OH $ ^{*} $ and the oxygen on the

第 15 页

surface is covered by $ \mathrm{H^{} } $ to form the stable bri3OH_H $ ^{} $ structure (Phase III). As the potential further increases to $ \geq 1.23\mathrm{V} $ (RHE), the surface is protected by $ \mathrm{OH^{} } $ to form a bri3OH $ ^{} $ stable structure (Phase IV). Under high pH and high potential, the surface of the La doped $ \mathrm{C o_{3} O_{4}} $ (111) facet tends to be oxidized by water, and the surface cations La and Co are protected by $ \mathrm{O^{} } $ by forming a stable structure $ \mathrm{L a^{0} 3 O_{-} C o^{T} O^{}} $ (Phase V).

The calculated Pourbaix diagram demonstrates that the stability of LMCF catalyst is defined by the combination of cell potential and pH. It also agrees well with our experimental observation. For example, under no electric field (potential = 0) at pH < 7, the catalyst is in Phase I and thermodynamically unstable in the acidic solution. During our RDE study in $ 0. 1 \mathrm{M} \mathrm{H C l O}_{4} $ （ $ \mathrm{p H}=1 $ ）at cell potentials between 1.5 V and 2 V, only a very small fraction of tests at low cell potential will overlap with Phase II, which could explain why the dissolution rate was higher in the RDE test. In our PEMWE test, the actual cell voltage runs from 1.5 V to 3.0 V, (between two red dashed lines) within the anodic pH between 2 to 4 (defined by the two vertical green dashed lines) in Fig. 4A. Therefore, the actual PEMWE anode operates in a window within these two boundaries, as marked by the area covered by the green diagonal stripes. This operating window falls within Phase V: $ \mathrm{L a}^{0} 3 \mathrm{O} _ {-} \mathrm{C o}^{T} \mathrm{O}^{*} $ , which is stable, thus offering a preliminary explanation of the stability of the LMCF catalyst in the PEMWE.

For comparison, we also calculated the Pourbaix diagram of the pure $ \mathrm{C o_{3} O_{4}} $ (111) facet and confirmed its weaker stability than the La-doped surface (fig. S30). We furthermore calculated Pourbaix diagrams of the LMCF (110) facet (fig. S31) and (100) facet (fig. S32), respectively. Both facets offer good stability under PEMWE operating conditions, according to the calculation. Our study revealed the critical role of surface La in stabilizing multiple $ \mathrm{C o_{3} O_{4}} $ facets against corrosion under working PEMWE condition.

Another critical issue in electrocatalysis is the inherent electron conductivity of the oxide itself. Our calculation of LMCF shows that substituting a low concentration of $ \mathrm{C o}^{3+} $ ions with uniformly distributed $ \mathrm{M n}^{3+} $ ions in the $ \mathrm{C o}{3} \mathrm{O}{4} $ lattice induces two partially occupied defect states in the mid-bandgap (Fig. 4, C and D). The Mn induced electron wave function overlaps significantly with neighboring Co ions, causing obvious dispersion and hence good electron mobility. This provides a direct enhancement of bulk-based electron conductivity, which combined with the connectivity of the nanofibrous oxide network, offers an overall high conductivity value for LMCF. The improved electronic conductivity was further confirmed experimentally by the 4-probe van der Pauw method (fig. S9).

Outlook. This study offers prospective directions and design insight for the future development of PGM-free OER catalysts for hydrogen production using PEMWE technology. For example, the catalytic activity enhancement can be further explored by increasing the surface functional group density through elemental doping, primary size control and morphology innovation. The PEMWE durability can be improved by removing the electrochemically unattached oxide, therefore limiting metal ion dissolution due to the lack of electro-potential stabilization. Fundamental understanding of the OER mechanism with respect to mononuclear versus binuclear reaction intermediates and catalytic pathways will help to guide the precursor and catalyst designs for lower overpotential and better acid tolerance (33, 34). These improvements offer paths to the next-generation PGM-free OER catalysts as viable replacements for precious metals such as iridium.

Materials and Methods

Catalyst synthesis

Materials: All the chemicals including $ \mathrm{C o(NO_{3}){2}.6 H{2}O} $ ( $ \geq99.9\% $ ), 2-methylimidazole ( $ \geq98\% $ ), Polyacrylonitrile (PAN), 5% Nafion solution (Nafion $ ^{ \circ} $ 117 solution), $ \mathrm{L a(NO_{3}){3}.xH{2}O} $ ( $ \geq99\% $ ), $ \mathrm{M n(NO_{3}){2}.4 H{2}O} $ ( $ \geq99\% $ ), isopropyl alcohol (IPA, $ \geq99.7\% $ ), methanol ( $ \geq99.9\% $ ), Ir black (99.9% , powder) and $ \mathrm{C o_{3} O_{4}} $ (99.5% , nanopowder, $ < 50 $ nm) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and used directly without further purification. DI water was supplied by a Millipore system (Millipore Q).

Synthesis of $ \mathrm{C o(m I m)}{2} $ (Co-ZIF) and LaMn@Co-ZIF: Co-ZIF was synthesized according to the reported method (36). Typically, 2-methylimidazole (2.25 g) was dissolved in 80 ml of methanol, followed by addition of methanol solution (50 ml) containing $ \mathrm{C o(N O{3}){2}\cdot 6 H{2} O} $ (1.00 g). The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 20 min to form a homogeneous violet solution, which was then sealed and kept for 12 hours at room temperature. The violet precipitate was collected by centrifugation, washed with methanol thrice and dried under vacuum at $ 60^{\circ} \mathrm{C} $ overnight. For the synthesis of LaMn@Co-ZIF, the process was essentially the same as that of Co-ZIF, except that the lanthanum (La) and manganese (Mn) were introduced during the ZIF synthesis. Typically, 2-methylimidazole (2.25 g) was dissolved in 80 ml of methanol, followed by addition of a mixture methanol solution (50 ml) containing $ \mathrm{C o(N O_{3}){2}\cdot 6 H{2} O} $ (1.00 g), $ \mathrm{L a(N O_{3}){3}\cdot 6 H{2} O} $ (0.53 g) and $ \mathrm{M n(N O_{3}){2}\cdot x H{2} O} $ (0.33 g). Lanthanum doped Co-ZIF without addition of the second metal, La@Co-ZIF, was also synthesized by using the same method.

Synthesis of porous cobalt oxide nanofiber (CF) and La/Mn co-doped porous cobalt oxide nanofiber (LMCF): ZIF-derived CF and LMCF were prepared via electrospinning (19), followed

by post-activation under air. As-synthesized LaMn@Co-ZIF powder was added into DMF at a concentration of 50 mg ml $ ^{-1} $ , followed by a planetary ball-milling at 600 rpm for 6 h to produce ZIF suspended slurry. A DMF solution containing PAN at a concentration of 100 mg ml $ ^{-1} $ was added into the ZIF slurry at the volume ratio of 1:1, followed by ball-milling at 400 rpm for 0.5 hour to make the final precursor slurry, which was subsequently electrospun to polymeric nanofiber at the electrostatic voltage of 9 ~ 10 kV with the distance between the needle and the collection plate at ~15 cm. The LaMn@Co-ZIF/PAN composite nanofiber was transferred into a ceramic boat and placed in a temperature-programmed tube furnace. The sample was heated to $ 360 \, \mathrm{^\circ C} $ at a ramp rate of 3 $ ^{\circ C} $ min $ ^{-1} $ under flowing air and was maintained at that temperature for 6 h. The elemental analysis showed atomic ratio of Co:Mn:La = 80:8:12 in LMCF. The CF and LCF nanofibrous oxides were synthesized by the same method.

Electrocatalytic measurement

Catalyst ink was prepared to apply over RDE as the OER working electrode for testing. Briefly, 4 mg of catalyst was dispersed in a mixture solution containing 0.2 ml water, 0.44 ml 2-propanol and 4 ul 5% Nafion solution, followed by sonicating for 1 h to form the catalyst ink, of which 10 ul was applied to the pre-polished RDE with a gold disk (PINE, 5 mm diameter, 0.196 $ cm^{2} $ ) resulting in a catalyst loading of $ \sim 0.26\pm 0.03\mathrm{mg}\mathrm{cm}^{-2} $ after air dry. For Ir black coated RDE, the catalyst loading was $ \sim 0.23\pm 0.03\mathrm{mg}\mathrm{cm}^{-2}. $

Electrolysis experiments were performed using a CHI electrochemical station (model 760C) in a standard three electrode cell, which was composed of working electrode, reference electrode $ \mathrm{(H g / H g S O_{4}} $ ), and counter electrode (gold wire). Cell potential was calibrated to the reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) reference. All potentials in this study referred to RHE calibrated against saturated hydrogen reference electrode (HydroFlex $ ^{\circledR} $ /gaskatel GmbH). Cyclic voltammetry (CV)

scans for the initial activity were measured in oxygen saturated 0.1 M $ \mathrm{H C l O_{4}} $ or 0.5 M $ \mathrm{H_{2} S O_{4}} $ solution after the electrode was activated at a sweep rate of 50 mV $ s^{-1} $ for 20 cycles from 1.0 to 2.0 V until a stable voltammogram curve was obtained. The OER catalytic activity was measured by LSV at room temperature at 1600 rpm and a scan rate of 10 mV $ s^{-1} $ . LSV curves were corrected with 95 % iR-compensation. The reported data are based on at least three independent replicates. For catalyst stability study, the catalyst was first subject to an initial break-in in $ \mathrm{N_{2}} $ saturated 0.1 $ \mathrm{M H C l O_{4}} $ with potential cycling from 1.0 V-2.0 V at room temperature until the electrode is stabilized. The $ \mathrm{N_{2}} $ is then substituted by $ \mathrm{O_{2}} $ while potential cycling from 1.0 V-2.0 V continued at a scan rate of 100 mV $ s^{-1} $ with the rotating speed of 1600 rpm for up to 14 000 cycles. LSV curve was recorded in every 2000 cycles. Chronopotentiometry data were collected at fixed current density of 10 mV $ \mathrm{cm}^{-2} $ in 0.1 $ \mathrm{M H C l O_{4}} $ aqueous electrolyte. The catalyst loading was 0.9 mg $ \mathrm{cm}^{-2} $ for chronopotentiometry study.

The oxygen produced during OER over LMCF catalyst was quantitatively measured by an in situ gas chromatography (GC) with two separate columns (Agilent D990 Micro GC) and a syringe sampling GC (Shimadzu, Nexis GC-2030) to analyze the Faradaic efficiency (FE) of $ \mathrm{O_{2}} $ formed from the water splitting. The integrated GC signal intensities for the analytes of interest (e.g. $ \mathrm{O_{2}} $ $ \mathrm{CO_{2}} $ $ \mathrm{CO} $ ) were first calibrated using the commercial gas standards of known concentrations analyzed and provided by the supplier. The experiments were performed in an electrochemical H-cell consisted of a LMCF coated working electrode, a gold wire counter electrode and a $ \mathrm{H g / H g_{2} S O_{4}} $ reference electrode in 0.1 M $ \mathrm{H C l O_{4}} $ electrolyte. An ultrahigh purity argon gas (99.999%) with an accurately calibrated flowrate of 20 ml $ \mathrm{m i n}^{-1} $ was used to purge the H-cell. For the in situ micro-GC, two channels were applied, one has zeolite column that is efficient in separating $ \mathrm{O_{2}} $ $ \mathrm{N_{2}} $ and CO. The other one has a polymeric absorbent column that is efficient in

第 32 页

separating Ar and $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ . The Micro GC is connected directly to the effluent flow of a tightly sealed half-cell reactor where the OER catalysts were tested at different potentials/current densities. The gas sampling was carried out after the air was nearly completely purged out. For the syringe analysis, the gas sample was drawn after extensive purging of Ar. For both analyses, baseline measurements were first established before the OER was turned on. The OER gas samples were collected at four constant current densities of $ 1 0 \mathrm{m A c m}^{-2} $ $ 2 0 \mathrm{m A c m}^{-2} $ $ 3 0 \mathrm{m A c m}^{-2} $ and $ 5 0 \mathrm{m A c m}^{-2} $ , respectively. The FE of oxygen formation from each OER current density can be determined by averaging four measurements using the following equation:

$$ F E _ {O 2} = \frac {4 F * n _ {O 2}}{Q} $$

Where F is the Faraday constant, $ n_{O_{2}} $ is the number of moles of $ O_{2} $ produced measured by GC, Q (C) is the charge passed through the cell at the specific constant current in the measurement time.

The turnover frequency (TOF) at a given potential was determined according to Equation 2 (37):

$$ \mathrm {T O F} = \frac {J \times A}{4 \times F \times m} $$

Where J $ \mathrm{(m A c m^{-2})} $ is the current density measured at the applied potential, A is the geometric area of gold disk electrode (GDE), F is the Faraday constant $ \mathrm{(9 6, 4 8 5 C m o l^{-1})} $ m is the mole number of metal on the electrode, and the number 4 refers to four electrons per $ \mathrm{O_{2}} $ . The TOFs were calculated assuming that all the metal ions were catalytically active, therefore representing the lower limit of the activity.

MEA fabrication and PEMWE cell test

The MEAs were prepared by using the catalyst coated membrane (CCM) method with LMCF catalyst at the anode, commercial Pt/C (40 wt.% BASF) at the cathode, and Nafion-115 or Nafion-212 proton exchange membrane as the electrolyte. The anode and cathode inks were prepared by ultrasonically blending catalyst with Nafion $ ^{\circ} $ solution, DI water and isopropanol followed by air-sprayed directly onto the Nafion membrane of $ 5-\mathrm{c m}^{2} $ geometric areas. The Pt loading at the cathode was fixed at $ 0.4 \mathrm{m g}_{\mathrm{P t}} \mathrm{c m}^{-2} $ , and LMCF loading at the anode was controlled between 1.0 mg $ \mathrm{c m}^{-2} $ to 2.0 mg $ \mathrm{c m}^{-2} $ for different tests. The Nafion $ ^{\circ} $ ionomer-to-catalyst ratio was ~0.4 for both anode and cathode. After spraying, the catalyst-coated membranes were hot pressed at 300 pounds for 2 min at $ 120^{\circ} \mathrm{C} $

The PEMWE cell consists of a gold-coated titanium current collector at the anode and a graphite plate at the cathode with serpentine flow channels of $ 5-\mathrm{c m}^{2} $ area on both sides. The MEAs were sandwiched between a platinum-plated titanium porous transport layer (PTL) in the anode compartment, and a Freudenberg H23C6 gas diffusion layer (GDL) in the cathode compartment. At least four independent MEAs were tested for replication. The MEAs were tested as they were fabricated without further activation. The polarization voltages were supplied by two potentiostats with current limits of 2 amps and 15 amps and the cell temperature was maintained at $ 60 \mathrm{~}^0 \mathrm{C} $ or $ 80 \mathrm{~}^0 \mathrm{C} $ under atmospheric pressure, respectively. The pre-heated de-ionized water was pumped into anode and MEAs were conditioned by holding the cell at a current density at 0.01 $ \mathrm{A c m}^{-2} $ for 2 hours while the operating temperature was ramped up to the operating value. During the accelerated stress test (AST), the cell was subject to multiple square-wave voltage sweeps between 1.4 V and 1.7 V at a scan rate of 300 mV $ \mathrm{s}^{-1} $ with 10 second dwell time at each voltage for up to 10 000 cycles. The polarization was taken at a 100-cycle interval for the first 3000 cycles, and then at 1000 cycle interval until reaching 10 000 cycles. For the galvanostatic test of the catalyst

durability, PEMWE current density was set at the designated values while the cell voltage was measured as the function of time.

ICP-OES/MS measurements and lifetime estimation

The elemental composition of as-prepared LMCF was analyzed by inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) (PerkinElmer Avio 500). The transition metal elements leached into an electrochemical cell containing 40 ml of $ 0. 1 \mathrm{M} \mathrm{H C l O}_{4} $ electrolyte during a galvanostatic stability test at $ 1 0 \mathrm{m A} \mathrm{c m}^{-2} $ were quantitatively analyzed by a separate ICP-OES (Agilent 5110). Solution containing multi-elements at the concentration of 0.01 ppm, 0.02 ppm, 0.03 ppm, 0.05 ppm and 0.4 ppm were used for calibration. At various designated time intervals, 1 ml of electrolyte was taken for analysis and cell was replenished by fresh electrolyte at the same

第 37 页

amount. The leached element concentration of the loaded catalyst was calculated using the following equation:

$$ C = \frac {V \cdot a \cdot C _ {I C P (M)} + a \cdot \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {M - 1} C _ {I C P (i)}}{b \cdot m} $$

where C is the weight ratio of the leached element; $ C_{ICP(M)} $ is the concentration of metal leached into the electrolyte measured by ICP; a is the dilution factor. V is the volume of ICP solution; b is the weight ratio of metal in the catalyst. m is the total loading mass of the catalyst. The calculation is based on the cumulated metal concentration in the electrolyte measured by ICP-OES.

The stability number $ \left( \mathrm{S}{1} \right) $ is calculated based on the ratio of the molar of evolved oxygen (calculated from $ Q{\mathrm{total}} $ ) to the molar of dissolved Co using the equation below (25):

$$ S _ {1} = \frac {n _ {O 2 (O E R)}}{n _ {C o (d i s s o l v e d)}} $$

And the catalyst lifetime was estimated based on the following equation (25):

$$ t = \frac {S _ {1} \times Z \times F \times m}{j \times M} $$

where t is the lifetime of the catalyst, $ S_{1} $ is the stability number, Z is the number of electrons per evolved $ O_{2} $ , F is the faraday constant, m is the loaded mass of Co (g $ cm^{-2} $ ), j is the applied current density (A $ cm^{-2} $ ), M is the molar mass of Co. The leaching rate of Co during the 81-hour testing was calculated to be $ \sim1.9 $ wt.% , which led to an estimated lifetime of LMCF of ca. $ 4800\pm 500 $ h.

The activity-stability Factor (ASF) was calculated based on equation below (26):

$$ A S F = \frac {J - S _ {2}}{S _ {2}} | _ {\eta} $$

where $ \eta $ is the overpotential, J is the current density, $ S_{2} $ is the dissolution current density.

In the in-situ ICP-MS (Agilent ICP-MS 7850) measurement to check whether metal leached out from the MEA in the operating PEMWE, solution containing multi-elements at the concentration of 0.00 ppb, 5.00 ppb, 20.00 ppb, 100.00 ppb were used for Co calibration, and 0.00 ppb, 0.20 ppb, 1.00 ppb, 5.00 ppb, 20.00 ppb were used for Mn and La calibrations, respectively. In the designed time interval, the outlet of the anode side was connected to ICP-MS instrument for the test. The leached element concentration from the MEA in the operating PEMWE single cell was calculated using the following equation:

$$ C = \frac {v \cdot t \cdot C _ {I C P (M)}}{b \cdot m} $$

where $ C_{\mathrm{ICP(M)}} $ is the concentration of metal leached from the anode of the MEA in the operating PEMWE cell measured by ICP. $ v $ is the flow rate of water in and out of the cell. $ t $ is the collecting time. b is the weight ratio of metal in the catalyst. m is the total loading mass of the catalyst. The calculation is based on the instant metal concentration sampled in the constant DI effluent flow by ICP-MS.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculation.

The plane-wave package PWmat (39) based on GPU was used for density functional calculations. The norm-conserving pseudopotentials (40) and GGA functional, Perdew-Burke Erzenhof (PBE) functional with exchange-correlation corrections (41), provided by NCPP-SG15 PBE in PWmat website was used in all calculations. The plane wave cutoff energy for

第 39 页

wavefunction is set to 60 Ryd, and the cutoff energy for the soft charge density and the potential is 120 Ryd. The self-consistent field (SCF) convergence criterion of $ 5 \times1 0^{-5} $ eV was chosen in SCF calculations. The spin-polarized DFT calculations with specified initial magnetic moment in the input files were conducted, and the constraint on magnetic moment was also applied when necessary. To correct the strong on-site Coulomb repulsion between the 3d electrons of Co, the DFT+U approach was then employed by introducing a Hubbard-like repulsion term $ (U_{\mathrm{eff}}=U-J) $ (42). Various $ U_{\mathrm{eff}} $ values were tested by calculating the electronic and magnetic properties of the crystal $ \mathrm{C o}{3} \mathrm{O}{4} $ . Our calculations show that the $ U_{\mathrm{eff}} $ values of 3.5 and 2.5 eV for octahedral $ \mathrm{C o}^{3+} $ and tetrahedral $ \mathrm{C o}^{2+} $ atom respectively, which are able to produce results that agree well with the experimental observations. In addition, the $ U_{\mathrm{eff}} $ value of 3.5 eV was also found to be able to reproduce the experimental results by other researchers (43). For Mn, a $ U_{\mathrm{eff}} $ value of 3.68 eV was chosen based on the previous report (44). For surface structure calculations, including surface Pourbaix diagrams, implicit solvent model was used to mimic the dielectric effect of water.

The Crystallographic Information Files (CIFs) files used for DFT Pourbaix diagram calculations of La doped cobalt spinel at (111) (110) and (100) facets can be found at Reference (35).
