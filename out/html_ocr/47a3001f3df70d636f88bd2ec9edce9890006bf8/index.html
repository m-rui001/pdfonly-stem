# $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ electrolysis to multi-carbon products in strong acid

Abstract

$ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ electroreduction $ \left( \mathrm{C O_{2} R} \right) $ is being actively studied as a promising route to convert carbon emissions to valuable chemicals and fuels. However, the fraction of input $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ that is productively reduced has typically been very low, $ < 2 \% $ for multicarbon products; the balance reacts with hydroxide to form carbonate in both alkaline and neutral reactors. Acidic electrolytes would overcome this limitation, but hydrogen evolution has hitherto dominated. Here we report that concentrating potassium cations in the vicinity of electrochemically active sites accelerates $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ activation to enable efficient $ \mathrm{C O_{2} R} $ in acid. We achieve $ \mathrm{C O_{2} R} $ on copper at pH $ < 1 $ with a single pass $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ utilization of 77%, including a conversion efficiency of 50% toward multicarbon products (ethylene, ethanol, and 1-propanol) at a current density of $ 1.2 \mathrm{~A/cm}^{2}. $ The full cell voltage is 4.2V.

第 3 页

Main Text

Electrochemical reduction of $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ $ (\mathrm{C O_{2} R}) $ using renewable electricity offers an attractive approach to produce widely needed chemicals and feedstocks while mitigating greenhouse gas emissions (1, 2). Effort has been dedicated to developing catalysts that achieve high faradaic efficiency (FE) toward carbon monoxide and formate, and to promoting C-C coupling toward multi-carbon $ (\mathrm{C}_{2+}) $ products such as ethylene and ethanol (3-6). Lowering overpotentials of these reactions and increasing their productivity (current density) have been priorities for the field (7, 8).

Despite many recent advances, $ \mathrm{C O_{2} R} $ remains far from practical viability because strong local alkaline conditions are present (Fig.1A). Rather than being reduced, a major fraction of the input $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ is instead consumed in the electrolyte through reaction with $ \mathrm{O H^{-}} $ to produce $ \mathrm{C O_{3}}^{2-} $ (eq.1):

$$ 2 \mathrm {O H} ^ {-} _ {(\mathrm {a q})} + \mathrm {C O} _ {2} _ {(\mathrm {g})} \rightarrow \mathrm {C O} _ {3} ^ {2 -} _ {(\mathrm {a q})} + \mathrm {H} _ {2} \mathrm {O} _ {(\mathrm {a q})} $$

Carbonate formation imposes a limit of carbon utilization efficiency (fraction of $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ in the input feed converted to $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ R products) that is prohibitively low (9). For every $ \mathrm{H_{2}O} $ /electron pair transferred during the $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ R, one hydroxide is produced at the cathode, reacting with $ \frac{1}{2} $ $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ to form carbonate. As a result, the maximum carbon efficiency is 50% for two-electron-transfer processes such as $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ to CO.

For $ \mathrm{C O_{2} R} $ to more valuable $ \mathrm{C_{2+}} $ products, the effect is even more acute: the carbon efficiency of $ \mathrm{C O_{2} R} $ to ethylene/ethanol is limited to 25% as six electrons are needed per $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ reacted. In practice, due in part to non-unity selectivity and use of alkaline electrolyte, the carbon efficiency is even lower than these best-case theoretical limits (Fig.1B) (7,10-15).

Dealing with $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ loss in alkaline and neutral environment leads to a severe energy penalty if one seeks to recycle the emitter $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ from carbonate or cathodic and anodic streams (16). Techno-economic analysis (TEA) of alkaline $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ electrolyzers shows that over 50% of input energy is used to regenerate $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ lost to carbonate (Fig.1C, tables S1-S2; details in Supplementary Materials). $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ electrolyzers employing neutral electrolyte produce a local alkaline environment under operating conditions, and thus also suffer from carbonate formation and crossover (17,18). The problem of inefficient $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ utilization in $ \mathrm{C O_{2} R} $ is central to the field and severely limits its prospects (9). While advances in FE and current density have been steady, the utilization challenge demands a new approach.

第 4 页

$ \mathrm{C O_{2} R} $ in acidic media offers an avenue to reduce carbonate formation to near-zero, and thus also eliminate $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ crossover (Fig.1D,1E). Specifically, when $ \mathrm{H_{3} O^{+}} $ is the proton source for $ \mathrm{C O_{2} R} $ no $ \mathrm{O H^{-}} $ is generated and $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ conversion can proceed without carbonate formation; when $ \mathrm{H_{2} O} $ is the proton source, any carbonate generated locally will lie within the diffusion layer and be converted back to $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ by protons in bulk electrolyte (19). Initial tests using phosphate buffer electrolytes (pH 1 to 4, detailed preparation methods in Supplementary Materials) showed no measurable loss of $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ to the anode at 400 mA/cm $ ^{2} $ over 6 hours compared to a loss of $ \sim 70\% $ of input $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ in the reference case with bicarbonate electrolyte (Fig.1F, fig. S1). However, under acidic conditions, the kinetically more favorable hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) outcompetes the reduction of $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ , with $ \mathrm{C O_{2} R} $ FE close to zero in strong acids (pH <1). These results are in agreement with past work on acidic $ \mathrm{C O_{2} R} $ , in which only single-carbon products, such as CO and methane, were observed in electrolytes of elevated pH in the range 3 to 5 (19-25).

We herein pursued a cation augmenting strategy to improve $ \mathrm{CO_{2}} $ activation kinetics in strong acid (pH <1). We used high-concentration phosphate buffer (total phosphorus species kept to 1 M) as electrolyte to keep pH locally at the cathode as close as possible to the bulk (26). Modelling of reaction and diffusion of species within a typical diffusion layer of 50 $ \mu\mathrm{m} $ indicates that, in the phosphoric acid $ \mathrm{(H_{3}PO_{4},1 M,pH1.05)} $ electrolyte, the surface (distance to cathode = 0) pH is similar to the bulk at current densities $ < 200 \mathrm{mA/cm^{2}} $ while becoming neutral and alkaline when current densities increase further (Fig. 2A, fig. S2, tables S3-S5; details in Supplementary Materials). The locally alkaline conditions result from a consumption rate of local protons that exceeds mass transport of protons from bulk (27). Despite elevated pH at the surface, pH decreases to acidic range within a short distance of the cathode. Even at a current density as high as $ 1 \mathrm{A/cm^{2}} $ the pH decreases to 6.3 $ \mathrm{(pKa_{1}} $ of carbonic acid) within 33 $ \mu\mathrm{m} $ of the electrode. This confinement assures that any locally generated carbonate would be converted back to $ \mathrm{CO_{2}} $ for ensuing reduction, avoiding carbonate crossover and the associated loss of reactant $ \mathrm{CO_{2}} $ . In comparison, similar conditions (pH 6.3 at distance to cathode = 30 $ \mu\mathrm{m} $ ) are reached at much lower current densities $ (< 200 \mathrm{mA/cm^{2}}) $ in electrolytes of pH 2 to 4 (fig. S2). In the interest of realizing economic $ \mathrm{CO_{2}} $ electrolyzers (28), we focus this study on high-rate $ \mathrm{CO_{2}} $ electrolysis in strong acid $ \mathrm{(pH \leqslant1)} $

To circumvent the kinetically more favorable HER in acid, we sought to operate $ \mathrm{C O_{2} R} $ at current densities where the $ \mathrm{H_{3}O^{+}} $ mass-transport limitation occurs and $ \mathrm{H_{2}O} $ becomes the main

proton donor at the cathode surface (19, 22). Modelling shows that the surface pH approaches neutrality when the current density reaches 100 mA/cm $ ^{2} $ for electrolytes with pH 2 to 4 or above $ 200\mathrm{mA/cm}^{2} $ for electrolyte with pH 1 (Fig. 2B). Indeed, we observed experimentally a marked decrease of HER selectivity in electrolytes with pH 2 and 4 at current density range of 100 to 400 $ \mathrm{mA/cm}^{2} $ ; however, no measurable $ \mathrm{CO_{2}R} $ products were detected in electrolyte with pH 1, even if the current density were $ 400\mathrm{mA/cm}^{2} $ (Fig. 2C, fig. S3), at which the surface pH was modelled to be 7.

When we added 0.5 M KCl into the $ \mathrm{H_{3}PO_{4}} $ electrolyte (pH 1) in the middle of the reaction, however, we observed a slight decrease in FE toward hydrogen, and detected $ \mathrm{CH_{4}} $ (FE 2.1 $ \pm 0.3\% $ ) at a current density of $ 200\mathrm{mA/cm^{2}} $ (fig. S4). Noting that the phosphate buffer electrolytes with different pH were prepared by mixing $ \mathrm{H_{3}PO_{4}} $ and $ \mathrm{KH_{2}PO_{4}} $ at different ratios, we posited that ion species, particularly cations (29-31), might steer kinetics of $ \mathrm{CO_{2}R} $ catalysis at bulk $ \mathrm{pH} < 1. $

To test this hypothesis, we carried out Tafel analysis at different pH and found that the Tafel slope decreased along with an increase in $ \mathrm{K}^{+} $ concentration in the electrolytes (Fig. 2D). The slope reaches a minimum of 0.28 V/dec at an electrolyte composition of $ 1 \mathrm{M H}{3} \mathrm{PO}{4} $ with 3 M KCl. This result suggests that the change in Tafel slope is not attributable to pH and that the rate determining step (RDS) is the adsorption of $ \mathrm{CO}{2} $ (25). The presence of cations is key to $ \mathrm{CO}{2} $ activation on the catalyst surface. Previous studies attributed the enhanced activation achieved with cations to their electrostatic interactions with the electric dipole of adsorbates or changes of surface charge density (30, 31). We assessed this activation enhancement on silver catalysts: in $ 1 \mathrm{M H}{3} \mathrm{PO}{4} $ electrolyte, no $ \mathrm{CO}{2} $ R reactivity was observed, whereas $ \sim 50\% $ $ \mathrm{CO}{2} $ R selectivity was achieved in the presence of 3 M $ \mathrm{K}^{+} $ in the same electrolyte (fig. S5). The $ \mathrm{CO}{2} $ R selectivity on Cu catalysts was dependent on current density, and the FE of the main $ \mathrm{CO}{2} $ R product, $ \mathrm{CH}_{4} $ , reached a maximum of 27% at 600 mA/cm $ ^{2} $ (Fig. 2E).

We studied the impact of local pH (equivalent to concentration of protons accessible to $ \mathrm{C O_{2} R} $ and HER) on the activation of $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ and suppression of HER. At current densities $ < 2 0 0 \mathrm{m A / c m^{2}} $ in which the local pH in 1 M $ \mathrm{H_{3} P O_{4}} $ electrolyte exhibits pH $ < < 7 $ , the addition of $ \mathrm{K^{+}} $ into the electrolyte did not affect voltametric properties of the Cu electrode regardless the concentration of $ \mathrm{K^{+}} $ and the atmosphere $ \mathrm{(N_{2} \mathrm{~v s. C O_{2}})} $ (fig. S6), suggesting that $ \mathrm{K^{+}} $ does not play a role in the activation of $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ , nor did it suppress HER (from proton reduction) in a locally acidic environment.

第 6 页

However, when operating at a higher current density of $ 4 0 0 \mathrm{m A / c m^{2}} $ to deplete local protons (local $ \mathrm{p H} > 7 $ ), adding $ \mathrm{K}^{+} $ , while not affecting applied potentials, tuned selectivity from HER (from water reduction) to $ \mathrm{C O_{2} R} $ : the change of gas flow from $ \mathrm{N_{2}} $ to $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ increased the $ \mathrm{C O_{2} R} $ partial current density from 0 to nearly $ 2 0 0 \mathrm{m A / c m^{2}} $ and decreased HER partial current density by approximately the same amount, while we did not observe any $ \mathrm{C O_{2} R} $ activity in the electrolyte without $ \mathrm{K}^{+} $ (fig. S7). These observations indicate a full mechanistic picture for $ \mathrm{C O_{2} R} $ in bulk acidic electrolyte: under proton-depletion local environment, the cation triggers $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ activation, which suppresses the HER from water reduction, consistent with prior reports (19, 32).

The effect of anions on $ \mathrm{C O_{2} R} $ reactivity is not significant: substitution of $ \mathrm{S O_{4}}^{2-} $ or $ \mathrm{I}^{-} $ for $ \mathrm{C l}^{-} $ showed similar product distribution to $ \mathrm{C l}^{-} $ case (fig. S8). It is unlikely that the $ \mathrm{K}^{+} $ affects the oxidation state of Cu catalysts: only metallic Cu was observed by operando X-ray absorption spectroscopy (fig. S9).

We examined $ \mathrm{C O_{2} R} $ product distribution in 1 M $ \mathrm{H_{3} P O_{4}} $ with different concentrations of $ \mathrm{K^{+}} $ and current density remained constant at 400 mA/cm $ ^{2} $ (Fig. 2F). The HER selectivity decreased with an increase in $ \mathrm{K^{+}} $ concentration, and $ \mathrm{C O_{2} R} $ selectivity increased. Selectivity for $ \mathrm{C H_{4}} $ was the highest, at $ \sim 2 8 \% $ , for 1 M $ \mathrm{K^{+}} $ . The FE toward $ \mathrm{C_{2} H_{4}} $ , though not dominant, increased steadily from 3.1% with 0.5 M $ \mathrm{K^{+}} $ to 9.3% with 3 M $ \mathrm{K^{+}} $ .

We sought to steer selectivity further toward $ \mathrm{C_{2} H_{4}} $ in view of its high value and broad application in chemical manufacturing (33). However, the solubility of $ \mathrm{K}^{+} $ in aqueous electrolytes is limited while maintaining a low pH. We turned to the enrichment of $ \mathrm{K}^{+} $ at the Cu surface by a cation-augmenting layer (CAL). We employed a cationic perfluorosulfonic acid (PFSA) ionomer composed of tetrafluoroethylene and sulfonyl fluoride vinyl ether. The acidic $ -\mathrm{S O_{3} H} $ group is expected to exchange its protons with $ \mathrm{K}^{+} $ from the bulk electrolyte in a non-acidic local environment, sustaining a high $ \mathrm{K}^{+} $ concentration at the catalyst surface (Fig. 3A). In addition, the CAL allows cation (e.g., $ \mathrm{H}^{+} $ and $ \mathrm{K}^{+} $ ) transport in the direction from electrolyte to catalyst surface while retarding $ \mathrm{O H^{-}} $ diffusion out, leading to higher surface pH that was reported to facilitate C-C coupling (10,15,34). The ionomer was loaded onto the sputtered Cu surface as a blend with carbon nanoparticles (NPs) in order to increase its adhesion to the catalyst (fig. S10; details in Supplementary Materials).

第 7 页

The CAL-modified Cu showed a further increase of FE toward $ \mathrm{C_{2} H_{4}} $ to 13% and a much lower FE toward $ \mathrm{CH_{4}} $ of <1% compared to the bare Cu catalyst, while the remaining $ \mathrm{CO_{2} R} $ gaseous product was CO at a current density of $ 400 \mathrm{mA/cm^{2}} $ in $ 1 \mathrm{M H_{3} P O_{4}} $ with 3 M KCl (Fig. 3B). The product selectivity shift was attributed to electrostatic interactions of cation species (e.g., $ \mathrm{K^{+}} $ ) with the electric dipole of specific adsorbates that favors $ \mathrm{C_{2+}} $ reaction pathways (31, 35). The FE toward $ \mathrm{C_{2} H_{4}} $ was around 10% for current densities in the range 300 to $ 800 \mathrm{mA/cm^{2}} $ (fig. S11). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) showed a marked increase of potassium on the CAL-modified Cu surface compared with the bare Cu after $ \mathrm{CO_{2} R} $ operation (fig. S12), confirming the preservation of $ \mathrm{K^{+}} $ by the ionomer layer.

To improve $ \mathrm{C O_{2} R} $ productivity still further, we increased the electrochemically active surface area of the electrode by forming a Cu-NPs/PFSA composite material (fig. S13; details in Supplementary Materials) (7, 36). Similar to the case of bare Cu, the $ \mathrm{C O_{2} R} $ selectivity was dependent on the bulk concentration of $ \mathrm{K}^{+} $ in 1 M $ \mathrm{H_{3} P O_{4}} $ : the FE toward $ \mathrm{C_{2} H_{4}} $ increased from around 10% with 1 M $ \mathrm{K}^{+} $ to 26% with 3 M $ \mathrm{K}^{+} $ at a current density of 1.2 A/cm $ ^{2} $ (Fig. 3C, fig. S14). The overall $ \mathrm{C O_{2} R} $ selectivity reached 61% including a total $ \mathrm{C_{2+}} $ FE of 40%.

Employing a slim, low-resistance flow cell (fig. S15), we operated the high-surface-area CALmodified Cu electrode at a current density of 1.2 A/cm $ ^{2} $ stably for an initial 12-hour test (details in Supplementary Materials). The full-cell voltage was 4.2 V (without iR compensation) and the FE toward $ \mathrm{C_{2}H_{4}} $ was constantly $ \sim 25\% $ (Fig. 3D, fig. S16). In-depth XPS analyses indicate that K distributed evenly across the top layer of the composite electrode after $ \mathrm{CO_{2}R} $ reaction (fig. S17). The percentages of Cl and P were $ 5\times $ lower than that of K, suggesting that the observed K concentration was sustained by the ionomer, not caused by residual electrolyte salts.

$ \mathrm{C O_{2} R} $ in acid enables $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ electrolysis without carbonate formation and crossover in bulk electrolytes, circumventing the $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ utilization limit that is fundamental to neutral and alkaline systems, and permitting a carbon efficiency that is capable of increasing further in the direction of unity. To reduce energy demand of product separation from dilute streams (37), we pursued singlepass carbon efficiency (SPCE) toward the new theoretical limit.

By gradually reducing the flow rate of $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ from 50 to 5 standard cubic centimeters per minute (sccm), the $ \mathrm{C_{2+}} $ FE was improved to 48% (31% toward $ \mathrm{C_{2} H_{4}} $ , 12% toward $ \mathrm{C_{2} H_{5} O H} $ , 4% toward

$ \mathrm{C_{3}H_{7}OH} $ , and 1% toward $ \mathrm{CH_{3}COOH} $ (fig. S18). This combination of current density and selectivity results in a high overall $ \mathrm{C_{2+}} $ productivity of $ 600\mathrm{mA/cm^{2}} $ (Fig. 3E).

By further lowering the flow rate of $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ to 3 sccm, we achieved, at a current density of 1.2 $ \mathrm{A/cm^{2}} $ an SPCE of $ \sim 77\% $ for all the $ \mathrm{C O_{2} R} $ products, including $ \sim 50\% $ for $ \mathrm{C_{2+}} $ products (Fig. 3F). This outperforms previously-reported alkaline and neutral $ \mathrm{C O_{2} R} $ electrolyzers (Fig. 1B, table S6).

The cation augmentation takes $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ electrolysis from high-pH neutral and alkaline electrolytes to pH <1 acidic environment. The approach overcomes the problems, otherwise present, of carbonate formation and $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ crossover. A voltage breakdown analysis shows that the full-cell voltage required to operate in this acidic system is comparable to that in the state-of-art neutral MEA cells (fig. S19) (7). We note that further improvement in selectivity and a decrease in operating voltage will be required on the path to energy-efficient electrochemical $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ conversion.
