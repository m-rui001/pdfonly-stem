Abstract: Discovered in 2005, CAACs are among the most nucleophilic ( $ \sigma $ -donating) and also electrophilic ( $ \pi $ -accepting) stable carbenes known to date. These properties allow them to activate a variety of small molecules and enthalpically strong bonds, to stabilize highly reactive main group and transition metal diamagnetic and paramagnetic species, and to bind strongly to metal centers, which gives rise to very robust catalysts. The most important results published up to 2013 are briefly summarized, while the majority of this review focuses on findings reported within the last three years.

1. Introduction

Thirty years ago, when the first singlet carbenes were isolated, $ ^{[1,2]} $ they were regarded as laboratory curiosities. Nowadays, stable carbenes are recognized to be among the most powerful tools in organic, inorganic and organometallic chemistry, and they have even found medicinal and material science applications. $ ^{[3]} $ Many different types of singlet carbenes have been isolated I-XXXVIII $ ^{[4-38]} $ (Figure 1). All of them, except cyclopropenylidenes XXXIV $ ^{[34]} $ and mesoionic carbenes XXXVI $ ^{[36]} $ feature either a phosphorus or a nitrogen directly bonded to the carbene center. For many years, imidazolin-2-yldenes XII, $ ^{[13]} $ imidazol-2-yldenes XIII, $ ^{[2]} $ and 1,2,4-triazol-5-yldenes XVI, $ ^{[16]} $ the so-called N-heterocyclic carbenes (NHCs), were by far the most studied. $ ^{[39]} $ More recently, another type of carbene, namely cyclic (alkyl) (amino)carbenes (CAACs) have attracted considerable attention, and have already been the subject of several review articles. $ ^{[40,41]} $ Herein, in each section, we first briefly discuss the most important results published from their discovery in 2005 $ ^{[42]} $ up to 2013, while the majority of this review is devoted to findings published within the last three years.

2. Synthesis of CAACs

So far, CAACs have always been prepared by deprotonation of the corresponding cyclic iminium salts (Scheme 1). These salts were initially synthesized by reacting readily available aza-allyl anions with epoxides, followed by addition of triflic anhydride. $ ^{[42]} $ Then, we developed the hydro-iminiumation route, $ ^{[43]} $ which involves the addition of HCl to readily available alkenyl aldimines, followed by heating; this straightforward protocol allows for kilogram-scale syntheses of CAAC precursors. For both routes, a variety of $ R^{1} $ and $ R^{2} $ substituents are compatible, but only aryl groups are tolerated on the nitrogen, as N-alkyl-substituted aza-allyl anions react via the nitrogen center. Limitations also exist for the deprotonation of the cyclic iminium salts. Due to the weak acidity of the aldiminium proton, a quaternary carbon is required at the $ \alpha $ -position of nitrogen, otherwise deprotonation leads to azomethine-ylides. $ ^{[44]} $ Similarly, deprotonation of the aryl substituent on nitrogen can occur, although the carbene is presumably the kinetic product. Indeed, deprotonation of a cyclic iminium salt bearing a 2,6-diethylphenyl group at nitrogen does not allow for the isolation of the free carbene. However, when the same reaction is performed in the presence of a metal fragment, the corresponding CAAC metal complexes are formed. $ ^{[45]} $ This short analysis explains the prevalence of the 2,6-diiso propylphenyl (Dipp) group.

3. Electronic properties of CAACs

It is first important to note that the electronic properties of CAACs differ significantly from those of NHCs (imidazolin-2-ylidenes XII and imidazol-2-ylidenes XIII). The substitution of one of the two electronegative and $ \pi $ -donating amino substituents of NHCs by a $ \sigma $ -donating alkyl group makes CAACs more nucleophilic ( $ \sigma $ -donating) and also more electrophilic ( $ \pi $ -accepting) than NHCs. These differences were quantified by computational studies, which found that the HOMO of CAACs is slightly higher, and the singlet-triplet gap slightly smaller, than those of NHCs (HOMO: $ \sim $ -4.9 versus -5.2 eV; $ \Delta E_{S/T} $ : $ \sim $ 45 versus 68 kcal.mol $ ^{-1} $ ）(Figure 2). $ ^{[51]} $

Experimentally, the Tolman electronic parameters $ ^{[54]} $ derived from (CAAC)- and (NHC)-Rh(CO) $ {2} $ Cl and -Ir(CO) $ {2} $ Cl complexes indicate that donation of CAACs is only slightly greater than that of NHCs. $ ^{[41,55]} $ However, it is important to note that the TEP value is an indication of the overall donating properties of a ligand (i.e. $ \sigma $ -donation minus $ \pi $ -acidity). Recently, several methods have been developed to assess the $ \pi $ -accepting properties of ligands, and when combined with the TEP value, they allow for an estimate of the $ \sigma $ -donation. One of these methods is based on the $ ^{31} $ P NMR chemical shift of readily available phenylphosphinidene-carbene adducts (See Section 6, Scheme 19). $ ^{[56]} $ Two extreme canonical forms have to be considered. Resonance form A features a genuine P=C $ \pi $ -bond, resulting from full $ \pi $ -back donation, and accordingly displays a low field $ ^{31} $ P NMR chemical shift (Figure 3). By contrast, in form B the two electron lone pairs remain at phosphorus giving rise to a signal in the high field region of the spectrum. Within the set of cyclic carbenes that have been investigated, it can be concluded that the $ \pi $ -accepting ability of CAACs is among the strongest, and is only surpassed by diamidocarbenes XVIII and XXIV. Note that, unlike the TEP values, large differences of $ ^{31} $ P chemical shifts are observed (>150 ppm). $ ^{[56,57]} $ Shortly after our group reported the "P-scale," Ganter et al. established a ranking of the $ \pi $ -accepting ability of carbenes using the $ ^{77} $ Se NMR chemical shift of carbene-selenium adducts. $ ^{[58]} $ A similar order of carbenes' electrophilicity was obtained. This methodology is advantageous when the

4. CAACs in group-1 and -2 element chemistry

A handful of group-1 and -2 CAAC complexes have been isolated. $ ^{[61]} $ Even beryllium has been explored, which has been described by Dutton and Frenking as "the least investigated chemical element of the first octal row of the periodic table except inert neon." $ ^{[62]} $ Braunschweig et al. $ ^{[63]} $ have shown that the reduction of a (CAAC) $ \mathrm{BeCl_{2}} $ complex in the presence of a CAAC afforded the $ (\mathrm{CAAC}){2}\mathrm{Be} $ adduct 1, in which the beryllium atom is in the formal zero oxidation state (Scheme 5). This is the first example of a neutral compound containing a zero-valent s-block metal. Interestingly, computational studies predicted that NHCs were potentially the best ligands to stabilize a Be(0) as the tricoordinated derivative $ (\mathrm{NHC}){3}\mathrm{Be}. $ $ ^{[64]} $ However, despite several attempts, all complexes that has been prepared feature a Be in the oxidation state +2. $ ^{[65]} $ The superiority of CAACs to access Be(0) complexes is due to their stronger $ \pi $ -accepting properties. Note that theoretical studies predict that Mg(0) complexes are synthetically unviable $ ^{[64a]} $ with classical ligands (CO, $ \mathrm{PR}_{3}, $ NHC); however it remains to be proven that CAACs cannot do it.

5. CAACs in group-13 element chemistry

The chemistry of group-13 elements is dominated by compounds in which the element adopts the +3 oxidation state and act as a Lewis acid. To compensate for its intrinsic electron-deficiency, boron often participates in multicenter bonds, and numerous compounds involving hypervalent boron centers are known. The first uses of CAACs in boron chemistry can appear paradoxical since they consisted of preparing nucleophilic organoborons, $ ^{[66]} $ including tricoordinated boron anions, $ ^{[67]} $ and low-valent boron derivatives.

Indeed, in 2011, we reported the preparation of the first stable neutral, nucleophilic, tricoordinate boron derivative $ 2^{[6 6,6 8]} $ (Scheme 6). This compound, which is isoelectronic with amines and phosphines, was prepared by reduction of a CAAC- $ \mathrm{B B r}{3} $ adduct in the presence of a CAAC. Similarly, Braunschweig et al. have recently shown that reduction of a CAAC-B(CN) $ \mathrm{B r}{2} $ adduct in the presence of triethylphosphine led to $ (\mathrm{C A A C})(\mathrm{P E t}{3})\mathrm{B C N} $ 3. $ ^{[6 9]} $ Interestingly, when the reduction was carried out in the absence of phosphine the tetramer 4 was obtained, which upon addition of an NHC afforded the $ (\mathrm{C A A C})(\mathrm{N H C})\mathrm{B C N} $ derivative 5. The same group also reported that addition of isonitriles to the $ [( \mathrm{C A A C})\mathrm{B} ]{2} $ adduct 6 gave rise to 7. Note that the same reaction using the analogous bis(NHC) $ \mathrm{B}{2} $ adduct led to decomposition. $ ^{[7 0]} $ Although this route has a narrow scope for the preparation of $ (\mathrm{L}{1})(\mathrm{L}{2})\mathrm{B R} $ compounds, this is good illustration of the very different electronic properties of NHCs and CAACs. We have developed a stepwise, more versatile, synthesis that allows for the isolation of several unsymmetrically substituted derivatives of the type $ (\mathrm{L}{1})(\mathrm{L}_{2})\mathrm{B R} $ 8. $ ^{[7 1]} $ Importantly, Kinjo et al. $ ^{[7 2]} $ have recently

6. CAACs in group-14 element chemistry

6.1. Stabilization of carbon radicals

As early as 1997, Fukuzumi et al. $ ^{[86]} $ reported the first example of a carbene-stabilized carbon radical (Scheme 11). They demonstrated that the electrochemical oxidation of an enolate derived from a thiazolylidene produced the corresponding neutral radical 23, which is persistent for several hours at room temperature. Inspired by this work, in 2013 we prepared, isolated and crystallographically characterized the oxyallyl radical cation 24 $ ^{[87]} $ based on an anti-Bredt NHC. $ ^{[22]} $ Since, prior to this work, oxyallyl radicals were only observed in inert matrices at low temperatures or in the gas phase, $ ^{[88]} $ we concluded that a variety of carbene stabilized organic radicals should be isolable. $ ^{[89]} $ However, in 2004 Clyburne et al. $ ^{[90]} $ showed that the reduction of protonated imidazol-2-ylidenes is irreversible and produces the free carbene and $ \mathrm{H}_{2} $ , indicating that the corresponding neutral radical 25 is highly reactive. Thus when comparing the electronic properties of thiazolylidenes, anti-Bredt NHCs, and imidazol-2-ylidenes, the logical conclusion is that $ \pi $ -accepting carbenes are the most appropriate to stabilize radicals; therefore CAACs are good candidates.

Scheme 11. $ \pi $ -accepting carbenes are the most appropriate to stabilize radicals.

Indeed, we found that carboxy radicals 26, including polyradicals 27, derived from CAACs exist as monomers both in solution and in the solid state (Scheme 12). $ ^{[91]} $ These radicals can be stored at room temperature for years under inert atmosphere

without apparent decomposition. Even more striking, when a sterically hindered electron withdrawing carboxy substituent is present, their half-life in air reaches up to several days. $ ^{[92]} $ Even radical 28, featuring the electron withdrawing and moderately bulky $ \mathrm{C_{6} F_{5}} $ group, can be handled in a solution opened to air for several hours without significant decomposition. This is in marked contrast with other (amino)(carboxy)radicals which readily undergo either dimerization as exemplified by (dimethylamino)(carboxy) radicals, $ ^{[93]} $ or fragmentation and rearrangements as observed for the carboxy radical 29 derived from the bis(diiso propylamino)carbene. $ ^{[94]} $

These results suggest general guidelines for the design of highly persistent (amino)(carboxy)radicals, primarily by emphasizing the key advantage of cyclic scaffolds. Their rigidity prevents rearrangements, and offers an optimal $ \pi $ -donation of the amino group thereby allowing for a fully conjugated NCCO fragment with a planar local environment.

9. CAACs in transition metal catalysis

While the propensity of NHC ligands to improve the catalytic activity of transition metal centers has been well acknowledged over the years, $ ^{[39]} $ significantly fewer reports deal with CAAC complexes. However, it has already been shown that the peculiar steric and electronic properties of CAACs improved known processes and even allowed for the discovery of new ones. In 2005, we found that (CAAC)PdCl(allyl) complexes promote the $ \alpha $ -arylation of propiophenone with aryl chloride under much milder conditions (room temperature, and up to 7,000 TON) $ ^{[42]} $ than phosphine and NHC palladium complexes (A) (Scheme 24). 11. Conclusion and Perspectives

Starting with the work of Dumas $ ^{[197]} $ in 1835, and for most of the $ 20^{\mathrm{th}} $ century, scientists struggled to isolate carbenes under ambient conditions. The lessons learned from the many great pioneers: Curtius, $ ^{[198]} $ Staudinger, $ ^{[199]} $ Meerwein, $ ^{[200]} $ Doering, $ ^{[201]} $ Breslow, $ ^{[186]} $ and Wanzlick, $ ^{[202]} $ to cite a few, ultimately allowed for the preparation of stable carbenes. $ ^{[1,2]} $ Nowadays, many carbenoid species are readily available and belong to the day-to-day armamentarium of chemists. One of the most recent developments can appear paradoxical since it consists of using carbenes to stabilize highly reactive species. For this task, CAACs appear to be very effective as demonstrated by the isolation of a variety of allegedly unstable organic, main group and transition metal diamagnetic and paramagnetic species. This is due to their lowlying LUMO and small singlet-triplet gap. These electronic properties also allow free CAACs to activate small molecules and enthalpically strong bonds, and therefore to imitate, to some extent, transition metals. To fully compete with the latter, it

第 41 页

remains to be seen if they can operate as catalysts. However, they shine when used in conjunction with metals, to which they bind strongly, giving rise to very robust catalysts capable of working under very harsh conditions. The (CAAC)Au(I)-promoted hydroamination reactions with ammonia and hydrazine, and the (CAAC)Ru(II)-catalyzed ethenolysis of the seed-oil derivative methyl oleate, $ ^{[203]} $ well-illustrate the high impact of CAACs in homogeneous catalysis. One recent development is the use of CAACs to isolate transition metal complexes that were postulated to be key catalytic intermediates, but believed to be only short-lived. $ ^{[204]} $ The availability of these reactive species allows for a better understanding of known catalytic reactions but, more importantly, paves the way for the rational design of new ones. Looking ahead, CAACs should find practical applications beyond academia. Some premises can be found in recent publications. For example, the group of Radius $ ^{[205]} $ has prepared a (CAAC)Co(CO) $ ^{2} $ (NO) complex as a precursor for thin-film deposition, which is a rapidly evolving area in integrated circuit manufacturing. Bochmann et al. $ ^{[206]} $ have reported that (CAAC)CuX complexes exhibit high photoluminescence, and could be used in LED devices. We recently initiated collaborative projects aiming to explore the use of (CAAC)Au complexes for their antitumor activities on various cancer cell lines, a study whose preliminary results are extremely encouraging.

The last few years have witnessed the development of facile and cheap synthetic methods for the preparation of CAACs and their corresponding late transition metal complexes. Combined with the commercial availability of some CAAC precursors, and with the growing number of research groups utilizing CAACs, it is safe to predict that applications in and around the fields of chemistry will only continue to increase.
