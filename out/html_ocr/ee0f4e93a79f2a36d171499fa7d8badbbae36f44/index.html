Sulphide kesterite $ \mathrm{C u_{2} Z n S n S_{4}} $ provides an attractive low-cost, environmentally benign and stable photovoltaic materials, yet the record power conversion efficiency has been stagnant at around 9% for years. Severe non-radiative recombination within the heterojunction region is one of major causes limiting voltage output and overall performance. Here, we report a certified 11% efficiency $ \mathrm{C u_{2} Z n S n S_{4}} $ solar cell with a high 730 mV open-circuit voltage using a heat treatment to reduce heterojunction recombination. This heat treatment facilitates elemental inter-diffusion, directly inducing Cd atoms to occupy Zn or Cu lattice sites, and promotes Na accumulation accompanied by local Cu deficiency within the heterojunction region.

第 2 页

Consequently, new phases are formed near the hetero-interface and more favourable conduction band alignment is obtained, contributing to reduced non-radiative recombination. Using this approach, we also demonstrate a certified centimetre-scale $ (1.11\mathrm{cm}^{2}) $ 10% efficiency $ \mathrm{Cu_{2}ZnSnS_{4}} $ photovoltaic device; the first kesterite cell (including selenium-containing) of standard centimetre-size to exceed 10%.

The large and long-term future deployment of photovoltaics demands stable, abundant and non-toxic materials similar to silicon. Pure sulphide kesterite $ \mathrm{C u_{2} Z n S n S_{4}} $ (CZTS), a quaternary compound semiconductor, has emerged as an excellent candidate for such next generation photovoltaic technology. It has attracted attention due to its non-toxicity, high efficiency and low-cost potential $ ^{1,2} $ . Kesterite belongs to the diamond-type structural chalcogenide family, and can be regarded as a direct derivative of commercialised CdTe and $ \mathrm{C u I n_{x} G a_{1-x} S e_{2}} $ (CIGSe) cell technologies. These are both known for their favourable optical and electronic properties and demonstrated $ > 2 2 \% $ efficiency $ ^{3} $ , suggesting a high efficiency potential for kesterite $ ^{4} $ . In addition to the use of low-cost abundant materials, most reported efficient CZTS solar cells have been produced using industrially viable sputtering methods developed for CIGSe, which will facilitate upscaling this technology for the future commercialisation.

Photovoltaic devices fabricated from the absorber CZTS are among the most efficient of the earth-abundant, non-toxic thin film solar cells under investigation at present. The bandgap of CZTS is 1.5eV, close to the optimal bandgap required for a single junction solar cell. More importantly, the bandgap of CZTS can be easily tuned in a wide range (1.5eV~2.1eV) when alloying with other elements such as Ge or Ag, making it well matched to the high bandgap requirement for the uppermost cells for Si-based tandem cell stacks $ ^{1,5} $ . In this regard, development of high performance of CZTS cells may be considered a key prerequisite for a successfully developing PV to its full potential, given the stability and toxicity issues with contending tandem cell options.

Despite its promise, the highest reported efficiency for CZTS has been stagnant at around $ 9 \% $ for years $ ^{5,6} $ . This is far lower than its detailed balance limiting efficiency of $ 33 \% $ $ ^{7} $ and the efficiency demonstrated by counterpart high bandgap $ \mathrm{C u I n_{x} G a_{1-x} S_{2}} $ (CIGS) cells $ ^{8} $ , mainly due to a large $ \mathrm{V_{O C}} $ deficit $ (\mathrm{E g / q - V_{O C}})^{4} $ . Several factors have been suggested for this $ \mathrm{V_{O C}} $ deficit, including a large number of charged defects associated with band tailing $ ^{4,9} $ , an undesirably thick $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ layer $ ^{10,11} $ at the rear of the CZTS cell, co-existence of secondary phases $ ^{11,12} $ and unfavourable band alignment at the CZTS/CdS heterojunction interface $ ^{13} $ . These problems simultaneously cause non-radiative recombination within CZTS solar cells, limiting $ \mathrm{V_{O C}} $ and efficiency. To deal with some of these problems, we have developed an approach using an ultra-thin $ \mathrm{A l_{2} O_{3}} $ layer at the rear of the CZTS cell to reduce $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ thickness and to simultaneously limit the decomposition of the CZTS in this region, which often causes the formation of voids and secondary phases $ ^{14,15} $ . A combined SnS and S sulfurization atmosphere has been introduced during the annealing of precursor layers to reduce SnS loss $ ^{16} $ , supress Sn related defects (deep level defects for kesterite $ ^{17,18} $ ) and inhibit defects at the CZTS surface $ ^{19} $

These combined efforts enabled the improvement of our baseline CZTS cell efficiency to $ \sim 8\% $ (the base-line CZTS cell configuration is with a $ \sim 60 $ nm thick CdS buffer layer $ ^{14,19-} $ $ ^{21} $ . Despite these efforts, the thermal activation energies deduced from the temperature-dependent $ V_{\mathrm{OC}} $ study for reported high efficiency CZTS cells were still much lower than the estimated cell bandgap $ ^{22-26} $ . In addition, the ideality factors of CZTS cells with respectable efficiencies ( $ \sim 8\% $ ) are generally above 1.5 or even above two in some cases $ ^{14,19,21,26} $ , much higher than those of other high-efficiency thin film PV technologies (with a typical ideality factor of around 1.3) $ ^{27,28} $ . These features suggest that non-radiative recombination in the CZTS/CdS heterojunction region, including at the p-n hetero-interface and in the very top part of CZTS within the space charge region (SCR), likely dominates the performance loss of our CZTS cells, leading to a large Voc deficit.

In this work, by employing post-heat treatment of the heterojunction, we demonstrate CZTS solar cells of certified efficiency >10% for both standard square centimetre-sized cell

第 4 页

and nonstandard sized cell, which appear to be the highest for $ \mathrm{C u_{2} Z n S n S_{4}} $ cells of each size to date $ ^{3} $ . Indeed, a centimetre-scale CZTS solar cell exceeding the 10% efficiency barrier sets a record efficiency for any kesterite solar cell of similar size, whether pure sulphide material or by incorporating $ \mathrm{S e}^{3} $ . The heat treatment of the CZTS/CdS heterojunction greatly reduces the heterojunction non-radiative recombination, confirmed by time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL), temperature-dependent $ \mathrm{V_{O C}} $ and $ \mathrm{S u n s- V_{O C}} $ measurements. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and Raman measurement reveal the formation of new phases because of the heat-treatment induced elemental inter-diffusion. In addition, secondary ion mass spectrometry (SIMS) confirms the Na accumulation within the heterojunction and atom probe tomography (APT) reveals Na containing nanometre-scale clusters along with Cu-deficiency, indicating the likely formation of a $ \mathrm{C u_{2-x} N a_{x} Z n S n S_{4}} $ phase, which is thermodynamically possible providing the Na content is low. Consequently, a more favourable band alignment at the p-n interface is established.

Device performance

The baseline process for fabricating CZTS solar cells is based on two key steps: co-sputter deposition of Cu/SnS/ZnS precursor followed by a high temperature sulfurization annealing. Additional refinements were ultra-thin $ \mathrm{A l_{2} O_{3}} $ layers that were deposited prior to the absorber precursor deposition and sulfurization annealing that was conducted in a combined SnS plus S atmosphere, yielding CZTS solar cells of around 8% baseline efficiency $ ^{14,19} $ . The heat treatment (HT) processes were performed immediately after the chemical bath deposition of CdS on top of the CZTS absorber. We tried a wide range of processing temperatures, from $ 2 0 0^{\circ} \mathrm{C} $ to $ 5 0 0^{\circ} \mathrm{C} $ Treatment at temperatures around $ 3 0 0^{\circ} \mathrm{C} $ gave the best and most reproducible performance results and was therefore favoured for comprehensive analysis. The traditional CdS hetero- structure for CIGS and CZTS was used, with full details presented in the methods section.

After the optimized HT process, the efficiency increased from $ \sim 8 \% $ (without HT) to over 10% (with HT), as shown in Figure 1a (Supplementary Figure 1 for device performances

第 5 页

at different HT temperatures). The average $ \mathrm{V_{OC}} $ of CZTS cells was significantly improved from ~670 to around 730 mV, attributing to reduced non-radiative recombination since the dark saturation current density $ \mathrm{J}{0} $ (single diode model) is decreased by four orders of magnitude after HT (see Table 1). The decrease in ideality factor A from 2.61 to 1.44 suggests that recombination within the SCR has been reduced. The FF followed a similar trend to $ \mathrm{V{OC}} $ increasing from 54% to 65% on average, which can be attributed to the higher $ \mathrm{V_{OC}} $ lower value of A and a reduced series resistance $ (\mathrm{R_{S}}) $ . The short-circuit current density $ \mathrm{J_{SC}} $ only shows a slight increase upon heat-treatment (from 20.7 to 21.7 mA/cm $ ^{2} $ ), arising from an improved long wavelength response (See Supplementary Figure 2). A bandgap reduction from 1.51 to 1.50 eV is estimated by fitting EQE curves, widening the absorption spectrum. The performance of the most efficient device was independently certified by the National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL) under standard test conditions. The measured current density-voltage and external quantum efficiency (EQE) characteristics are shown in Figure 1c and 1d, respectively. The certified power conversion efficiency of this $ 0.23 \mathrm{~cm}^{2} $ CZTS cell is 11.01%, with corresponding Voc of 730.6 mV, Jsc of 21.74 mA/cm $ ^{2} $ and FF of 69.27%. This is the highest certified efficiency so far for a sulphide $ \mathrm{Cu_{2} Z n S n S_{4}} $ solar cell. This significant efficiency improvement over the previous CZTS 9.5% record (with Voc of 673mV, Jsc of 21.2 mA/cm $ ^{2} $ FF of 66.3%) $ ^{29} $ lies in the improvement from all three of these key parameters. With the same approach, we also obtained larger area $ (1.113 \mathrm{~cm}^{2}) $ CZTS cell with certified efficiency of 10.04% (Voc of 708.3 mV, Jsc of 21.77 mA/cm $ ^{2} $ and FF of 65.13%). To the authors' best knowledge, this is the first certified result breaking the 10% efficiency barrier for standard sized kesterite cell $ (\mathrm{Area}>1 \mathrm{~cm}^{2} $ , See Supplementary Figure 3 and 4). $ ^{3} $ We also measured a 10.8% efficient CZTS cell without encapsulation after 225 days (storage in air). The Voc and Jsc do not show significant degradation, despite of some degradation in FF. This decrease in

Previously other groups have tried similar heat-treatment on CZTS/CdS stacks (See Table 2). $ ^{6,30,31} $ The efficiency improvement seems to be highly dependent on the quality of CZTS absorber which is known to vary under different processing conditions. Recombination analysis

We applied a variety of characterisation techniques to understand the underlying mechanisms responsible for the dramatic efficiency improvement from HT. At first, we conducted TRPL measurements on the CZTS devices with and without HT using two excitation wavelengths (470 nm and 640 nm). From our knowledge of the light absorption coefficient of CZTS (Supplementary Figure 6), the excitation by the 470 nm laser generates carriers mostly at the p-n junction interface and in the very top region of the CZTS absorber, whereas 640 nm laser generates carriers more uniformly through the bulk. The TRPL decay curves for the two excitation wavelengths are shown in Figure 2a and 2b respectively; CZTS with HT shows a significantly slower decay compared to that without HT by using excitation of 470 nm laser. By contrast, these decay curves are very similar if 640 nm laser excitation is used. By fitting the TRPL spectra with a bi-exponential function model $ ^{32} $ , the effective lifetime of CZTS with HT was estimated to be 5.4 ns, which is more than double that achieved without HT when using 470 nm excitation, whereas the extracted lifetime is similar at 3.4-3.6 ns when using the 640 nm excitation. This suggests that the heat-treatment significantly affects the recombination in the heterojunction region rather than deep in the CZTS bulk.

Further evidence is obtained from Suns- $ V_{\mathrm{O C}} $ , which measures $ V_{\mathrm{O C}} $ as a function of incident light intensity $ ^{33} $ . The obtained Suns-Voc data were fitted by the two-diode model as follows:

$$ \begin{array}{l} J = J _ {L} - J _ {0 1} \left(\exp \left(\frac {V _ {o c}}{V _ {T}}\right) - 1\right) - J _ {0 2} \left(\exp \left(\frac {V _ {o c}}{2 V _ {T}}\right) - 1\right) - \frac {V _ {o c}}{R _ {s h}} \\ S u n s = \frac {J _ {L} \left(l i g h t i n t e n s i t y\right)}{J _ {L} \left(o n e S u n\right)} \tag {2} \\ \end{array} $$

$ V_{T} $ is the thermal voltage, $ J_{01} $ and $ J_{02} $ the saturation current for n = 1 and n = 2 diodes respectively, and $ \mathrm{R_{SH}} $ the shunt resistance. Electrical parameters extracted from the fitting are shown in Figure 2c and Supplementary Figure 7. $ J_{01} $ arises mainly from recombination within the neutral bulk CZTS, whereas $ J_{02} $ indicates recombination within the SCR $ ^{34} $ . Notably $ J_{01} $ does not change with the HT process, suggesting again this HT process has no obvious impact on the neutral bulk recombination. In contrast, $ J_{02} $ underwent a significant decrease with HT by almost one order of magnitude, indicating substantial suppression of recombination within SCR. This reinforces the hypothesis established by the previous TRPL analysis that the HT process mainly reduces non-radiative recombination within the SCR. The $ J_{0} $ values derived here are unaffected by $ \mathrm{R_{s}} $ since Suns-$ \mathrm{V_{OC}} $ involves no external current flow $ ^{33} $ . Interestingly, the pseudo FF with the HT process is increased by 4~5% despite lower $ \mathrm{R_{SH}} $ (Supplementary Figure 7) compared to results without HT. This suggests that the 4~5% increment in FF should result from a better p-n junction diode quality $ \left( \mathrm{n_{mpp}} \right) $ owing to the reduced SCR recombination. Note that applying further higher temperatures for HT process (300-330 $ ^{\circ} \mathrm{C} $ ) led to a deterioration in p-n junction diode quality, largely due to the increased non-radiative recombination, reduced $ \mathrm{R_{SH}} $ (Supplementary Note 1 and Supplementary Figure 7) and over-diffusion of Zn into CdS (Supplementary Figure 15 and Supplementary Table 2).

With a significant recombination reduction within the heterojunction region, it is interesting to understand what dominates device performance loss in our champion CZTS cells. We conducted temperature-dependent current-voltage measurements to obtain an activation energy $ \mathrm{E_{A}} $ by extrapolating the $ \mathrm{V_{O C}} $ to a temperature of $ 0 \mathrm{~K}^{3 5} $ . As shown in Figure 2d, the CZTS device with HT shows an $ \mathrm{E_{A}} $ of $ \sim1.25 $ eV, close to its optical bandgap (Eg) of 1.5 eV (estimated from EQE), whereas the device without HT gives significantly lower $ \mathrm{E_{A}} $ of $ \sim0.9 $ eV, far below its

optical band gap (Eg) of 1.51eV. This strongly suggests that interfacial recombination has been greatly reduced after the HT process, which again echoes the findings from TRPL and Suns $ \mathrm{V_{OC}} $ measurements.

Elemental inter-diffusion and aggregation

While we have ascertained that the non-radiative recombination within the heterojunction region has been greatly reduced, the next step is to investigate what changes occurred in SCR upon heat-treatment. XPS elemental depth profiles were conducted, as shown in Figure 3a. Clear elemental inter-diffusion occurs in the heterojunction region upon heat-treatment. On the CdS buffer side, Zn was found to have diffused into the CdS buffer with a concentration gradient depth of around 15 nm, which is about a quarter of the CdS thickness (about 60nm). On the CZTS absorber side, after HT process, a significant Cd tail is apparent, having a diffusion depth of around 200 nm; assuming the Ar etching rates for CdS and CZTS are similar. As $ \mathrm{Z n_{x} C d_{1-x} S} $ can be formed at similar temperature by $ \mathrm{S I L A R}^{2 0} $ or at temperature below $ 1 0 0^{\circ} \mathrm{C} $ by $ \mathrm{C B D}^{3 6,3 7} $ , we speculate that an ultra-thin $ \mathrm{Z n_{x} C d_{1-x} S} $ layer is formed at the CdS/CZTS interface region where there is much higher Zn content, with this requiring further investigation.

On the CZTS side, it is interesting to know how Cd interacts with CZTS. Hence highresolution TEM was performed. Figure 3b shows the bright field TEM image of the CZTS device with HT process with the corresponding device structure schematic on the left. We scrutinized the top CZTS region, which is closest to the CZTS/CdS interface using a highresolution high-angle annular dark field (HAADF) image. The contrast of atomic columns in the HAADF-STEM images is approximately proportional to the mean square atomic number $ \left(Z^{2}\right) $ of the constituent atoms $ ^{38,39} $ . Based on this reasoning, Figure 3c presents a filtered atomic resolution HAADF image taken at the region near the CdS/CZTS interface (See M1-M3 sites definition in Supplementary Note 2 and Supplementary Figure 8-9). From the enlarged image on the right, a clear contrast can be observed representing different cation columns. Figure 3d shows the intensity profile of a row of cations as marked by the red rectangle in Figure 3c. The

第 12 页

Sn columns on M1 sites can be clearly distinguished as their intensity is at least twice as strong as most of the M2 sites in between. This shows good consistency with the aforementioned $ Z^{2} $ dependence between Sn and Cu/Zn occupied atomic columns. Meanwhile, it is obvious that two columns on M2 sites show much stronger intensity than the rest of M2 sites and even higher than half the Sn intensity on the M1 sites. This is a strong indication of localized cation exchange by an element with larger Z number than Cu or Zn. The above M2 intensity variation is less likely due to Sn atoms being on $ \mathrm{C u / Z n} $ sites as they are thermodynamically unfavourable $ ^{1 7, 4 0} $ . Instead, it is highly likely due to Cd occupying the site of $ \mathrm{C u / Z n} $ . The direct atomic-level observation of M2 intensity variation showing only in the interface region (see Supplementary Figure 10) seems to be consistent with the XPS results, where Cd diffusion was clearly detected at the interface region.

This atomic-level finding also provides direct evidence for Cd diffusion from CdS into CZTS lattice occupying the Cu position, forming the $ \mathrm{C d_{C u}} $ anti-site and/or occupying Zn positions $ (\mathrm{C d_{Z n}}) $ forming $ \mathrm{C u_{2} C d_{x} Z n_{1-x} S n S_{4}} $ material. If Cd is on the Cu site, a $ \mathrm{C d_{C u}} $ antisite defect may form in the top part CZTS which would contribute to n-type doping $ ^{40} $ . If this is the case, the top region of CZTS would exhibit a weak p-type doping, or intrinsic or even n-type doping, which would usually enlarge the depletion region. However, this seems to contradict our measurements discussed later (doping, depletion region width from C-V and fermi level position with respect to the VBM by XPS). A large amount of Cd on Cu sites seems not to happen here.

If Cd were on Zn sites, $ \mathrm{C u_{2} C d_{x} Z n_{1-x} S n S_{4}} $ is expected to form which may be crossdetected by Raman scattering. After heat treatment, the Raman (514 nm excitation) peak of the surface absorber shows an obvious red-shift compared to results without heat treatment, indicating formation of the lower bandgap $ \mathrm{C Z C T S}^{2 1,4 1} $ after heat treatment (See Supplementary Figures 11-14). This might explain why the bandgap Eg, determined from EQE, shows a slight decrease after the HT process.

In addition to the significant elemental inter-diffusion between CdS and CZTS absorber, we also found that Na accumulates near the heterojunction interface region using SIMS. As shown in Figure 4a, much higher Na signals were detected in both the CdS and upper CZTS layers after the HT process. Previous publications reported that, after a post-heat treatment on either CZTS absorber $ ^{42} $ or full devices $ ^{43} $ , similar Na accumulation near the surface of the absorber was seen, leading to a higher doping of the kesterite absorber $ ^{42} $ . We conducted C-V measurements and found that the depletion width reduces and the doping of CZTS significantly increases after the HT process (Supplementary Figure 17). In order to understand the origin of higher doping, we compared the doping of CZTS, CZTS after HT and CZTS/CdS heterojunction after HT (Supplementary Table 6). Both the accumulation of Na and diffusion of Cd at top part of CZTS should contribute to a higher doping in agreement with previous reports $ ^{41,44} $ . Moreover, first principle calculation suggests Na is able to saturate dangling bonds at grain boundaries $ ^{45} $ . Thus, we speculate that excess Na near the hetero-interface can also saturate dangling bonds at the CdS/CZTS interface, thereby passivating this interface.

The specific distribution of Na accumulation within these regions were obtained by APT measurements on a sample after HT. Figure 4c shows a 3D atom probe reconstruction showing only Na ions. A high density of Na is observed in the top section of the tip, where the CdS layer is located. Within the absorber, Na can be found at grain boundaries (highlighted with a red arrow in Figure 4c) which is frequently observed and considered beneficial in chalcogenide solar cells $ ^{45} $ . Unexpectedly, we see some Na-accumulated clusters of about 5-10 nanometers in size visible within the CZTS absorber. A proxigram using the largest Na precipitates within the dataset (see Figure 4d with black arrow) shows there is a negative correlation between the concentration of Na and Cu, while concentrations of other elements such as Zn, Sn remain unchanged.

This correlation between the alkaline element and Cu seems similar to the effect from post deposition treatment by alkali elements in chalcopyrite absorber, which is reported usually beneficial to chalcopyrite device performance $ ^{46} $ . Our first-principles calculations showed that the formation enthalpy of Na dopant on the Cu site (Na replacing Cu) is very low (lower than 0.3 eV) and can be even negative if Cu is deficient. Therefore, it is thermodynamically possible to form $ \mathrm{N a_{x} C u_{2-x} Z n S n S( S e){4}} $ alloys with a low Na composition (the average x value in our case is around 0.07, see Supplementary Table 7), although high Na composition alloys are thermodynamically unfavourable. These Na-rich and Cu-deficient clusters can be considered as grains with the $ \mathrm{N a{x} C u_{2-x} Z n S n S( S e)_{4}} $ alloy composition. This new phase might have different electrical properties from previous publications on Na-doping effect $ ^{44} $ such as the doping density, dopant energy level and even bandgap, again requiring further investigation.

Band alignment study

To provide insight into the impact of HT on the band structure due to induced element interdiffusion, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements including core-level spectroscopy and valence band (VB) spectroscopy were conducted $ ^{4 7} $ . Figure 5a shows the XPS VB data of bulk CdS and CZTS, from which the valence band maximum (VBM) positions can be readily estimated $ ^{1 3} $ . Without HT, the VBM positions relative to Fermi-energy $ \left( \mathrm{E_{F}} \right) $ for CdS and CZTS resulted to be -1.48 eV and -0.36 eV, respectively, assuming the binding energy below $ \mathrm{E_{F}} $ is negative. After the HT process, the VBM positions relative to $ \mathrm{E_{F}} $ for CdS and CZTS changed to -1.40 eV and -0.27 eV, respectively. After HT, the Fermi-level of CZTS moves close to its VBM, suggesting the doping level of the CZTS had increased. This is consistent with the C-V measurements where the doping of CZTS nearly doubles after HT (Supplementary Figure 17).

If the bandgaps of bulk CdS and CZTS are assumed unchanged, with the band bending $ \left( \mathrm{V_{b b}} \right) $ estimated from the core-levels energy of each element at the interface as well as in bulk CdS and CZTS $ ^{47} $ (Supplementary Note 4 and Supplementary Figure 18), the CBO without and with HT can be estimated at -0.13 eV and -0.04 eV, respectively (See Figure 5b and c). This implies a more favourable band alignment after HT. However, the HT-induced Zn diffusion into CdS would increase its bandgap by elevating the CBM of the buffer near interface, whilst HT-induced Cd diffusion into CZTS decreases its bandgap by downwardly shifting both the CBM and VBM $ ^{13} $ . Therefore, upon heat-treatment, the CBO at hetero-junction is expected to take the form of a "cliff", shallower than -0.04 eV, or even correspond to flat band conditions (see Figure 5c). This more favourable band alignment may effectively reduce the hetero-

第 17 页

interface recombination $ ^{4 8} $ , likely contributing to higher $ \mathrm{E_{A}} $ . This agrees with the aforementioned TRPL and Suns-Voc analysis that the reduced recombination mainly takes place within the SCR. The cliff-like or flat-band CBO, together with the fact that $ \mathrm{E_{A}} $ is still smaller than the bandgap, suggests the current recombination may still be interface-dominated.

For future work, optimizing the band alignment and/or applying interface passivation with high bandgap dielectric materials are feasible to further reduce the heterointerface recombination. Absorber engineering by understanding band tail origins and defect properties, as well as cation substitutions and band grading are of high priority to further reduce Voc deficit and boost efficiency.

Conclusion

In summary, we have demonstrated the highest certified efficiency reported for sulphide kesterite CZTS solar cells with 11% for small area cells $ (0.23\mathrm{cm}^{2}) $ and 10% for a standard sized cell $ (1.11\mathrm{cm}^{2}). $ The latter 10% CZTS cell is also the first kesterite-type cell (including

第 18 页

selenium-containing) of standard centimetre-size to exceed 10% efficiency. The performance improvement of $ \mathrm{C u_{2} Z n S n S_{4}} $ solar cells is achieved by using a simple heterojunction heat treatment process. This approach enables elemental inter-diffusion, leading to a Zn gradient within CdS and a Cd gradient within CZTS, as well as a Na accumulation and local Cu-depletion at the heterojunction. The approach also leads to the formation of new phases: Cd diffusion into the lattice of CZTS surface forms a thin layer of $ \mathrm{C u_{2} C d_{x} Z n_{1-x} S n S_{4}} $ while Zn diffusion into the CdS likely forms ultrathin $ \mathrm{Z n_{x} C d_{1-x} S} $ ; and a new phase of $ \mathrm{C u_{2-x} N a_{x} Z n S n S_{4}} $ (average x~0.07) nanoclusters are revealed. Thanks to this elemental inter-diffusion, a more favourable band alignment is achieved and the recombination at heterojunction region is significantly reduced. The findings in this paper also suggest that all functional layers in the heterojunction devices are inter-related. The effect of any particular process heavily depends on the initial baseline quality and strategies that have been shown effective or non-effective in efficiency improvement need constant re-evaluation. With CZTS being stable, environmentally-friendly and low-cost, our work highlights significant research progress and the potential for further improving the efficiency of CZTS by reducing non-radiative recombination and further extending our understanding of this material.

Methods

Solar cell fabrication. All devices were deposited on a Mo coated glass, which has been cleaned with ammonia (7%) and DI water in sequence. CZTS precursors were deposited by co-sputtering Cu/ZnS/SnS material (thickness of 500 nm) using a magnetron sputtering system (AJA International, Inc., model). The DC power for Cu target (3 inch) was 23 W, whereas the RF powers for ZnS (3 inch) and SnS (3 inch) were 77 W and 53 W, respectively, in order to confine the Cu/Sn ratio within 1.7~1.8 and Zn/Sn ~1.2. The CZTS films were synthesized by sulfurization of the precursors using Rapid Thermal Processor (AS-One 100) within a combined sulfur and SnS atmosphere at $ 5 6 0 \, \mathrm{^ {\circ} C} $ for three minutes. PV parameters under different annealing and processing conditions are presented in Supplementary Figure 19, Supplementary Table 5, and Supplementary Table 9-10 in the supplementary information. The final thickness of CZTS film is around 800 nm. The ramping rate of sulfurization was controlled at $ 1 0^{\circ} \mathrm{C} $ per min, and the cooling rate was controlled at $ 3 0 \, \mathrm{^ {\circ} C} $ per min. The conventional cadmium sulfide buffer layer of thickness 50~60 nm was deposited by the CBD method. The chemicals during the reaction were $ \mathrm{C d S O_{4}} $ (0.16 mol/L), thiourea (1.6 mol/L), ammonia (1.2 mol/L). The duration of CBD process is 7 min at $ 8 0 \, \mathrm{^ {\circ} C} $ . Then,

第 19 页

the heterojunction heat treatments were conducted at $ 2 7 0^{\circ} \mathrm{C} $ $ 3 0 0^{\circ} \mathrm{C} $ and $ 3 3 0^{\circ} \mathrm{C} $ for ten minutes under $ \mathrm{N_{2}} $ atmosphere by using a two-zone tube furnace (MTI). After that, a thin high resistivity i-ZnO (50~60 nm) was deposited using RF sputtering at power of 90W (3 inch target) with a very slow power ramping rate, to protect the heterojunction from bombardment damage. Then an ITO ( $ \sim 2 4 0 $ nm) layer with sheet resistance better than $ 3 0 \, \Omega / \square $ was deposited using the AJA RF sputtering with power of 95 W using 3 inch target with pressure of 1.5 mTorr and gas flow rate (Ar) of 40 sccm.. Two stainless steel anti-templates (with designed of $ \sim 0. 2 4 \, \mathrm{c m^{2}} $ and $ \sim 1. 0 7 \, \mathrm{c m^{2}} $ area, respectively) were used for thermal evaporated Al patterning with a height of $ \sim 2. 5 \, \mu \mathrm{m} $ as the top contact for the device. The Al pattern and Al height were optimized according to our own ITO sheet resistance to reduce the Al shading as well as maintain low resistance for current collection. After that, a 91~100 nm $ \mathrm{M g F_{2}} $ layer was thermally evaporated as the anti-reflection coating (ARC). The ARC thickness was optimized with target wavelength centre of 550nm. The total areas of the final cells were either $ \sim 0. 2 3 \, \mathrm{c m^{2}} $ or $ \sim 1. 0 7 \, \mathrm{c m^{2}} $ defined by mechanical scribing.

Transmission electron microscopy. The TEM specimens were made using a focused ion beam (FIB) with a micro-sampling system (FEI xT Nova NanoLab 200). The surface of the specimen was finished with a gallium ion beam of 16 keV and 50 pA. The thickness of the specimen by FIB is around 100 nm. The electron microscope used in this study was a JEOL JEM-ARM200F, which is a 200 kV probe corrected scanning transmission electron microscope (STEM). At 200 kV the microscope features a resolution of <0.08 nm. The microscope is also equipped with a Cold Field Emission Gun with a resolution of 0.3 eV, which coupled with the GIF Gatan Quantum 963 SE imaging filter. Noisy contrast of the acquired images was removed using a Wiener filter and implemented with Gatan Digital- Micrograph version 3.1. Fast Fourier Transformation (FFT) of the images and low-pass filtering were also performed using the Digital Micrograph.

Time-resolved photoluminescence. Time-resolved photoluminescence (TR-PL) characterization was performed on the devices using time-correlated single photon counting (TCSPC) (Microtime200, Picoquant), with excitation wavelength of 470 nm and 640 nm, respectively.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy. The XPS measurements were conducted utilizing an ESCALAB250Xi (Thermo Scientific, UK) under ultra-high vacuum (better than $ 2 \times10^{-9} $ millibars). The X-ray source was monochromated Al Ka ( $ \mathrm{h v}=1 4 8 6. 6 8 $ eV) with binding energy scale calibrated using carbon reference. Depth profiling data were obtained by mildly sputtering the buffer/CZTS surface with mild Ar etching (at 1 keV) to minimise the influence from ion bombardment. over an etching area of $ 2. 5 \times2. 5 \mathrm{~ m m}^{2}. $

Solar cell performance measurements. The J-V curves were performed using a solar simulator (ABET IV Tester) with AM1.5G illumination (100 mW/cm $ ^{2} $ ) calibrated with a Fraunhofer WPVS reference cell (KG3 filter) reference. External quantum efficiency (EQE) measurements were conducted by utilizing a QEX10 spectral response system (PV measurements, Inc.) calibrated by the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) certified reference Si and Ge photodiodes.

Temperature dependent measurements. The samples are mounted on a gold-plated copper stage inside a liquid $ \mathrm{N_{2}} $ cryostat. A solar simulator (Newport) was used to illuminate the device at one Sun light intensity and an Agilent meter (B2912A) was used for the I-V data collection.

Capacitance measurement. C-V measurements were carried out using an impedance analyser at a frequency of 10 kHz with a DC bias voltage sweeping from -1.0 to 0.3 V.

Suns-Voc measurement. The Suns-Voc measurement is performed using a decaying light pulse with a full-width half maximum (FWHM) of about 2 ms from a xenon flash lamp, which enables the quasi-steady state photovoltage $ V_{\mathrm{OC}} $ to be measured across the p-n junction in the open-circuit condition. The light intensity is monitored by a single-crystal silicon reference cell plotted against Voc.

Atom probe tomography. Needle shaped specimens with a tip diameter of less than 100 nm were prepared via FIB-SEM sample preparation. To ensure the CZTS layer was located close to the apex of the specimen, simultaneous transmission Kikuchi diffraction (TKD) and energy dispersive x-ray spectroscopy (EDS) characterisation was carried out. The atom probe measurements were conducted at a temperature of $ \sim 5 8-6 0 $ K, with a pulse frequency of 250 kHz, a pulse energy of 50 pJ and a detection rate of 0.5 %. A proxigram is used to analyse the three-dimensional atom-probe data. Proxigrams are histograms that display compositional variations in three dimensions in the vicinity of an interface, with respect to the distance from the local normal of the interface. Distance zero corresponds to the position of the cluster surface, the negative distance to the outside of the cluster, and the positive distance to the inside of the cluster

Secondary ion mass spectrometry. Secondary Ion Mass Spectrometry (SIMS,Ca-meca IMS-5fE7) depth profiling was performed using a primary 15keV Caesium beam. Elemental composition information was collected from a 10 mm spot in the centre of the $ 1 8 0 \times1 8 0 \mathrm{~ m m}^{2} $ crater.
