# 2D MXenes: A New Family of Promising Catalysts for the Hydrogen Evolution Reaction

Guoping Gao, Anthony P. O'Mullane and Aijun Du $ ^{*} $

School of Chemistry, Physics and Mechanical Engineering, Science and Engineering Faculty, Queensland University of Technology (QUT), Gardens Point Campus, Brisbane, QLD 4001, Australia

ABSTRACT

Developing highly conductive, stable, and active non-precious hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) catalysts is a key step for the proposed hydrogen economy. However, few catalysts, except for noble metals, meet the entire requirements. By using state-of-the-art density functional calculations, herein we demonstrate that 2D MXenes, like $ \mathrm{T i}{2} \mathrm{C}, $ $ \mathrm{V}{2} \mathrm{C} $ and $ \mathrm{T i}{3} \mathrm{C}{2} $ are terminated by a mixture of oxygen atoms and hydroxyl, while $ \mathrm{N b}{2} \mathrm{C} $ and $ \mathrm{N b}{4} \mathrm{C}{3} \mathrm{O}{2} $ are fully terminated by oxygen atoms under standard conditions (pH = 0, $ \mathrm{p} \left( \mathrm{H}{2} \right)=1 $ bar, $ \mathrm{U}=0 $ V SHE), which is in good agreement with experimental observation. Furthermore, all these MXenes are conductive under standard conditions, thus enabling high charge transfer kinetics during the HER. Remarkably, the Gibbs free-energy for the adsorption of atomic hydrogen $ \left( \Delta G{H^{*}}^{0} \right) $ on the terminated O atoms (e.g. $ \mathrm{T i}{2} \mathrm{C O}{2} $ ) are close to the ideal value (0 eV). Our results demonstrate terminated oxygens as catalytic active sites for the HER at these materials and highlight a family of promising 2D catalysts for water splitting.

Keyword: Water splitting, surface Pourbaix diagrams, termination, volcano curve, Gibbs free-energy of hydrogen adsorption, functional groups.

Introduction

Hydrogen production from water splitting offers an essential solution for current problems in the environment $ ^{1-2} $ . So far, the most efficient hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) catalysts are noble metals, such as platinum $ ^{3} $ , however, the scarcity of noble metals hamper their applications as catalysts for large-scale hydrogen generation $ ^{4} $ . In recent years, some promising noble-metal-free catalysts with high activity toward the HER have been extensively studied including $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}}^{5-7} $ , metal phosphides $ ^{8} $ , $ \mathrm{g-C_{3} N_{4}}^{9-11} $ and nanocarbon $ ^{12} $ . However, one significant drawback of these 2D catalysts is that they are semi-conducting with poor charge transfer performance $ ^{5} $ . Even though $ 1 \mathrm{T}-\mathrm{M o S}_{2} $ is conductive, it is unstable under ambient conditions $ ^{7} $ . Another drawback is that the catalytically active sites are confined to the edge or defective atoms, while the majority of the in-plane atoms are catalytically inert $ ^{6,12-13} $ . Therefore, the ongoing search for new stable 2D HER catalysts with excellent charge transfer kinetics and many catalytically active sites is of paramount significance.

2D MXenes, early transition metal (M) carbides and nitrides (X), have recently been exfoliated via selective etching of the 'A' element in the counterpart MAX phases $ ^{14} $ . Here, M, A and X represent an early transition metal, a main group IIIA or IVA element and a carbon and/or nitrogen atom, respectively. The chemical formula of 2D MXenes is $ M_{n}X_{n-1} $ （n=2-4), where the top and bottom layers consist of the transition metals that are exposed. Since the synthesis of the first MXene, i.e. $ \mathrm{T i}{3} \mathrm{C}{2} $ from the MAX $ (\mathrm{T i}{3} \mathrm{A l C}{2}) $ phase in 2011 $ ^{15} $ , this new family of 2D materials has attracted intensive research attention $ ^{14,16-} $ $ ^{17} $ . Although a variety of 2D MXenes have been theoretically predicted $ ^{18} $ , only a few of them including $ \mathrm{T i}{2} \mathrm{C} $ $ ^{19} $ , $ \mathrm{V}{2} \mathrm{C}^{20} $ , $ \mathrm{N b}{2} \mathrm{C}^{21} $ , $ \mathrm{T i}{3} \mathrm{C}{2} $ $ ^{15} $ and $ \mathrm{N b}{4} \mathrm{C}_{3} $ $ ^{22} $ , have been synthesized to date. Experimentally, the exposed M-atoms in 2D MXenes are generally terminated by F $ ^{} $ , OH $ ^{} $ and O $ ^{} $ groups $ ^{14,23} $ , where $ ^{} $ represents one adsorption site on the surface. Significantly, the surface O-terminated 2D MXenes have demonstrated promising applications in Li-ion batteries $ ^{24-25} $ , supercapacitors $ ^{26-27} $ , fuel cells $ ^{28-29} $ , hydrogen evolution $ ^{30} $ , CO oxidation $ ^{31} $ and gas sensors $ ^{32} $ due to their excellent conductivity and tuneable surface terminations $ ^{33} $ .

第 4 页

The purpose of this study is to explore (i) the stability of 2D MXenes with oxygen/hydroxyl $ \mathrm{(O^{} / O H^{})} $ termination $ ^{1 4,3 4} $ and (ii) whether the experimentally realized MXenes $ \mathrm{T i_{2} C, V_{2} C, N b_{2} C, T i_{3} C_{2}} $ and $ \mathrm{N b_{4} C_{3}} $ with $ \mathrm{O^{}} $ or $ \mathrm{O H^{}} $ termination can act as efficient HER catalysts. Firstly, we find that 2D MXenes commonly terminated with $ \mathrm{O^{} / O H^{}} $ group in the experiment are metallic. Then surface Pourbaix diagrams were constructed to identify the most stable surface structures of 2D MXenes under relevant potentials vs the standard hydrogen electrode $ \left( \mathrm{U}{\mathrm{S H E}} \right) $ and pH. Under standard conditions, the $ \mathrm{T i{2} C, V_{2} C,} $ $ \mathrm{T i_{3} C_{2}} $ are terminated by a mixture of $ \mathrm{O H^{}} $ and $ \mathrm{O^{}} $ , while $ \mathrm{N b_{2} C} $ and $ \mathrm{N b_{4} C_{3}} $ are fully terminated by $ \mathrm{O^{}} $ The HER activity of oxygen-terminated MXenes are further evaluated by calculating the Gibbs free- energy of the adsorption of atomic hydrogen $ \left( \Delta G_{H^{}}^{0} \right) $ . Remarkably, surface oxygen atoms are for the first time demonstrated to be active sites for the HER. In addition, the HER performances of MXences with and without oxygen termination are compared by volcano curve. The results show that oxygen termination promotes the hydrogen releasing and increases the exchange current dramatically.

Computational Details

All calculations were performed by using Density functional theory (DFT) as implemented in the Vienna Ab-initio Simulation Package (VASP) code $ ^{35-36} $ . Blöchl's all-electron, frozen-core projector augmented wave (PAW) method $ ^{37} $ was used to represent nuclei and core electrons, and only the electrons in brackets of $ \mathrm{Ti}[3\mathrm{d}^{2}4\mathrm{s}^{2}], $ $ \mathrm{V}[3\mathrm{d}^{3}4\mathrm{s}^{2}], $ $ \mathrm{Nb}[4\mathrm{p}^{6}4\mathrm{d}^{4}5\mathrm{s}^{1}], $ $ \mathrm{O}[2\mathrm{s}^{2}2\mathrm{p}^{4}], $ and $ \mathrm{H}[1\mathrm{s}^{1}] $ , are treated the valence electrons. The exchange-correlation interactions were described by the generalized gradient approximation $ ^{38} $ in the form of the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof functional $ ^{39} $ . The van der Waals interaction was described by using the empirical correction in Grimme's scheme, i.e. $ \mathrm{DFT+D_{3}} $ in all the calculation $ ^{40} $ . The electron wavefunctions were expanded by plane waves with cut-off energies of 500 eV, and the convergence tolerance for residual force and energy on each atom during structure relaxation were set to 0.005 eV/$ \mathrm{\AA} $ and $ 10^{-5} $ eV, respectively. The vacuum space in Z-direction was more than 20 $ \mathrm{\AA} $ , which was enough to avoid the interaction between periodical images. The Brillouin zone was sampled with the Monkhorst-Pack mesh with a K-point of $ 9\times 9\times 1 $ for unit cell, and $ 5\times 5\times 1 $ for $ 2\times 2\times 1 $ supercell respectively in reciprocal space

The Gibbs free-energy of hydrogen adsorption: Under standard conditions, the overall HER pathway can be described by Eq (1),

$$ H ^ {+} (a q) + e ^ {-} \rightarrow \frac {1}{2} H _ {2} (g) \quad \Delta \mathrm {G} ^ {0} = 0 e V $$

comprising an initial state $ H^{+}(aq)+e^{-} $ , an intermediate adsorbed $ H^{} $ , and the final product $ \frac{1}{2} H_{2}(g). $ The total energies of $ H^{+}(aq)+e^{-} $ and $ \frac{1}{2} H_{2}(g) $ are equal. Therefore, the Gibbs free-energy of the adsorption of intermediate hydrogen on catalyst $ (\Delta G_{H^{}}^{0}) $ is a key descriptor to describe the HER activity of the catalyst, and is obtained by Eq (2)

$$ \Delta \mathrm {G} _ {\mathrm {H} ^ {*}} ^ {0} = \Delta \mathrm {E} _ {\mathrm {H}} + \Delta \mathrm {E} _ {\mathrm {Z P E}} - \mathrm {T} \Delta S _ {\mathrm {H}} $$

where $ \Delta E_{\mathrm{ZPE}} $ , and $ \Delta S_{\mathrm{H}} $ are the difference in zero point energy, and the entropy between atomic hydrogen adsorption and hydrogen in the gas phase respectively. The contributions from the catalysts to both $ \Delta E_{ZPE} $ and $ \Delta S_{H} $ are small and neglected. Therefore, $ \Delta E_{ZPE} $ is obtained by Eq (3) $ ^{43} $

$$ \Delta \mathrm {E} _ {\mathrm {Z P E}} = \mathrm {E} _ {\mathrm {Z P E}} ^ {\mathrm {n H}} - \mathrm {E} _ {\mathrm {Z P E}} ^ {(\mathrm {n} - 1) \mathrm {H}} - 1 / _ {2} \mathrm {E} _ {\mathrm {Z P E}} ^ {\mathrm {H} _ {2}} $$

where, $ \mathrm{E_{ZPE}^{nH}} $ is the zero point energy of n-adsorbed atomic hydrogens on the catalyst without the contribution of catalyst. The vibration frequency of $ \mathrm{H^{*}} $ adsorption on $ \mathrm{Ti}{3}\mathrm{C}{2}\mathrm{O}{2} $ with 1/8 coverage (n=1) is $ 3705.0\mathrm{cm}^{-1} $ , which is not sensitive to the coverages. $ E{ZPE}^{H_{2}} $ is the zero point energy of $ \mathrm{H}{2} $ in gas phase. The calculated frequency of $ \mathrm{H}{2} $ gas is $ 4289.4\mathrm{cm}^{-1} $ . The $ \Delta S_{H} $ is obtained by Eq (4)

$$ \Delta S _ {\mathrm {H}} \cong - ^ {1} / _ {2} S _ {\mathrm {H} _ {2}} ^ {0} $$

And $ S_{\mathrm{H}{2}}^{0} $ is the entropy of $ \mathrm{H}{2} $ gas at the standard condition $ ^{4 4} $ . Therefore, Eq (2) can be rewritten as Eq (5):

$$ \Delta \mathrm {G} _ {\mathrm {H} ^ {*}} ^ {0} = \Delta \mathrm {E} _ {\mathrm {H}} + 0. 3 0 \mathrm {e V} $$

$ \Delta \mathrm{E}_{\mathrm{H}} $ is the differential hydrogen adsorption energy, which is defined by Eq (6):

$$ \Delta \mathrm {E} _ {\mathrm {H}} = \mathrm {E} _ {\mathrm {n H} *} - \mathrm {E} _ {(\mathrm {n} - 1) \mathrm {H} *} - 1 / _ {2} \mathrm {E} _ {\mathrm {H} _ {2}} $$

where $ ^{} $ donates the catalyst. $ E_{n H^{}} $ $ E_{(n-1)H^{*}} $ , and $ E_{H_{2}} $ represents total energies of catalyst plus n adsorbed hydrogen atoms, total energies of catalyst plus n-1 adsorbed atomic hydrogen atoms, and $ H_{2} $ gas, respectively.

Surface Pourbaix Diagrams: The surface Pourbaix diagrams of all five MXenes were constructed by plotting the most stable surface state under the relevant $ U_{SHE} $ and pH. In our model, we assumed that the oxidation of water to $ OH^{} $ and $ O^{} $ on MXenes occurred through the following steps as suggested in ref. $ ^{45} $ :

$$ H _ {2} O + * \rightarrow O H ^ {*} + H ^ {+} + e ^ {-} $$
$$ O H ^ {*} \rightarrow O ^ {*} + H ^ {+} + e ^ {-} $$

Under standard conditions, the free energy of $ \mathrm{H}^{+}+ \mathrm{e}^{-} $ is equal to $ \frac{1}{2} \mathrm{H}_{2} $ as indicated in Eq (1). Therefore, Eq (7) and Eq (8) can be rewritten as Eq (9) and Eq (10):

$$ H _ {2} O + * \rightarrow O H ^ {*} + \frac {1}{2} H _ {2} \Delta G _ {9} ^ {0} $$
$$ O H ^ {*} \rightarrow O ^ {*} + \frac {1}{2} H _ {2} \quad \Delta \mathrm {G} _ {1 0} ^ {0} $$

The Gibbs free-energies of Eq (9) $ \left( \Delta G_{9}^{0} \right) $ and Eq (10) $ \left( \Delta G_{10}^{0} \right) $ are obtained by Eq (11)

$$ \Delta G ^ {0} = \Delta E + \Delta E _ {Z P E} - T \Delta S $$

where, $ \Delta E $ is the energy difference of Eq (9) or Eq (10). The values for $ \Delta E_{ZPE}-T\Delta S $ are calculated based on values of Table I in ref. $ ^{46} $

Both Eq (7) and Eq (8) are dependent on the pH and potential U through the chemical potential of $ \mathrm{H}^{+} $ and $ \mathrm{e}^{-} $ respectively, while Eq (9) and Eq (10) are not. To include the effects of pH and potential U,Eq (11) are rewritten as Eq (12) and Eq (13):

$$ \Delta \mathrm {G} _ {9} = \Delta \mathrm {G} _ {9} ^ {0} - e U _ {S H E} - k _ {b} T l n 1 0 \times p H $$
$$ \Delta \mathrm {G} _ {1 0} = \Delta \mathrm {G} _ {1 0} ^ {0} - e U _ {S H E} - k _ {b} T l n 1 0 \times p H $$

The free energy change of $ \mathrm{O H^{} } $ and $ \mathrm{O^{}} $ termination can be express by Eq(14) and Eq (15):

$$ \Delta \mathrm {G} _ {O H *} = \Delta \mathrm {G} _ {9} $$
$$ \Delta \mathrm {G} _ {O H *} = \Delta \mathrm {G} _ {9} + \Delta \mathrm {G} _ {1 0} $$

Based on Eqns. 14 and 15, we can calculate the free energy of MXenes with different coverages of $ \mathrm{O H}^{} $ and $ \mathrm{O}^{} $ termination at different coverages and conditions. The free energy of MXenes with a mix of terminations $ \left(\mathrm{M}{n}\mathrm{C}{n-1}(\mathrm{O H}){x}(\mathrm{O}){y},\mathrm{x}+y\leq 2\right) $ are obtained by

$$ \begin{array}{l} \Delta \mathrm {G} _ {m i x} = \mathrm {x} \Delta \mathrm {G} _ {O H *} + \mathrm {y} \Delta \mathrm {G} _ {O *} \\ = (x + y) \Delta \mathrm {G} _ {9} + \mathrm {y} \Delta \mathrm {G} _ {1 0} \\ = (x + y) \Delta \mathrm {G} _ {9} ^ {0} + y \Delta \mathrm {G} _ {1 0} ^ {0} - (x + 2 y) \left(e U _ {S H E} + k _ {b} T l n 1 0 \times p H\right) \\ \end{array} $$

The most stable state of the surface under the relevant conditions is used to construct the surface Pourbaix diagrams. A numerical example of $ \mathrm{T i}_{2} \mathrm{C} $ can be found in Table S1 in supporting information.

The volcano curve

In the volcano curve, the average Gibbs free-energy of hydrogen adsorption $ \left( \Delta G_{H^{}}^{a} \right) $ on bare MXenes with hydrogen coverage of $ \theta=1 $ and oxygen terminated MXenes with hydrogen coverage of $ \theta=1 / 2 $ are used to calculate the theoretical exchange current $ \mathbf{i}{0} $ . To obtain $ \Delta G{H^{}}^{a} $ , only Eq(6) were rewritten to

$$ \Delta \mathrm {E} _ {H} ^ {a} = \mathrm {E} _ {\mathrm {n H} *} - \mathrm {E} _ {\mathrm {c a t}} - \mathrm {n} / _ {2} \mathrm {E} _ {\mathrm {H} _ {2}} $$

Where $ E_{\mathrm{cat}} $ is the total energies of catalyst.

The exchange current is based on the Norskov's assumption $ ^{47} $ (please see the reference for details). If the $ \Delta G_{H^{*}}^{a} \leq0 $ , the following expressing for the exchange current at pH=0 are used:

$$ i _ {0} = - e \mathrm {k} _ {0} \frac {1}{1 + \exp \left(- \Delta \mathrm {G} _ {H *} ^ {a} / k _ {b} T\right)} $$

For other case $ \left( \Delta\mathrm{G}_{H^{*}}^{a} > 0\right) $ , the exchange current is calculated by:

$$ i _ {0} = - e \mathrm {k} _ {0} \frac {1}{1 + \exp \left(- \Delta \mathrm {G} _ {H *} ^ {a} / k _ {b} T\right)} $$

Where $ \mathrm{k}{0} $ is the rate constant. As there are no experimental data available, the $ \mathrm{k}{0} $ is set to 1.

Results and Discussions

Excellent Electrical Conductivity

The structure models of MXenes $ \left( \mathrm{M}{n} \mathrm{C}{n-1} \right) $ , oxygen-terminated MXenes $ \left( \mathrm{M}{n} \mathrm{C}{n-1} \mathrm{O}{2} \right) $ and hydroxyl terminated MXenes $ \left( \mathrm{M}{n} \mathrm{C}{n-1} (\mathrm{OH}){2} \right) $ , are presented in Figure 1. The 2D MXenes investigated in this work are 2D $ \mathrm{T i}{2} \mathrm{C} $ $ \mathrm{V}{2} \mathrm{C} $ $ \mathrm{N b}{2} \mathrm{C} $ $ \mathrm{T i}{3} \mathrm{C}{2} $ and $ \mathrm{N b}{4} \mathrm{C}_{3} $ , which have all been experimentally realized $ ^{15,19-22} $ . As shown in Figure 1a, 1d, and 1g, the bare MXenes are packed in the face-centred cubic arrangement with two exposed metal layers, displaying P3m1 symmetry. The calculated band structures of bare MXenes are presented in Figure 2a-e, clearly indicating that all the MXenes are metallic. Since the exposed metal atoms are electron donors, they are vulnerable to electronegative functional groups, such as $ \mathrm{O H^{}} $ and $ \mathrm{O^{}} $ group during their experimental synthesis.

High Thermal Stability

In the following, the surface Pourbaix diagrams of all five 2D MXenes are constructed by plotting the thermodynamically most stable surface state under the relevant $ \mathrm{U_{SHE}} $ and pH (see Figure 3) [21]. As alluded to above, the bare MXenes are very vulnerable to electron accepting functional groups such as $ \mathrm{O H^{}}, $ and $ \mathrm{O^{}}. $ In acidic solution (pH = 0), negative $ \mathrm{U_{SHE}} $ values (reducing environment) as low as -2.63 V, -1.91 V, -2.04 V, -2.53 V, and -1.51 V are required to protect $ \mathrm{T i_{2} C}, $ $ \mathrm{V_{2} C}, $ $ \mathrm{N b_{2} C}, $ $ \mathrm{T i_{3} C_{2}} $ and $ \mathrm{N b_{4} C_{3}} $ from oxidation by $ \mathrm{H_{2}O}, $ respectively. pH with a slope of -0.059 V/pH. When the potential is above the cathodic protection potential of the 2D MXenes, water starts to oxidize and the MXenes are covered by 1/8 ML OH $ ^{} $ . As the potential increases, more OH $ ^{} $ adsorbs on the 2D MXenes, and it finally reaches to 1 ML OH $ ^{} $ （ $ M_{n}X_{n-1} (OH){2} $ ). The 2D MXenes must exhibit high stability and excellent HER activity at $ U_{\mathrm{SHE}}=0 \mathrm{~V} $ if they are to be used as efficient HER catalysts. Herein, we investigated the relative stability of MXenes with different terminations as a function of pH at $ U_{\mathrm{SHE}}=0 \mathrm{~V} $ (see Figure 4). The bottom line corresponds to the states with the lowest free energy, i.e. the most stable state at a given pH value. The $ \mathrm{O}^{} / \mathrm{O H}^{} $ terminated MXenes are much more stable than bare 2D MXenes or partially terminated MXenes (1/8 ML $ \mathrm{O H}^{} $ ). In acidic solution ( $ \mathrm{pH}=0 $ ), the most stable states of $ \mathrm{N b}{2} \mathrm{C} $ and $ \mathrm{N b}{3} \mathrm{C}_{2} $ are $ \mathrm{O}^{} $ terminated surfaces, while, $ \mathrm{T i}{2} \mathrm{C}, \mathrm{V}{2} \mathrm{C}, $ and $ \mathrm{T i}{3} \mathrm{C}{2} $ terminated by the mixture of $ \mathrm{O}^{} $ and $ \mathrm{O H}^{} $ are the most energetically favourable. As the pH value is increased, the hydrogen atom in the hydroxyl group on the 2D MXenes $ (\mathrm{T i}{2} \mathrm{C}, \mathrm{V}{2} \mathrm{C}, $ and $ \mathrm{T i}{3} \mathrm{C}{2} $ ) can be dissociated. When the pH values are above 3.6, 8.4 and 5.5 for $ \mathrm{T i}{2} \mathrm{C}, \mathrm{V}{2} \mathrm{C}, $ and $ \mathrm{T i}{3} \mathrm{C}{2} $ respectively (the black lines cross the green lines in Figure 4 a, b, d), these MXenes are fully terminated by $ \mathrm{O}^{} $ . Collectively, these are consistent with the experimental observation that MXenes terminated by a mixture of $ \mathrm{O H}^{} $ and $ \mathrm{O}^{} $ are highly stable $ ^{14,23} $ and thus it is reasonable to investigate MXenes terminated by a mixture of $ \mathrm{O H}^{} $ and $ \mathrm{O}^{*} $ as the most appropriate catalysts for the HER.

To fully understand the process of HER on the MXenes, the two generally accepted HER mechanisms, Volmer-Heyrovsky and Volmer-Tafel reactions $ ^{48} $ were studied as shown in Figure S2 of supporting information. The difference in above two pathways is the second step: Heyrovsky $ (H^{+}+e^{-}+H^{*}\rightarrow H_{2}) $ and Tafel step $ (H^{*}+H^{*}\rightarrow H_{2}) $ . Herein, we compare the activation barrier during HER follows Heyrovsky pathway and Tafel pathway on a $ (3\times 3\times 1) $ $ \mathrm{Ti}_{2} \mathrm{CO}_{2} $ supercell with one surface covered by $ \mathrm{H^{*}} $ . 8 $ \mathrm{H_{2}O} $ are used to simulated the solution effects. As we can see, the barrier Heyrovsky step (0.29 eV) is much smaller than Tafel step (0.99 eV). Therefore, The HER follows the Heyrovsky mechanism on oxygen terminated MXenes.

Conclusions

In summary, we have demonstrated that 2D MXenes commonly terminated with a mixture of O $ ^{} $ and OH $ ^{} $ in experiment exhibit excellent HER performance. Under standard conditions, the surface Pourbaix diagrams clearly indicated that O $ ^{} $ /OH $ ^{} $ terminated MXenes are the most stable states, and all of them are metallic, thus favouring excellent charge transfer. The surface oxygen atoms on the top and bottom layers of 2D MXenes function as the catalytic active sites for the HER with a suitable interaction strength between H $ ^{*} $ and 2D MXenes. If coupling with a suitable photocatalyst, metallic 2D MXenes under standard conditions could be an efficient co-catalyst for the photocatalytic HER. Our findings demonstrate that O-terminations act as catalytic active sites for efficient HER and highlight a new family of promising noble metal-free HER catalysts for future experimental verification.
