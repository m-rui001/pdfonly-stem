Abstract

Since their discovery in 2011, the number of two-dimensional (2D) transition metal carbides and nitrides (MXenes) kept steadily increased. In time, they may develop into the largest family of 2D materials. Currently more than 40 MXene compositions exist. The ultimate number and is far greater. MXenes' unique properties, such as their metal-like electrical conductivity reaching $ \sim 2 0, 0 0 0 $ S/cm, render them very useful in a large number of applications including energy storage, optoelectronic, biomedical, communications and environmental. The number of MXene papers and patents published has been growing quickly. The first MXene generation was synthesized using selective etching of metal layers from the MAX phases, layered transition metal carbides and carbonitrides using hydrofluoric acid. Since then, multiple synthesis approaches have been developed, including selective etching in a mixture of fluoride salts and various acids, non-aqueous etchants, halogens and molten salts, allowing for the synthesis of new MXenes with better control over their surface chemistries. Herein, we provide a historical overview of the first ten years of MXene research and a perspective on their synthesis and future development. The fact that their production is readily scalable in aqueous environments with high yields, bodes well for their commercialization.

Introduction

After the discovery of the unusual physical properties in single- and few-layer graphene in 2004, two-dimensional (2D) materials captured the imagination of scientist, engineers and investors, becoming a very dynamic and exciting research field. $ ^{1} $ This discovery attracted the attention to 2D materials beyond graphene and quickly led to the exfoliation and exploration of other materials having readily available layered precursors, from graphite-like hexagonal boron nitride $ h $ -BN to dichalcogenides and layered oxides. $ ^{2} $ Soon after, 2D Si, $ ^{3} $ Ge, $ ^{4} $ Sn $ ^{5} $ and several other materials that do not have weakly bonded layered precursors was demonstrated. This was a clear indication that the family of 2D materials was projected to expand, covering numerous compounds with a variety of structures and chemistries. In 2018, the computational 2D Materials Database (C2DB) reported on a variety of structural, thermodynamic, elastic, electronic, magnetic, and optical properties of around 1500 2D materials distributed over more than 30 different crystal structures. $ ^{6} $ This potentially allows for an exciting prospective to use 2D building blocks with complementing properties to create artificial materials and build entire devices using various assemblies of 2D flakes. $ ^{7-8} $

In 2011, and in spite of the existence of numerous 2D materials, the vast majority were semiconductors, semimetals or insulators with low electronic conductivities and carrier concentrations. As importantly, few 2D materials could be made in amounts sufficient for applications outside the microelectronics field. Solution processed 2D materials, except for graphene and h-BN, were quite small in flake size due to low mechanical strength leading to fracture of 2D sheets during delamination. Many of the the 2D materials were also hydrophobic and unstable in air. Therefore, the discovery of a family of 2D carbides and nitrides with metallic conductivity, hydrophilicity, ease of processing, relatively high yields and large flakes, had a profound effect on the entire materials science field.

Early transition metal carbides and nitrides are an important class of materials due to their high metallic electrical conductivity, high hardness, and excellent chemical stabilities. $ ^{9-} $ $ ^{11} $ They have been explored for decades as bulk ceramic materials mostly for high temperature applications and cutting tools. $ ^{12} $ Other applications include, but are not limited

to, functional composites, catalysts and electrochemical energy storage. $ ^{12} $ However, strong bonding in all directions between the transition metal and carbon/nitrogen atoms (mostly covalent/metallic bonds) $ ^{13} $ rendered reducing their dimensionality - from bulk three-dimensional (3D) solids to nanomaterials including 2D sheets and 1D nanoribbons or nanotubes - a challenging task. Thus, unorthodox approaches were necessary to reduce their dimensionality. Our approach utilized the difference in strength of metal-metal bonds vs metal-carbon/nitrogen bonds to selectively etch monoatomic metal layers from the MAX phases. The latter are layered ternary carbides and nitrides with a composition of $ \mathrm{M}{n+1}\mathrm{AX}{n} $ where M is an early transition metal, A is mostly group 13 and 14 elements, X is carbon and/or nitrogen, and n =1-4. $ ^{14-15} $ In a seminal paper in 1996, Barsoum and El-Raghy were the first to make single phase pure samples of $ \mathrm{Ti}{3}\mathrm{SiC}{2} $ and demonstrate that it was readily machinable and an excellent conductor of electricity and heat. $ ^{16} $ In 1997, $ ^{17} $ they showed that $ \mathrm{Ti}{3}\mathrm{SiC}{2} $ was one 50 phases, most of which were discovered by Nowotny and coworkers in the sixties. $ ^{18} $ In 1999, $ \mathrm{Ti}{4}\mathrm{AlN}{3} $ was discovered $ ^{19} $ which led one of us to label them the $ \mathrm{M}{n+1}\mathrm{AX}{n} $ or MAX phases. $ ^{20} $ Structurally, the MAX phases are layered hexagonal ( $ \mathrm{P6}_{3}/\mathrm{mmc} $ space group) materials and can be described as transition metal carbide/nitride sheets of octahedral blocks, where the X atoms are in the centers of the octahedrons, glued together with pure A layers.

In 2011 we showed that by simply immersing $ \mathrm{T i}{3} \mathrm{A l C}{2} $ in HF at room temperature we could selectively etch the Al layers leaving behind a 2D titanium carbide $ - \mathrm{T i}{3} \mathrm{C}{2} $ - for the first time. $ ^{21} $ A year later $ ^{22} $ we showed that this method of selective etching was applicable to many other MAX phases with the aluminum A-layer, which is one of the more ubiquitous Aelements in MAX phase. It was at this juncture that we labelled this new family MXenes to emphasize their relationship to the MAX phases and their dimensionality. $ ^{22-23} $ In 2011, there were $ \approx $ 70 MAX phases; today they number $ 1 5 0^{2 4} $ , with new ones discovered on a routine basis, proving a large number of precursors for MXenes. More important than the number of patents filed and issued is the very broad rnage of applications possible, ranging from electronics to medicine, sensing, communication, optoelectronics and tribology among many others.

MXene Synthesis

As noted above, our first report on 2D transition metal carbide was focused on titanium carbide $ \left( \mathrm{T i}{3} \mathrm{C}{2} \right) $ which was synthesized using aqueous hydrofluoric acid, HF, as an etchant for Al at room temperature, RT. $ ^{21} $ It is worth noting that HF was used to selectively etch the A layers with the goal of creating more space for electrochemical Li intercalation/deintercalation in the MAX phases, which were researched for Li-ion battery anodes, not with the intention to make a 2D material. More details about the facinating story of the MXenes discovery can be found in the preface we wrote in Ref $ ^{25} $ . Like $ \mathrm{T i}{3} \mathrm{C}{2} $ , other representatives of the first generation of MXenes were all synthesized by selectively etching the Al, layers from different MAX phases $ \left( \mathrm{T i}{2} \mathrm{A l C}, \mathrm{V}{2} \mathrm{A l C}, \mathrm{N b}{2} \mathrm{A l C}, \mathrm{N b}{4} \mathrm{A l C}{3}, \mathrm{T a}{4} \mathrm{A l C}{3}, \mathrm{T i}{3} \mathrm{A l} \left( \mathrm{C}{0.5} \mathrm{N}{0.5} \right){2}, $ $ \left( \mathrm{T i}{0.5} \mathrm{N b}{0.5} \right){2} \mathrm{A l C}, $ $ \left( \mathrm{V}{0.5} \mathrm{C r}{0.5} \right){3} \mathrm{A l C}{2}, $ $ \left( \mathrm{T i}{0.5} \mathrm{V}{0.5} \right){2} \mathrm{A l C}, $ $ \left( \mathrm{T i}{0.5} \mathrm{V}{0.5} \right){3} \mathrm{A l C}{2}, $ and $ \left( \mathrm{N b}{0.5} \mathrm{V}{0.5} \right){4} \mathrm{A l C}_{3} $ using aqueous HF at RT. $ ^{23,26-28} $ However, the etching conditions (acid concentration, temperature, time) had to be modified for each composition to achieve the conversion, since the bond strengths between the different M elements and Al varied. $ ^{29} $ Another reason for the importance of tuning the etching conditions is to maintain high yields and minimize the degradation of 2D flakes in the acid, because their chemical stability varies depending the structure and composition.

Since the synthesis is carried out in a fluoride containing aqueous solution, the transition metal surfaces of MXene are terminated by O, OH and F (for simplicity $ \mathrm{T}{x} $ is used hereafter to describe mixed surface terminations). $ ^{21-22} $ The resulting MXene, after HF etching, is a multilayer material with a morphology that resembles kaolinite clay. Due to surface terminations, these multilayers are held together by a mixture of hydrogen and van der Waals bonds. $ ^{30} $ Unlike the pristine MAX phases, these secondary bonds allow for intercalation between the layers. Intercalation of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) in $ \mathrm{T i}{3} \mathrm{C}{2} \mathrm{T}{x} $ followed by sonication results in a colloidal solution of delaminated $ \mathrm{T i}{3} \mathrm{C}{2} \mathrm{T}_{x} $ in water. $ ^{31} $ Interestingly, the DMSO intercalation was not a universal approach, since it did not intercalate all MXenes. The discovery in 2014 that one can not only etch some of the MAX phases with a combination of fluoride salts such as LiF and more beginn acids such as HCl, but also delaminate them in one step was a major boost to the field. Ghidiu et al. showed that when $ \mathrm{T i}{3} \mathrm{A l C}{2} $ was immersed in a LiF and HCl solution the in situ formation of HF not only converted the MAX phase to MXene, but the resulting product behaved like a clay rheologically, because the intercalated cations - $ \mathrm{L i}^{+} $ hydrated by water in this case - allowed the product to be rolled and formed into different shapes. $ ^{33} $ Further hydration of the cations can lead to significant swelling and MXene delamination in water. $ ^{33} $ The importance of this work goes beyond eliminating the extra steps of intercalation that are usually required to delaminate HF-etched MXenes. However, tuning of already existing synthesis protocols and modifying MAX phases are other important approaches to alter MXene properties and performance. Fine tuning the MAX phases can affect the synthesis conditions of MXenes. For example, adding very small amount of Sc to the precursors of $ \mathrm{V}{2} \mathrm{A l C} $ results in formation of a solid solution of V-Sc that reduces significantly the etching time needed to make $ \mathrm{V}{2- \mathrm{y}} \mathrm{C T}{x} $ $ \mathrm{(y < 0.05)} $ since traces of Sc change the stability of the MAX phase. $ ^{49} $ Having a solid solution at the A sites in the MAX phase like in the case of $ \mathrm{T i}{3} \left( \mathrm{S i}{0.75} \mathrm{A l}{0.25} \right) \mathrm{C}{2} $ and $ \mathrm{T i}{3} \left( \mathrm{S i}{0.1} \mathrm{A l}{0.9} \right) \mathrm{C}{2} $ weakens the bonding between the carbide sheets through the A layers and therefore allows for exfoliating these materials with the aid of ultrasonication, which is not possible for either $ \mathrm{T i}{3} \mathrm{S i C}{2} $ nor $ \mathrm{T i}{3} \mathrm{A l C}{2}. $ $ ^{50} $ Here the end result is not MXenes however, but thin MAX platelets. Another example of the effect of MAX phase chemistry on MXene is the work by Mathis et al. $ ^{51} $ who showed significant enhancement in both stability and conductivity of MXene by having excess Al during the synthesis of $ \mathrm{T i}{3} \mathrm{A l C}_{2} $ , which leads to formation of highly stoichiometric MAX and MXene. Simple modifications for the etching conditions such as the ratio of fluoride salt to acid, $ ^{52} $ or bubbling nitrogen gas during etching $ ^{53} $ may change the properties of the resulting MXene significantly.

For MXenes beyond $ \mathrm{T i}{3} \mathrm{C}{2} \mathrm{T}_{x} $ - the first and most studied MXene - further optimization and modification of the synthesis conditions are needed to improve flake size and minimize the concentration of defects.

Diversity of Structures and Compositions

Any advancements in the MAX phases synthesis or expansion of their compositional and structural diversity directly benefit the MXenes family and, in fact, many of the recent studies on MAX phases have been motivated by the goal to make new MXenes. We will give here a few examples of such advancements to highlight this correlation. MAX phases with more than one transition metal in the form of solid solutions have been known for decades. $ ^{54-55} $ Their MXenes, per force, were only recently realized; here we cite a few examples. $ ^{15,22,56-61} $ In 2014, an ordered double transition metal MAX phase, viz., $ \mathrm{C r_{2} T i A l C_{2}} $ was reported. In this out-of-plane ordered MAX phase ( $ \mathrm{P 6_{3}}/$ mmc space group) the Cr atoms occupy almost exclusively the outer layers of M (4f Wyckoff sites) in the $ \mathrm{M_{3} C_{2}} $ blocks, while the Ti atoms occupy the center layer (2a Wyckoff sites). $ ^{62} $ Not long after that, Anasori et al. $ ^{63} $ discovered $ \mathrm{M o_{2} T i A l C_{2}} $ and $ \mathrm{M o_{2} T i_{2} A l C_{3}} $ and reported on three new out-of-plane ordered MXenes $ (\mathrm{M o_{2} T i C_{2} T_{x}},\mathrm{M o_{2} T i_{2} C_{3} T_{x}} $ and $ \mathrm{C r_{2} T i C_{2} T_{x}} $ ). In 2017, Meshkian et al. $ ^{64} $ reported on out-of-plane ordered $ \mathrm{M o_{2} S c A l C_{2}} $ and its corresponding MXene $ \mathrm{M o_{2} S c C_{2} T_{x}} $

Another type of ordered double transition metal layered carbide (viz. $ ( \mathrm{M o}{2 / 3} \mathrm{S c}{1 / 3}){2} \mathrm{A l C} ) $ was reported by Tao et al. $ ^{65} $ in which two transition metals (Mo and Sc) are in-plane ordered within the transition metal carbide sheet. During etching, the Sc atoms are etched as well. This results in a $ \mathrm{M o}{4 / 3} \mathrm{C T}{x} $ MXene with ordered vacancies on the M sites, where the Sc atoms existed initially. $ ^{65} $ Other in-plane ordered MAX phases with new transition metals for the MAX phases such as $ ( \mathrm{W}{2 / 3} \mathrm{Y}{1 / 3}){2} \mathrm{A l C} $ (W or Y alone do not form a MAX phase) $ ^{66-67} $ and their corresponding MXenes with ordered vacancies (e.g., $ \mathrm{W}{4 / 3} \mathrm{C T}{x} $ were reported. Random vacancies at M sites were reported for $ \mathrm{N b}{1.33} \mathrm{C T}{x} $ by etching both Sc and Al from $ (\mathrm{N b}{2 / 3} \mathrm{S c}{1 / 3}){2} \mathrm{A l C}. $ $ ^{68} $ Persson et al. $ ^{69} $ reported that tuning the etching conditions for $ (\mathrm{M o}{2 / 3} \mathrm{Y}{1 / 3}){2} \mathrm{A l C} $ , which exhibits in-plane ordering, can result in etching only the Al layers while leaving behind an in-plane ordered double M (viz., $ (\mathrm{M o}{2 / 3} \mathrm{Y}{1 / 3}){2} \mathrm{C T}{x} $ MXene.

MAX phases with n > 3 were only observed as minor impurity phases in bulk synthesis or sputtered thin films. $ ^{70-71} $ However, recently Deysher et al. $ ^{15} $ reported on the synthesis of a n = 4 phase with a composition $ (\mathrm{M o}{0.8}\mathrm{V}{0.2}){5}\mathrm{A l C}{4} $ using powder metallurgy bulk synthesis at $ 1 6 5 0 \mathrm{~}^\circ \mathrm{C} $ . The compositional and structural space of the MAX phases and their corresponding MXenes will further expand in the coming years, especially in the direction of more than one M per MXene (solid solution, ordered out-of-plane and/or in-plane structures) since this is a powerful approach to tailor MXenes' properties. $ ^{58} $

Compared to C-based MXenes, there are very few reports on nitride and carbonitride MXenes $ ^{22,38,74-77} $ which is in part because of a limited number of nitrogen containing MAX phases and the difficulties in synthesis of nitride MXenes, as nitride layers tend to dissolve in the acids. Surface Terminations and Intercalation

Surface termination of as synthesized MXenes depends largely on the synthesis approach. For example, when aqueous HF is used, mixed O, OH and F terminations are usually found with different ratios, depending on each MXene and etching conditions. $ ^{85} $ When molten chloride salts are used, Cl terminations dominate; $ ^{45} $ when water-free $ \mathrm{N H_{4} H F_{2}} $ is used, F-rich surfaces ensue. $ ^{36} $ Post-synthesis treatments can also be used to alter the terminations. $ ^{32, 86-87} $ Kamysbayev et al. $ ^{46} $ substituted Br and Cl terminations of their as-produced MXenes synthesized by etching of MAX phases in molten $ \mathrm{C d B r_{2}} $ and $ \mathrm{C d C l_{2}} $ , respectively - by S, Se, Te, and NH through post-synthesis reactions with Li and Na salts in molten bromides at 300 $ ^{\circ} \mathrm{C} $ , followed by annealing and anhydrous washing. terminations enriches their already vast compositional space, making MXenes potentially the largest family of 2D materials.

Many cations and organic molecules can be intercalated spontaneously in-between the MXene sheets, further adding to the versatility of their compositions and properties. $ ^{31} $ For example, very recently, Brady et al., $ ^{88} $ reported on the spontaneous intercalation of up to 2 Na per $ \mathrm{Ti}{3} \mathrm{C}{2} $ unit by soaking multilayer MXenes in a solution of Na-biphenyl radical anion complex at RT. In addition to the chemical versatility that intercalation provides, it also offers a structural tunability as pre-intercalation of molecules with different sizes allows for engineering the d-spacing in MXene that can be utilized to match certain application requirements. An example for this is using multilayer MXene as electrode material for supercapacitors in room-temperature ionic liquid (RTIL) electrolyte. Without engineering the d-spacing of MXene, the large cations of RTIL cannot intercalate in-between the MXene layers limiting the capacitance to the low outer surface area of multilayer MXene. $ ^{89} $ However, at a d-spacing of 2.2 nm, which was achieved by pre-intercalation of alkylammonium cations, the interlayer spacing of MXenes was unlocked for the reversible electrochemical intercalation/de-intercalation of RTIL cations, leading to a superior capacitance and energy density. $ ^{90} $

Properties

In the 2D world, MXenes stand out because they offer a unique set of properties and characteristics that render them attractive for many applications. The most prominent is their metallic conductivity, while soe MXenes can be semiconductors or superconductors, depending on the composition and surface terminations. $ ^{46,91-92} $ Interestingly, the continuous optimization of the synthesis of $ \mathrm{T i}{3} \mathrm{C}{2} \mathrm{T}{x} $ - the most studied MXene - keeps pushing its electrical conductivity to higher values. Recently, Zeraati et al., $ ^{53} $ reported a conductivity of up to 24 000 S/cm for $ \mathrm{T i}{3} \mathrm{C}{2} \mathrm{T}{x} $ by a modification of the etching process. Such high electrical conductivities combined with the surface terminations, which allow for covalent or electrostatic anchoring of other molecules and nanoparticles to achieve strong interfaces, render MXenes ideal candidates as conductive additives, $ ^{93-94} $ electrical contacts, $ ^{95} $ conductive transparent electrodes, $ ^{96} $ and films for electromagnetic interference shielding. $ ^{97} $ Like 3D transition metal carbides/nitrides, MXenes offer high strength and moduli of elasticity. $ ^{98} $ Lipatov et al., $ ^{99-100} $ reported Young's moduli of single layers to be 330 MPa and 390 MPa for

$ \mathrm{T i}{3} \mathrm{C}{2} \mathrm{T}{x} $ and $ \mathrm{N b}{4} \mathrm{C}{3} \mathrm{T}{x} $ , respectively. Both values are higher than those for other 2D materials (e.g., graphene oxide or $ \mathrm{M o S}_{2} $ ) produced by liquid exfoliation. The trio of metallic electrical conductivities, high mechanical properties and surface functionalities provide MXenes an advantage over other nanoscale reinforcements for polymers, ceramics and metals to manufacture strong functional composites. $ ^{101-102} $ The excellent electrical conductivity, in addition to the ability to host ions and provide fast ionic transport, make MXenes promising electrode materials for ion batteries $ ^{103} $ and supercapacitors. $ ^{104} $ MXenes have been explored and showed promise in many other fields and such as environmental, $ ^{105} $ biomedical, $ ^{106} $ sensing, $ ^{107} $ and catalysis. $ ^{108} $

Unlike many other 2D materials that can only be produced in small amounts, often as single layers under high-vacuum conditions, the simplicity of MXenes' synthesis by selective etching of ceramic precursors makes it fairly straightforward to scale their synthesis to meet industry needs. $ ^{109} $ In particular, titanium based MXenes are made of abundant elements, show no cytotoxicity, and produce non-toxic products, such as $ \mathrm{TiO_{2}} $ $ \mathrm{CO_{2}} $ or $ \mathrm{CH_{4}} $ upon degradation. $ ^{110} $ Also, low-cost precursors, such as recycled aluminum scrap, waste tire-derived carbon $ ^{111} $ and transition metal oxides $ ^{111-112} $ can be used to produce MAX phases at significantly lower prices which can lower the overall cost. These advantages, combined with the unique properties and promising performance of MXenes in many applications, make MXenes attractive materials for technological applications, not just laboratory scale fundamental research.

Summary and Outlook

MXenes' unique properties, their promising performance in a wide range of applications and their scalable synthesis have made them attractive for research and technological development, as can be clearly seen from the large number of papers and patents emerging during the past couple of years. MXenes composition can be tuned by changing M, X, n, $ \mathrm{T}_{x} $ and the intercalants. With about 30 reported distinct stoichiometric MXenes and countless possible compositions due to solid solutions on both M and X sites and various terminations, the number of these materials will indubitably continue growing and in due course, may become the largest family of 2D materials.

Replacing carbon with nitrogen changes the electronic structure of MXene and therefore its properties. However, most of the MXenes reported are carbides with few

exceptions of nitrides and carbonitrides. Attempts to produce MXenes with $ \mathrm{X}=\mathrm{B} $ (MBenes) had a limited success so far. Other 2D carbides, nitrides and borides, which were predicted computationally, are still waiting to be synthesized. Efforts towards their synthesis should be undertaken. One of the challenges to producing some of the theoretically predicted MXenes is their stability in the etchants. Thus, the continuous development of different synthesis approaches is important to achieve new MXene compositions and produce controlled and homogeneous surface terminations. An example for this is the synthesis of $ \mathrm{C r_{2} C T_{x}} $ and $ \mathrm{T i_{2} N T_{x}} $ by etching Al from $ \mathrm{C r_{2} A l C} $ and $ \mathrm{T i_{2} A l N} $ , respectively. Earlier attempts to etch Al from these MAX phases using HF to synthesize MXenes failed and resulted in formation of fluorides and oxides. $ ^{57} $ Only when other etchants were used were these MXenes claimed experimentally; $ ^{75,113} $ still the synthesis should be reproduced by other researchers to confirm the validity of the approach. The nature of bonding between the layers in the parent bulk material is another important factor determining the success of MXene's synthesis. For example, to synthesize $ \mathrm{M o_{2} C T_{x}} $ $ \mathrm{M o_{2} G a_{2} C} $ is used instead of $ \mathrm{M o_{2} G a C} $ because the Ga-Ga bonds are weaker that the Mo-Ga bonds, therefore the selective etching of Ga can start by breaking the Ga-Ga bonds. $ ^{82} $

While the discovery of new MXenes will certainly continue, it is important to focus on answering the fundamental questions which have not been addressed by the MXenes community yet. A group of leading MXene researchers formulated a list of question in the MXenes field that should be addressed and those were discussed and ranked by several hundreds of attendees of the $ 3^{\mathrm{rd}} $ International Conference on MXenes in October 2020. $ ^{114} $ Environmentally friendly, safe, efficient, and scalable synthesis methods, control of surface chemistry, improvement in chemical stability, and understanding of physical properties of MXenes were rated as the highest priority for further expansion of the field and for moving towards practical applications. Understanding the health and environmental safety/toxicity of MXenes with different transition metals and surface terminations is also a high priority. A majority of the fundamental studies on synthesis optimization and effect of synthesis and processing parameters on MXenes' surface chemistry and properties have been carried out on $ \mathrm{T i}{3} \mathrm{C}{2} \mathrm{T}_{x} $ . Therefore, systematic studies of the effect of synthesis and processing conditions on structure, surface chemistry and properties of MXenes with different M, X and n will be of a great value for advancing our understanding of these materials.

第 12 页

MXenes are prone to oxidation at high temperatures and under oxidizing environments, $ ^{115} $ which can lead to novel architectures of nanohybrid structures of oxides/carbon $ ^{116} $ or oxide/carbon/MXenes that are found promising for use in electrodes for energy storage and conversion. $ ^{117-118} $ This oxidation is overlooked by many researchers as they report on the synthesis of MXenes under harsh oxidizing environments and the properties and performance of those materials are for a hybrid of MXene/oxide, not for pure MXene. Another, common mistake is to ignore secondary phases in the MXene samples, such as unreacted MAX phases, 3D binary carbide/nitride or unwashed salts or etching products (e.g., LiF or $ \mathrm{A l F_{3}} $ ). These impurities may strongly affect the behavior of MXene positively or negatively. Thus, careful washing to eliminate etching products from MXene and etching and thorough characterization for both MXenes and their precursors are critical to ensure reproducibility and accurate reports. $ ^{119-120} $

It is encouraging to see that several of the problems highlighted by the research community less than a year ago have already been addressed. Oxidation/degradation stability of $ \mathrm{T i}{3} \mathrm{C}{2} \mathrm{T}_{x} $ has been improved by using antioxidants $ ^{121} $ or capping the edges of MXene $ ^{122} $ and up to a year in water stability at room temperature was achieved by synthesizing highly stoichiometric MXene. $ ^{51} $ Appreciation of the effect of stoichiometry on the environmental stability is expected to lead to more stable MXenes of other compositions. These comments notwithstanding, the long term effectiveness of these oxidation mitigation strategies is unknown. This is especially true if MXenes are to be used in applications where water is used and open to the atmosphere like say in water filtration or desaltination.

Several new surface terminations have been achieved as a result of molten salt synthesis and termination exchange by Kamysbayev et al. $ ^{46} $ High-entropy MXenes have been reported. $ ^{72-73} $ New methods of synthesis utilizing salt solutions without acid additives, as well I, $ \mathrm{B r_{2}} $ and other halogens have been developed. $ ^{43,} $ $ ^{123} $ Termination-dependent superconductivity has also been demonstrated. $ ^{46} $ Modifying the surface chemistry of MXene by isocyanates rendered MXenes hydrophobic. $ ^{124} $ Exchanging Li from in-between layers with di(hydrogenated tallow)benzyl methyl ammonium chloride allows MXene to become organophilic and dispersible in nonpolar solvents. $ ^{125} $

Intercalation in MXenes plays a pivotal role in their processing, properties, and performance in many applications. However, not enough is known about the nature of

intercalants in confinement, their interaction with the catalytically active MXene nanosheets $ ^{126} $ and their interaction with solvents (e.g., confined water). Most of the work has focused on intercalation in $ \mathrm{T i}{3} \mathrm{C}{2} \mathrm{T}_{x}. $ $ ^{127-131} $ Fundamental and systematic studies are needed to gain a better understanding for intercalation in MXenes, which would allow for accurate correlation between intercalated MXenes' composition, their structure, properties and behavior in different applications.

Defects (e.g., vacancies, adatoms), their evolution mechanism during synthesis and processing and their effect on MXenes' stability and properties are important areas. Defect engineering will allow one to have more control over the properties.

Direct demonstration of band gap in semiconducting MXenes is still to be provided. Selective etching of A layers from MAX phase single crystals (viz., $ \mathrm{V}{2} \mathrm{A l C} $ and $ \mathrm{V}{4} \mathrm{A l C}_{3} $ ) results in MXenes with large lateral dimensions (hundreds of microns). $ ^{132-133} $ More efforts are needed in this direction to achieve large single crystal MXenes since their characterizations will advance our understanding of the physical properties of MXenes that might lead to new applications in the electronics industry. Roll-to-roll synthesis of single crystal MXenes is still to be demonstrated, as vapor deposition methods have not produced single-layer MXenes so far.

Hundreds of studies have been published on using MXenes alone or as additives to improve the performance of other materials. However, the limitations of MXenes are rarely discussed. More importantly, in-depth studies of the mechanisms leading to enhanced performance are limited. The MXenes community should learn from the graphene community and avoid hype, overpromises and a flood of useless and repetitive studies that do not result in advancing the knowledge or the understanding of this new materials system. $ ^{134} $

Modeling and simulation at various length and time scales have been used extensively to predict structure stability, properties and performance of MXenes in different applications, and machine learning is gaining the momentum. $ ^{135-138} $ However, we still do not know the theoretical limits for many physical properties of MXenes, such as their electronic conductivity. Many other predicted properties, from ferromagnetism to topological insulator behavior are to be demonstrated. By addressing those challenges and further improving synthesis and property characterization of MXenes, one can open pathways to many important applications.
