# Direct Atomic-Level Insight into the Active Sites of a High-Performance PGM-free ORR Catalyst

Abstract: Platinum group metal-free (PGM-free) metal-nitrogen-carbon catalysts have emerged as a promising alternative to their costly Pt-based counterparts in polymer electrolyte fuel cells (PEFCs), but still face some major challenges, including (i) the identification of the most relevant catalytic site and (ii) demonstration of competitive PEFC performance under automotive application conditions in the $ \mathrm{H_{2}} $ -air fuel cell. Herein, we demonstrate $ \mathrm{H_{2}} $ -air performance gains achieved with an iron-nitrogen-carbon catalyst synthesized with two nitrogen precursors that developed hierarchal porosity. Current densities recorded in the kinetic region of cathode operation, at fuel cell voltages greater than ca. 0.75 V, were as those obtained with a Pt cathode at a loading of $ 0.1\mathrm{mg_{P t}} \mathrm{cm}^{-2} $ . The proposed catalytic active site, $ \mathrm{FeN_{4}} $ , was directly visualized with aberration-corrected scanning transmission electron microscopy, and the contributions of these active sites associated with specific lattice-level carbon structures were was explored computationally.

One Sentence Summary: Active sites are explored based on direct observation of iron atoms in a highly active PGM-free ORR catalyst derived using a novel dual nitrogen-precursor approach.

The widespread integration of polymer electrolyte fuel cells (PEFCs) into vehicles will require substantial reductions in overall stack cost (1). The main stack cost contributor (ca. 46%) is the expensive Pt-based electrocatalysts (2). The oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) at the cathode is inherently six orders of magnitude slower than the hydrogen oxidation reaction at the anode (3) and contributes more to this cost. Thus, the implementation of high-performance and durable platinum group metal-free (PGM-free) ORR catalyst could greatly enable large-scale commercialization of fuel cell-powered vehicles.

Toward this goal, heat-treated metal-nitrogen-carbon (M-N-C) catalysts have been developed where the metal component is usually inexpensive and earth-abundant Fe or Co (4). A carbon-hosted $ \mathrm{F e N_{4}} $ structure is the most commonly proposed active site, arrived at based on both theory and experimental data from x-ray absorption, x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy, and Mössbauer spectroscopies (5-9), but this active site structure has not been directly observed and confirmed in the activated catalysts. Fuel cell performance has been improved through better control of the pore structure of PGM-free catalysts (10,11) to improve mass transport properties, as well as accessibility of active site(s). Serov et al. (11) incorporated commercial silica powder as a template in the high-temperature synthesis of M-N-C catalysts, followed by removal of the porosity-inducing template species with hydrofluoric acid. Others have used metal organic frameworks (MOFs) as precursors for PGM-free catalysts, starting with a Co-imidazolate framework (12). Among several MOF approaches, only Zn-MOF-derived PGM-free catalysts demonstrated high fuel cell performance (9,10,13), mainly attributed to the catalyst porosity resulting from Zn evaporation during the heat treatment (10).

Despite improvements in fuel cell performance with these approaches, the majority of M-N-C PEFC cathode studies have been performed under $ \mathrm{H}{2}-\mathrm{O}{2} $ conditions. The use of $ \mathrm{O}_{2} $ likely masks

the true effects of concentration polarization, commonly observed in $ \mathrm{H}{2} $ -air cells (stacks) for automotive applications. Thus, demonstration of high PGM-free catalyst performance under practical $ \mathrm{H}{2} $ -air conditions is required to validate such catalysts for practical systems.

Previously, our group has developed high-activity Fe-N-C catalysts derived from polymers and simple organic compounds, such as polyaniline (PANI) (14-16) or cyanamide (CM) (17, 18). In this the present work, PANI and CM precursors were deliberately combined to synthesize a catalyst that exhibits both hierarchical pore structures and remarkable activity, reflected by the current densities recorded in the kinetic region of the air cathode operation, at fuel cell voltages greater than ca. 0.75 V, as those obtained with a Pt cathode at a loading of $ 0.1 \mathrm{m g}{\mathrm{P t}} \mathrm{c m}^{-2} $ . Atomiclevel images of the $ \mathrm{F e N}{4} $ active sites were obtained with low-voltage (60kV), aberration-corrected scanning transmission electron microscopy (AC-STEM). Electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS) with single-atom resolution confirmed such $ \mathrm{F e N}{4} $ structures embedded with in carbon basal planes. However, much significantly higher concentrations of dispersed Fe single atoms were observed at or near the surfaces of graphitic domains or directly associated with exposed multi-layered graphene/graphite basal plane edges/steps. These Based on the prevalence of such edge-hosted structures observed in the catalyst, these specific configurations were explored using theoretical approaches, which pointed to a relatively high ORR activity for multi-layer graphene (basal-plane)-edge-hosted $ \mathrm{F e N}{4} $ structures. Noteworthy, these structures can be operational in the catalysis of a variety of other electrochemical reactions, some which have already been reported to be very promising (19).

The catalyst's hierarchical pore structure plays a crucial role in exposing the a large fraction of graphite (002) basal-plane edges of the carbon phases comprising the catalyst to dioxygen. These surface-terminated basal planes are dominant in the carbon structures and are most notably

associated with small, randomly oriented graphitic grains/crystals that form the predominant carbon phase, with Fe present at the exposed plane edges on the surfaces (see below). The high ORR activity measured through RRDE testing cannot be directly correlated to high fuel cell performance because of the fundamental differences in operating environment, temperature, and electrode structures between RDE and fuel cells. Thus, evaluation of PGM-free catalysts under realistic fuel cell operation, i.e., $ \mathrm{H}{2} $ -air conditions, is critical to assess performance from a practical standpoint, especially since the conventionally used $ \mathrm{H}{2}-\mathrm{O}_{2} $ test conditions do not capture the important effects of mass transport in the cathode.

Performance of (CM+PANI)-Fe-C cathode catalyst layers (CCLs) in the membrane electrode assembly (MEA) was investigated under realistic conditions for the cathode operated on air (Fig. 2B). The maximum power density reached with an electrode containing 35 wt.% Nafion ionomer was 0.39 W $ \mathrm{cm}^{-2} $ at 1.0 bar partial pressure of $ \mathrm{H}{2} $ and air (the sum of partial pressures of $ \mathrm{O}{2} $ and $ \mathrm{N}_{2} $ ). To better understand the source of high ORR activity, the atomic-level structure and chemistry of the (CM+PANI)-Fe-C catalyst was were studied in detail by aberration-corrected (AC)-STEM and electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS). Previous theoretical studies (6,33) suggest that edge-hosted $ \mathrm{F e_{x} N_{y}} $ structures had lower formation energies, i.e., an increased relative stability, than bulk-hosted structures. Thus, the equilibrium concentration of the edge-hosted structures is expected to be substantially higher than bulk-hosted structures. Because most of the Fe observed by STEM imaging is present at the basal plane edges and steps, and theoretical studies suggest higher concentration of edge-hosted $ \mathrm{F e N_{x}} $ structures, we used quantum chemical modeling to study the relative ORR reaction pathways of edge-hosted $ \mathrm{F e N_{4}} $ sites vs. bulk-hosted $ \mathrm{F e N_{4}} $ sites and to gain further insight into the possible predominant active sites in the (CM+PANI)-Fe-C catalyst.

In addition to relative equilibrium concentrations, previous quantum chemistry studies of M-N-C active-site structures suggest that edge-adjacent $ \mathrm{F e_{2} N_{5}} $ configurations have high ORR activity (34). Using the same methodology (20), we calculated the reaction pathways (fig. S13) and thermodynamic limiting potential, $ U_{l} $ , that serves as descriptor for relative ORR activity for the graphene bulk-hosted and nanoribbon zigzag edge-hosted structures. Similar to the previously reported edge $ \mathrm{F e_{2} N_{5}} $ structure (34), both bulk-hosted (Fig. 4, A, C, and E) and zigzag edge-hosted (Fig. 4, B, D, and F) $ \mathrm{F e N_{4}} $ structures spontaneously evolved an OH ligand at relevant potentials. With the OH ligand attached, the bulk-hosted $ \mathrm{F e N_{4}} $ does not exothermically bind $ \mathrm{O_{2}} $ , and a bound OOH spontaneously dissociates to a bound O and free OH. These results suggest that such an OH-

第 19 页

modified site may not act as a single reaction site on an associative ORR pathway. However, the zigzag edge-hosted $ \mathrm{F e N}{4} $ with OH ligand does bind $ \mathrm{O}{2} $ and, on an associative pathway, has a limiting potential of 0.80 V. This $ U_{l} $ is equal to the highest reported value (33, 34), in which the initial chemical adsorption of $ \mathrm{O}{2} $ was considered. Thus, quantum chemistry calculations show that the $ \mathrm{F e N}{4} $ structures follow different ORR reaction pathways, depending on whether they are hosted in the bulk or at the edge of the graphene, and that the edge-hosted sites, when spontaneously ligated by OH in the fuel cell environment, lead to highly ORR-active structures.

Though $ \mathrm{F e N_{4}} $ sites at the exposed basal plane edges could not be directly discerned via ACSTEM, the significantly higher concentration of Fe associated with edges and steps observed by STEM imaging combined with the quantum chemical calculations suggested supported higher populations as well as higher ORR activities with the edge-hosted $ \mathrm{F e N_{4}} $ compared with the bulkhosted $ \mathrm{F e N_{4}} $ . Thus, we can plausibly suggest that edge-hosted $ \mathrm{F e N_{4}} $ sites are likely the major contributors to the overall high activity observed in both the RDE and MEA testing of the (CM+PANI)-Fe-C catalyst.
