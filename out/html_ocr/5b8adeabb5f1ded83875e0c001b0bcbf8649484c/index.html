Abstract

Exploring efficient and low-cost electrocatalysts for Oxygen Reduction Reaction (ORR) and Oxygen Evolution Reaction (OER) is critical for developing renewable energy technologies like fuel cells, metal-air batteries, and water electrolyzers. A rational design of a catalyst can be guided by identifying descriptors that determine its activity. This article presents a descriptor study on ORR/OER of spinel oxides. With a series of $ \mathrm{M n C o_{2} O_{4}} $ , the Mn in octahedral sites was identified as active site. This finding was then applied to successfully explain the ORR/OER activities of other transition metal spinels, including $ \mathrm{M n_{x} C o_{3-x} O_{4}} $ （x=2,2.5,3), $ \mathrm{L i_{x} M n_{2} O_{4}} $ （x=0.7,1), $ \mathrm{X C o_{2} O_{4}} $ （X=Co,Ni,Zn), and $ \mathrm{X F e_{2} O_{4}} $ （X=Mn,Co,Ni). We conclude a general principle that the $ \mathrm{e_{g}} $ occupancy of active cation in octahedral site is the activity descriptor for ORR/OER of spinels, consolidating the role of electron orbital filling in metal oxide catalysis.

第 4 页

Oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) and oxygen evolution reaction (OER) are critical elements in many renewable energy technologies such as fuel cells, metal-air batteries, and water electrolyzers. The sluggish kinetics of these two oxygen electrocatalytic reactions results in the requirement of high overpotentials and therefore considerable energy loss. Great effort has been made in the past decades in exploring active catalysts for these two reactions. The most efficient catalysts for ORR and OER have been identified as $ \mathrm{P t} $ $ ^{[1a,4]} $ and $ \mathrm{I r O_{2}} $ & $ \mathrm{R u O_{2}} $ $ ^{[5]} $ respectively. Unfortunately, these state-of-art catalysts are precious metal-based and the scarcity of these elements hampered their large-scale application. With comparable activities to precious metal-based catalysts, earth-abundant metal oxides have become increasingly attractive in these years, particularly like manganese-based oxides for ORR $ ^{[6]} $ and cobalt-based oxides for OER. In pursuit of further enhanced oxygen electrocatalytic activity, it is necessary to understand the role of transition metal cations in oxides and to identify the activity descriptor. Inspired by the success of d-band center model in elucidating some catalysis reaction at metal surfaces (e.g., ORR, $ ^{[4,8]} $ hydrogen evolution reaction, $ ^{[9]} $ CO oxidation $ ^{[10]} $ and $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ reduction), recent efforts have focused on molecular orbital theory, which correlates the catalyst-reactant bond strength to the occupancy of antibonding orbitals. The established activity descriptors for perovskite family include the number of d electrons, $ ^{[12]} $ $ \mathrm{e_{g}} $ occupancy, $ ^{[13]} $ bulk formation energy, $ ^{[12b]} $ metal-oxygen covalency, $ ^{[3b,13a]} $ and position of O-p band center. $ ^{[3a,7a]} $ However, it is not apparent if these descriptors found in the perovskite structure can be applied universally to other oxide structural families such as the spinels, which consist of metal cations occupying in octahedral and tetrahedral sites. Recently, there has been increased interest in spinel oxides for ORR/OER. Remarkable performance has been demonstrated using spinel catalysts such as $ \mathrm{N i C o_{2} O_{4}} $ $ ^{[14]} $ $ \mathrm{M n F e_{2} O_{4}} $ $ ^{[15]} $ $ \mathrm{C o_{x} M n_{3-x} O_{4}} $ $ ^{[16]} $ $ \mathrm{C o_{3} O_{4}} $ /rmGO (reduced graphene

In the spinel structure, the coexistence of tetrahedral and octahedral sites (Figure 1a) provides multiple sites for accommodating different transition metal cations and in a wide range of valence states to form a large number of oxides. This work focuses on transition metal spinels and reports key parameters that govern the ORR and OER activities. We start from tuning the electronic structure of $ \mathrm{M n C o_{2} O_{4}} $ cubic spinels by varying the synthesis temperature. Utilizing the X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS), the correlation between ORR/OER catalysis and their physicochemical properties (e.g., Mn/Co valence state, cation distribution) has been systematically studied. Mn valence state on the octahedral sites forms a volcano trend with the ORR/OER activity. The discovered governing role of $ \mathrm{e_{g}} $ filling of the active cation in octahedral sites is further explored for explaining the origins of ORR/OER catalytic activities on other transition metal spinel oxides, such as $ \mathrm{M n_{x} C o_{3-x} O_{4}} $ tetragonal spinels, $ \mathrm{L i_{x} M n_{2} O_{4}} $ （x=0.7,1), $ \mathrm{X C o_{2} O_{4}} $ （X=Co, Ni, Zn), and $ \mathrm{X F e_{2} O_{4}} $ （X=Mn, Co, Ni) cubic spinels.

$ \mathrm{M n C o_{2} O_{4}} $ cubic spinels were synthesized by solid state chemistry method at different temperatures. Oxides used in this work were pure phase (Figure 1b) except for $ \mathrm{M n C o_{2} O_{4}} $ -500 $ ^{\circ} \mathrm{C} $ , where $ < 3 $ w% $ \mathrm{M n O_{x}} / \mathrm{C o O_{x}} $ is present (Figure S1). The impurities in $ \mathrm{M n C o_{2} O_{4}} $ -500 $ ^{\circ} \mathrm{C} $ are considered to be inevitable. Even calcination in form of pellets for a longer duration (with intermediate re-grinding and re-pressing) does not give a complete single phase (Figure S1). Transmission electron microscopy images (Figure S2) show as-synthesized $ \mathrm{M n C o_{2} O_{4}} $ particles are in polycrystalline, different sizes, and irregular shapes. This eliminates the concern that ORR/OER performance differs from different facets/orientation. $ ^{[5a]} $

In this work, the change in the Mn valence state has been correlated with ORR/OER activities. First, in the K-edge X-ray near edge spectroscopy (XANES, Figure 2a and 2b), Mn exhibits significant edge shifts with various synthesis temperatures; but the position of Co K-edge remains almost constant, which indicates that the valence state of Mn is decreasing with increasing temperature, but Co not. ORR and OER geometric current densities of $ \mathrm{M n C o_{2} O_{4}} $ -900 $ ^{\circ} \mathrm{C} $ , as an example, are shown in Figure 3a and 3b. The ORR limiting current density reaches $ \sim 5. 5 \mathrm{~ m A c m}^{-2}{\mathrm{d i s k}} $ , which is within 10% of the theoretical value for $ 4 \mathrm{e}^{-} $ pathway. $ ^{[23]} $ This rationalizes the application of Koutecky-Levich analysis on electron transfer number. $ ^{[23]} $ The Koutecky-Levich plot is parallel with Pt/C and the slope is 0.151 $ \mathrm{m A c m}^{-2}{\mathrm{d i s k}} $ rpm $ ^{-0. 5} $ (Figure 3a, inset), indicating a $ 4 \mathrm{e}^{-} $ ORR reaction. $ ^{[6 b]} $ Since catalysis only involves the atoms on the surface, $ ^{[24]} $ geometric activity does not reflect the intrinsic catalytic activity. To obtain the intrinsic activity, we normalize the ORR/OER kinetic current density to the surface area of the catalyst, which is determined by Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) measurement (Figure 1c and S6), generating the so-called specific activity $ \left( \mathrm{i}{s} \right) $ . It is reasonable that BET surface area is not equivalent to the real electrochemically active surface area. However, assessing ORR/OER activity by BET surface area has been well established as a benchmark methodology to screen metal oxide catalysts. $ ^{[25]} $ The drop-casted electrode is able to reflect the difference of surface area characterized by BET technique $ ^{[25]} $ This is further proved by our result that the capacitive current density recorded at the potential window of ORR/OER measurement (Figure S7) compares well with BET result, confirming the applicability of BET surface area to calculate the specific activity. The surface-area-normalized specific activity of all $ \mathrm{M n C o{2} O_{4}} $ cubic spinels are plotted as a function of iR-corrected potential (Figure 3c and 3d). To demonstrate the reliability of our electrochemical measurement, state-of-art catalysts were also tested. Commercial Pt/C (40 wt%, Sigma-Aldrich) gives an ORR limiting current density of $ \sim 5. 7 \mathrm{~ m A c m}^{-2}{\mathrm{d i s k}} $ (Figure S8a) and the Tafel plots of mass activity $ (1 6 0 \mathrm{~ m A m g}^{-1}{\mathrm{P t}} $ at 0.9 V vs. RHE) and specific activity $ (3 8 0 \mu \mathrm{A c m}^{-2}{\mathrm{P t}} $ at 0.9 V vs. RHE) are consistent with previous works. $ ^{[25 b, 2 6]} $ Moreover, OER specific current density (Figure S8b) of $ \mathrm{I r O{2}} $ polycrystalline nanoparticles also agrees well with landmark reports. $ ^{[27]} $

The ORR Tafel plots of different $ \mathrm{M n C o_{2} O_{4}} $ spinels (Figure 3c) locate across a potential window of 200 mV, indicating the activity difference is $ \sim 3 $ orders of magnitude; and the OER activity (Figure 3d) spans over $ \sim 2 $ orders of magnitude. Generally speaking (Figure 3c and 3d), higher synthesis temperature gives better ORR/OER activity. Of notable interest is that the intrinsic ORR/OER activity of $ \mathrm{M n C o_{2} O_{4}} $ $ - 9 0 0 \mathrm{~} ^ {\circ} \mathrm{C} $ is approaching to state-of-art $ \mathrm{P t / C} $ $ ^{[25\mathrm{b}}] $ and $ \mathrm{I r O_{2}} $ catalysts, $ ^{[27]} $ respectively.

To understand the different ORR/OER activities on $ \mathrm{M n C o_{2} O_{4}} $ , it is proposed that Mn cations on the octahedral site are key for catalyzing ORR/OER of spinels and that the ORR/OER activity is correlated with the Mn valence on the site. To support this hypothesis, we firstly compared the ORR/OER performance of $ \mathrm{C o_{3} O_{4}} $ and $ \mathrm{Z n C o_{2} O_{4}} $ (XRD, BET results in Figure S9 and S10). $ \mathrm{Z n}^{2+} $ cation has a strong preference for the tetrahedral site $ ^{[20a,28]} $ and is considered as inactive because of its $ \mathrm{d}^{10} $ electronic structure. Thus a comparison between $ \mathrm{C o_{3} O_{4}} $ and $ \mathrm{Z n C o_{2} O_{4}} $ gives an opportunity to reveal the role of tetrahedrally coordinated $ \mathrm{C o}^{2+} $ . As shown in Figure S11, both the ORR and OER Tafel plots of these two catalysts almost overlap, suggesting the tetrahedrally coordinated $ \mathrm{C o}^{2+} $ is not significantly more active than the octahedrally coordinated $ \mathrm{C o}^{3+} $ . Our finding is consistent with several previous studies on spinels: electrochemical deposited $ \mathrm{C o_{3} O_{4}} $ and $ \mathrm{Z n C o_{2} O_{4}} $ electrodes show same OER activity; $ ^{[28]} $ the replacement of $ \mathrm{L i^{+}} $ by $ \mathrm{C o}^{2+} $ at tetrahedral site does not improve the OER performance at $ \mathrm{L i_{2} C o_{2} O_{4}} $; $ ^{[7c]} $ on the other hand, $ \mathrm{C o_{3} O_{4}} $ outperforms $ (\mathrm{C o}^{2+}){\mathrm{t e t}}(\mathrm{F e}{2}^{3+}){\mathrm{o c t}} \mathrm{O}{4} $ in ORR. $ ^{[29]} $ Based on the above set of results, we suggest that the octahedral site plays the leading role in spinel catalysts. Secondly, metal cation with tetrahedral coordination has different d-orbital splitting compared with octahedral coordination. $ ^{[30]} $ In octahedral coordination (Figure S12), the high-lying $ \mathrm{e_{g}} $ orbital directly points to O, generating a strong spatial overlap with O-2p orbital. The strong overlap rationalizes the role of $ \mathrm{e_{g}} $ as the activity descriptor in perovskite catalysts. While in tetrahedral coordination (Figure S12), neither the high-lying $ \mathrm{e} $ nor the low-lying $ \mathrm{t_{2}} $ orbital directs its shape towards O. The angle between the e-orbital and the ligand is $ 54^{\circ}44^{\prime} $ ; for $ \mathrm{t_{2}} $ , is $ 35^{\circ}16^{\prime} $ . The mismatch causes weaker spatial overlap with O. $ ^{[30]} $ This results in a generally weak interaction between tetrahedrally-coordinated metal and O, as evidenced by $ \mathrm{C o}^{2+} $ in spinel tetrahedral site. $ ^{[31]} $

It should be noticed that earlier reports $ ^{32} $ consider that each site is only occupied by Mn cation of single valency (such as $ \mathrm{MnFe_{2}O_{4+\delta}} $ , where the tetrahedral site is solely occupied by $ \mathrm{Mn}^{2+} $ and octahedral site is only by $ \mathrm{Mn}^{3+} $ ). $ ^{32} $ Recent studies, $ ^{16a,21} $ however, have revealed that the multi-valency of Mn can present in both tetrahedral and octahedral sites. The above findings indicate that Mn governs the ORR/OER activity of $ \mathrm{MnCo_{2}O_{4}} $ spinels, while Co plays a minor role. This seems to be conflicting with the literature observation that the top oxygen electrocatalysts are usually Co-containing oxides. $ ^{7a,13b,25a} $ However, it needs to be noticed that when Co is put in the environment of spinel structure, the activity is usually low, $ ^{7c,31,33} $ and thus the catalysis relies more on other cations. We further examine if the correlation of Mn valency on the octahedral sites to the activity of ORR/OER can be applied to other transition metal spinels. To correlate activities across different transition metal cations, we therefore postulate that the $ e_{\mathrm{g}} $ occupancy of active transition metal cations at octahedral site is the activity descriptor for ORR/OER at spinels following earlier investigations of this issue. $ ^{[7\mathrm{c},28-29]} $ The cation distribution was quantified by EXAFS (Figure S14), and the $ e_{\mathrm{g}}/t_{2\mathrm{g}} $ electron assignment was obtained by knowing the valence state from XANES (Figure S15) and spin state from literature data (Table S6). The intrinsic ORR/OER activity of cubic spinel is aligned against the as-extracted $ e_{\mathrm{g}} $ occupancy (Figure 5). The striking volcano shape demonstrates the applicability of our principle to unify the ORR/OER activity prediction of transition metal spinels: $ e_{\mathrm{g}} $ filling of octahedral element and the occupation of the active element at the octahedral site simultaneously govern oxygen electrocatalysis performance. It can be seen that $ \mathrm{M n C o_{2} O_{4}} $ spinels locate at the left branch of the OER volcano plot, which confirms our previous explanation on the linear dependence in Figure 4b. This theory also explains the recent observation on $ \mathrm{L i_{2} C o_{2} O_{4}} $ $ ^{[7\mathrm{c},34]} $ where the spin state switch from $ \mathrm{C o}^{3+} $ $ (t_{2\mathrm{g}}^{6}-x\mathrm{e}{\mathrm{g}}^{\mathrm{x}}), $ low spin) to $ \mathrm{C o}^{3+} $ $ (t{2\mathrm{g}}^{6}-x\mathrm{e}{\mathrm{g}}^{\mathrm{x}}), $ $ x \approx 1 $ , intermediate spin) at the octahedral site gives drastically enhanced ORR/OER activity. Note that our principle is different from the $ e{\mathrm{g}} $ theory in perovskite, $ ^{[13]} $ where there is only one geometric site. The prerequisite of our finding is to resolve the occupation argument, identifying octahedral coordination as the active site with the aid of EXAFS fitting and electrochemical measurement.

The role of $ e_{g} $ electron at the octahedral site can be explained by the four-step proton/electron-coupled reaction mechanism, $ ^{[35]} $ where the binding strength of ORR/OER reaction intermediates is governed by $ e_{g} $ filling. $ ^{[13]} $ At the left branch of ORR volcano profile $ (e_{g} < 1) $ $ M_{\mathrm{oct}} $ - $ O^{2-} $ bond is so strong that the limiting step is $ \mathrm{O H}^{-} $ regeneration (step 4, Figure S16). At the right branch $ (e_{g} > 1) $ , the ORR kinetics is hindered by the formation of stable $ M_{\mathrm{oct}} $ - $ O O^{2-} $ and the limiting step is $ O O^{2-} / O H^{-} $ displacement (step 1). As for OER, too less $ e_{g} $ electron $ (e_{g} < 1) $ limits the rate of proton removal of $ M_{\mathrm{oct}} $ - $ O O H^{-} $ (step 3, Figure S16) to form $ M_{\mathrm{oct}} $ - $ O O^{2-} $ , and too much $ e_{g} $ electron filling $ (e_{g} > 1) $ retards the O-O bond formation in $ M_{\mathrm{oct}} $ - $ O O H^{-} $ (step 2). The ORR/OER activity can be optimized at a moderate $ e_{g} $ filling $ (e_{g} \approx 1) $ at the octahedral site, which balances the competition between rate-limiting steps.

Transition metal cations on the tetrahedral site give different electron assignments (Figure S12 and Table S6). The spatial overlap of d-orbitals with oxygen ligands is poor, and the e antibonding orbitals are over-filled (more than 2), indicating weak interactions with molecular oxygen and oxygenated species. The different crystal field on the octahedral site induced by the Jahn-Teller effect $ ^{[36]} $ (the elongation of c lattice parameter and consequently the increasing Mn-O bonding length along z-axis: see Figure S18) does not appear to influence the ORR/OER activity. Our results demonstrate tetragonal $ (\mathrm{M n}{x-2} \mathrm{C o}{3-x}){\mathrm{t e t}}(\mathrm{M n}{2}){\mathrm{o c t}} \mathrm{O}{4+ \delta} $ spinel (red circle, Figure 5) does not deviate from the observed trend in comparison to cubic $ (\mathrm{C o}){\mathrm{t e t}}(\mathrm{M n C o}){\mathrm{o c t}} \mathrm{O}{4+ \delta} $ . This observation is consistent with the ORR/OER examination on perovskites of 3 different space groups: the orthorhombic (space group, Pnma) crystals can have 3 metal-oxygen bonding lengths at the octahedral site (Table S7) but they all fit the $ e{\mathrm{g}} $ theory. $ ^{[13]} $ We notice that the spinels with Co or Ni as the active site $ (\mathrm{C o}{3} \mathrm{O}{4}, \mathrm{Z n C o}{2} \mathrm{O}{4}, $ and $ \mathrm{N i C o}{2} \mathrm{O}{4} $ positively deviate from the expected trend. In summary, we found that the ORR/OER activity of various $ \mathrm{M n C o_{2} O_{4}} $ exhibits a volcano shape as a function of the Mn valence state in octahedral sites. The summit of the ORR/OER volcano trend locates at the Mn valency of $ \sim+3 $ . The as-revealed governing role of octahedral-coordinated Mn valency was further extended to quantitatively describing the ORR/OER activities of other transition metal spinels (17 total). We conclude that the $ \mathrm{e_{g}} $ occupancy of the active cation on the octahedral sites is the activity descriptor for spinels. This work demonstrates that $ \mathrm{e_{g}} $ theory is not only applicable to perovskite family, but also spinel family. This may possibly inspire more insight studies into the catalysis in nature: oxygen evolving complex (OEC) in Photosystem II (PSII), $ ^{[37]} $ where the $ \mathrm{M n}^{3+} $ is under a quasi-spherical octahedral coordination and gives the best OER performance on this planet. $ ^{[3b]} $

Materials Synthesis: $ \mathrm{M n C o_{2} O_{4}} $ cubic spinels were synthesized by a modified conventional solid state chemistry method $ ^{[38]} $ with $ \mathrm{M n(NO_{3}){2}} $ and $ \mathrm{C o(NO{3}){2}} $ as precursors. The synthesis reaction was performed at various temperatures (400, 500, 700, and $ 9 0 0 \mathrm{~} ^ {\circ} \mathrm{C} $ ) to prepare $ \mathrm{M n C o{2} O_{4}} $ oxides with different physical properties. $ \mathrm{C o_{3} O_{4}} $ , $ \mathrm{Z n C o_{2} O_{4}} $ , $ \mathrm{N i C o_{2} O_{4}} $ , and $ \mathrm{M n_{x} C o_{3}-} $ $ \mathrm{x O_{4}} $ were synthesized by the same method. $ \mathrm{M n C o_{2} O_{4}-1 5 0} $ $ ^{\circ} \mathrm{C} $ was produced by hydrothermally treating $ \mathrm{C o(O A c){2}} $ (cobalt acetate) and $ \mathrm{M n(O A c){2}} $ (manganese acetate) in aqueous solution at $ 1 5 0 \mathrm{~} ^ {\circ} \mathrm{C} $ for 3 hr. $ \mathrm{M n C o_{2} O_{4}-3 0 0} $ $ ^{\circ} \mathrm{C} $ , $ \mathrm{C o F e_{2} O_{4}} $ , and $ \mathrm{N i F e_{2} O_{4}} $ nanoparticles were prepared through a modified colloidal method. $ \mathrm{M n(ac a c){3}} $ , $ \mathrm{C o(ac a c){2}} $ , $ \mathrm{F e(ac a c){3}} $ , and $ \mathrm{N i(ac a c){2}} $ were used as precursors and oleylamine served as the solvent, surfactants, and

第 12 页

reducing agent. After refluxing at $ 3 0 0 \mathrm{~} ^ {\circ} \mathrm{C} $ for 1 hr, nanoparticle powders were collected and washed by centrifuging in a mixture of hexane and iso-propanol. Before structure characterization and electrochemical tests, as-obtained nanoparticle powders were calcinated at $ 3 0 0 \mathrm{~} ^ {\circ} \mathrm{C} $ to remove the residual surfactant attached on catalyst surface. Detailed synthesis procedures are shown in the Supporting Information

Electrochemical Characterization: As-synthesized spinel catalyst, acetylene black (AB), and $ \mathrm{N a^{+}} $ -exchanged Nafion were mixed by ultrasonication in tetrahydrofuran (THF) to form the catalyst ink. 10 $ \mu\mathrm{L} $ of as-prepared ink with the final concentrations of $ 1.8\mathrm{m g_{O x i d e}} $ mL $ ^{-1} $ $ 4.2\mathrm{m g_{A B}} $ mL $ ^{-1} $ , and $ 1\mathrm{m g_{N a f i o n}} $ mL $ ^{-1} $ was drop-cast onto the rotating disk (glassy carbon) electrode (RDE, $ 0.196\mathrm{c m}^{2} $ , PINE instrument). Oxygen electrocatalysis experiments were conducted in 0.1 M KOH with a Pt wire counter electrode and a saturated calomel reference electrode. Capacity-correction, mass transport-correction, and iR-correction were performed to extract the ORR activity. OER activity was obtained after capacity- and iR-corrections. The resistance was measured by EIS impedance technique. Tafel plots were normalized to the catalyst surface area, which was determined by BET measurement. Procedures for electrode preparation, ORR/OER tests and data processing are detailed in the Supporting Information.

X-ray Absorption Spectroscopy: X-ray absorption near edge spectroscopy (XANES) and extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) experiments were carried out at beamline 9BM-C of the XOR Division, APS, ANL. Data reduction, data analysis and EXAFS fitting were performed with the Athena, Artemis and IFEFFIT software packages. Instead of the conventional inflection method, an integral method was used to accurately estimate the edge position. In order to reliably get site occupancy from EXAFS fitting, we employed multiedge

第 13 页

fitting method: Mn and Co K-edge at the same sample were fitted simultaneously. The site occupancy of each sample was described by one parameter, the number of Mn at tetrahedral sites. The theoretical model was built by referencing landmark works $ ^{[20]} $ and had been used in our previous work. $ ^{[39]} $ Because the fitting range (from 1 $ \mathrm{\AA} $ to $ \sim 3.5 \mathrm{\AA} $ ) covers a large number of scattering paths, some constraints and approximations were used. The model validity and fitting reliability were confirmed by R factor and $ x^{2} $ . Detailed description of XAS data analysis and additional data are included in the Supporting Information.
