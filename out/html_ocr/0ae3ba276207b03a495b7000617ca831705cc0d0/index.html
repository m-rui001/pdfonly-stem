Abstract

Electrochemically converting nitrate ions, a widely distributed nitrogen source in industrial wastewater and polluted groundwater, to ammonia represents a sustainable route for both wastewater treatment and ammonia generation. However, it is currently hindered by low catalytic activities especially under low nitrate concentrations. Here we report a high-performance Ru dispersed Cu nanowire catalyst which delivers an industrial-relevant nitrate reduction current of 1 A $ cm^{-2} $ while maintaining a high $ NH_{3} $ Faradaic efficiency of 93%. More importantly, this high nitrate reduction catalytic activity enables over 99% nitrate conversion to ammonia, from an industrial wastewater level of 2000 ppm to a drinkable water level < 50 ppm, while still maintaining over 90% Faradaic efficiency. Coupling the nitrate reduction effluent stream with an air stripping process, we successfully obtained high purity solid $ NH_{4} Cl $ and liquid $ NH_{3} $ solution products, suggesting a practical approach to convert wastewater nitrate into valuable ammonia products. Density functional theory calculations reveal that the highly dispersed Ru atoms provide active nitrate reduction sites while the surrounding Cu sites can suppress the main side reaction, the hydrogen evolution reaction.

Introduction

Ammonia $ \mathrm{(N H_{3}} $ is a versatile and foundational compound in modern society, used in everything from chemical synthesis and fertilizers to fuels and clean energy carriers $ ^{1-4} $ Today, global ammonia demand exceeds 150 million tons per year $ ^{5,6} $ while consuming 2% of the world energy and releasing 1.4% of global $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ emissions $ ^{6-8} $ This is mainly due to the energy-intensive Haber-Bosch ammonia synthesis process that currently dominates the worldwide production. In the Haber-Bosch process, steam-reformed hydrogen $ \left( \mathrm{H}{2} \right) $ reacts with $ \mathrm{N}{2} $ under elevated temperatures $ (\sim 500^{\circ} \mathrm{C}) $ and pressures $ (>100 $ atm), which not only contributes a significant carbon footprint, but also requires large infrastructure $ ^{9-11} $ . Recently, electrocatalytic methods have emerged as a clean energy route for decentralized ammonia production at room temperature, at a variety of infrastructure scales, and potentially driven by locally generated renewable energy $ ^{12-15} $ . While traditional ammonia production route is highly likely to persist in the near future considering the gigantic global demand, the electrochemical synthesis could significantly contribute to decarbonizing ammonia production.

Electrochemical $ \mathrm{N}{2} $ reduction reaction (NRR) for ammonia synthesis has been extensively studied for the past decade $ ^{16-20} $ . However, the current $ \mathrm{NH}{3} $ production rate through NRR is extremely low $ (\sim0.1\mathrm{mA}\mathrm{cm}^{-2}) $ due to the sluggish dissociation step of the highly stable $ \mathrm{N}\equiv\mathrm{N} $ bond $ ^{15,18,21} $ . This low production rate is far from industrial requirements and may even make experimental quantifications unreliable due to trace impurities $ ^{21-23} $ Conversely, the electrochemical nitrate reduction reaction $ (\mathrm{NO}{3}\mathrm{RR}) $ does not require the dissociation of high energy bonds, and thus achieves faster reaction rates for $ \mathrm{NH}{3} $ production $ ^{3,23-25} $ . Moreover, nitrate $ (\mathrm{NO}{3}^{-}) $ is an abundant nitrogen source particularly in industrial wastewater and polluted groundwater that contributes to the global nitrogen cycle imbalance $ ^{26-28} $ . Therefore, $ \mathrm{NO}{3}\mathrm{RR} $ not only stands out as a promising pathway for renewable $ \mathrm{NH}_{3} $ production, but simultaneously facilitates the denitrification of wastewater and balances the perturbed nitrogen cycle $ ^{23,29,30} $

Developing efficient electrocatalysts for nitrate reduction is a prerequisite. Commonly $ \mathrm{N O_{3} R R} $ is directed through the half-cell reaction:

The goal of catalysts is to direct reduction all the way to ammonia, while minimizing side products from different $ \mathrm{N O_{3}} $ RR pathways, or hydrogen from hydrogen evolution reaction (HER), to achieve high $ \mathrm{N H_{3}} $ Faradaic efficiencies (FEs). Previous studies have demonstrated different catalysts for selective $ \mathrm{N O_{3}}^{-} $ -to- $ \mathrm{N H_{3}} $ conversion $ ^{31-40} $ , but most of them were evaluated under relatively high nitrate concentrations where the competing reactions can be minimized. However, many practical nitrate resources such as industrial wastewater has low concentrations ranging from hundreds to thousands of $ \mathrm{p p m}^{2 9,3 0} $ . To date, it is still challenging to achieve both high current densities ( $ >1 0 0 \mathrm{m A c m}^{-2} $ ) and high ammonia FE ( $ >90\% $ ) under low nitrate concentrations due to the vigorous HER competition.

Here we developed a $ \mathrm{N O_{3} R R} $ electrocatalyst by dispersing Ru atoms into a Cu nanowire matrix (Ru-CuNW) for highly selective and active ammonia generation under low nitrate concentrations. Our Ru-CuNW catalyst organically combines Ru's low nitrate activation barrier and Cu's inert HER activity. As a result, our Ru-CuNW delivers an industrial-relevant $ \mathrm{N H_{3}} $ production rate of $ \sim1 $ A $ \mathrm{c m}^{-2} $ while maintaining a high $ \mathrm{N H_{3}} $ FE of 96% when operating at $ \mathrm{N O_{3}}^{-} $ concentrations of typical industrial wastewater (2000 ppm), outperforming previously reported catalysts $ ^{31-40} $ . More importantly, this high $ \mathrm{N O_{3} R R} $ activity enables an over 99% nitrate conversion to ammonia from an industrial wastewater level to a drinkable water level. By coupling electrochemical $ \mathrm{N O_{3} R R} $ with air stripping, we demonstrate a full conversion process from low-concentration nitrate sources to highpurity ammonia products for direct downstream applications. Our density functional theory (DFT) study reveals that the high nitrate-to-ammonia performance arises from the highly dispersed Ru active site, while the supporting Cu sites suppress the parasitic side reactions.

Catalyst synthesis and morphology characterizations. Ru-CuNW catalyst was synthesized through a cation exchange method on Cu nanowire matrix (Fig. 1a; see Methods). Cu nanowire (CuNW) and Ru nanoparticle (RuNP) control samples were also synthesized and characterized to compare catalytic performances (Supplementary Figs. 1a and 2). Other control samples, including different Ru doping levels, and different noble metal dopants such as Rh, Os, and Ir, which have been proposed by previous simulation studies to be active NRR candidates $ ^{16,17,41} $ , were also prepared, characterized, and evaluated (Supplementary Figs. 1b-d, 3-5).

Scanning electron microscope (SEM) images (Supplementary Fig. 6) showed the morphology changes from copper hydroxide nanowire $ \mathrm{(C u(OH)_{2} N W)} $ to Ru-doped CuO nanowire (Ru-CuONW); Transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Supplementary Figs. 7-9) and high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM, Fig. 1b-d and Supplementary Fig. 10) further revealed their morphology differences. The diameter of the nanowire expanded from $ \sim 50 $ (Fig. 1b) to $ \sim 100 $ nm after the Ru-ion exchange process (Fig. 1c,d), and became ultra-porous with an 5-fold increase in its electrochemically active surface area (ECSA, results will be discussed later in detail). Interestingly, we observed a hollow nanowire structure (Fig. 1c) in Ru-CuONW, which can be attributed to the Kirkendall effect $ ^{4 2-4 4} $ . This implies the lattice Cu atoms diffuse outward and exchange with the Ru ions during the synthesis. Notably, the hollow core was filled again after the electrochemical pre-reduction process, which reduced the surface oxide to metal and rearranged the lattice while maintaining the high-surface-area porous structure (Fig. 1d). We performed high-resolution HAADF-STEM and confirmed that the lattices in Ru-CuNW correspond to metallic Cu (Fig. 1e,f, Supplementary Fig. 11). Energy dispersive spectroscopic (EDS) elemental mapping revealed uniform dispersion of Ru dopants in the CuNW matrix in our catalysts (Fig. 1g, Supplementary Figs. 12-14), with a Ru:Cu atomic ratio of approximately 1:9 (Supplementary Fig. 15).

The electrochemical $ \mathrm{N O_{3} R R} $ performance of RuCuNW catalyst was investigated under ambient temperature/pressure in a standard threeelectrode H-type cell (See Methods). by ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectrophotometry with calibration curves (Supplementary Figs. 16-18; see Methods). An industrial wastewater relevant nitrate concentration of 2000 $ \mathrm{p p m}^{2 9,3 0} $ was used in our electrolyte for standard electrochemical characterizations of the catalysts. A continuous electrolyte flow system was employed to avoid the impacts of decreased nitrate concentration on measured catalytic performances (Supplementary Note 1). The two major control samples, CuNW and RuNP, presented distinct electrocatalytic behaviors compared to Ru-CuNW (Fig. 2a,b). RuNP showed an early onset of ammonia generation with $ \sim 80\% $ FE at 0.2 V, suggesting a facile $ \mathrm{N O}_{3} \mathrm{R R} $ kinetics on Ru sites. However, under higher overpotentials, particularly those more negative than HER thermodynamic potential (0 V), the ammonia FE starts to drop dramatically due to the strong HER competition (Supplementary Fig. 19c). This is unsurprising given that Ru is a widely recognized HER-active catalyst. In a sharp contrast, CuNW presented a sluggish ammonia onset. It required significant overpotentials to reach high ammonia FE, which also indicated the Cu's inertness for HER (Supplementary Fig. 19b).

Our Ru-CuNW catalyst organically integrates the favorable catalytic properties in Ru and Cu catalysts to deliver high-performance ammonia generation. Overall, Ru-CuNW not only showed a good onset potential, but also suppressed HER and undesired $ \mathrm{N O_{2}^{-}} $ byproducts at high current densities and negative potentials (Supplementary Figs. 19-21). The I-V plot (Fig. 2a) of Ru-CuNW showed an impressive 1 A $ \mathrm{c m}^{-2} $ current at only $ -0.13 $ V vs. RHE. Additionally, Ru-CuNW reached a maximum $ \mathrm{N H_{3}} $ FE of 96% at 0.04 V and maintained a high FE plateau across a wide potential range (Fig. 2b), which was dramatically improved from either CuNW or RuNP. These performance metrics represent a high ammonia partial current of 965 mA $ \mathrm{c m}^{-2} $ or 76500 $ \mu\mathrm{g} \ \mathrm{h}^{-1} $ $ \mathrm{c m}^{-2} $ ammonia generation rate (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 22), suggesting a significant improvement from previous reports (Supplementary Table 1) $ ^{31-40}。 $

We examined isotope-labeled $ \mathrm{N O}{3}^{-} $ coupled with $ ^{1} \mathrm{H} $ nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) analysis and confirmed all our $ \mathrm{N H}{3} $ products were generated by electrochemical $ \mathrm{N O}{3} \mathrm{R R} $ rather than any contaminations (Fig. 2d). To compare the intrinsic activities of the catalysts, we performed electrochemical double-layer capacitance $ \left( \mathrm{C}{\mathrm{d l}} \right) $ tests to normalize the current density over the ECSA (Supplementary Figs. 23, 24 and Supplementary Table 2). Different nitrate sources may have a wide range of nitrate concentrations; therefore, we examined the Ru-CuNW catalytic performance at different $ \mathrm{N O_{3}^{-}} $ concentrations to demonstrate its broad adaptability. Other than the 2000 ppm concentration (32.3 mM), 10 mM and 100 mM $ \mathrm{N O_{3}^{-}} $ were selected to cover the range of pollutant concentrations expected in household and textile/heavy-industrial wastewater, respectively $ ^{23,26,27} $ . While Ru-CuNW preserved its high ammonia FE and activity under higher nitrate concentration, the catalyst also presented impressive performance under 10 mM $ \mathrm{N O_{3}^{-}} $ , delivering a 400 mA $ \mathrm{c m}^{-2} $ current density with $ \sim 90\% $ ammonia FE (Fig. 2e, Supplementary Figs. 25 and 26). In general, delivering both high nitrate removal efficiency and high ammonia FE is challenging, since the competing HER could dominate when the remaining $ \mathrm{N O_{3}^{-}} $ is reduced. To evaluate Ru-CuNW's capability in nitrate removal, we performed a batch conversion test with initial 2000 ppm $ \mathrm{N O_{3}^{-}} $ and measured the remaining products (Fig. 2f). Impressively, nearly all N-sources were converted into $ \mathrm{N H_{3}} $ within 1 h. The $ \mathrm{N O_{3}^{-}} $ -to- $ \mathrm{N H_{3}} $ selectivity reached 99.8%, and the overall $ \mathrm{N H_{3}} $ FE remained over 90%. After 1 h of electrolysis, $ \mathrm{N O_{3}^{-}} $ and $ \mathrm{N O_{2}^{-}} $ are both significantly reduced below the World Health Organization (WHO) regulations for drinking water $ ^{25,27,29} $ . Product ratios over time (Supplementary Fig. 27) show we have successfully collected all the N-sources. These results demonstrate that Ru-CuNW not only achieves outstanding activity and $ \mathrm{N H_{3}} $ FE, but also serves as a promising catalyst for complete nitrate removal/conversion in wastewater.

Detailed structure understanding. A detailed understanding of the electronic and atomic structure of Ru-CuNW is required to unravel the reaction mechanism underlying the outstanding performance. There are two important questions need to be investigated. First, the oxidation state of Ru, whether the metallic or oxidized, must be determined since the electronic state underpins the electrocatalytic activity. Second, the Ru structure, either as isolated atoms or metal clusters, needs to be clarified.

obvious Cu and Ru peaks in Ru-CuNW. In the high-resolution Cu 2p spectrum (Supplementary Fig. 29), Ru-CuONW shows both $ \mathrm{C u}^{2+} $ and $ \mathrm{C u}^{0} / \mathrm{C u}^{+} $ peaks and the corresponding $ \mathrm{C u}^{2+} $ satellite peaks. However, Ru-CuNW only shows $ \mathrm{C u}^{0} / \mathrm{C u}^{+} $ peaks after the pre-reduction process, indicating its surface Cu is reduced. Raman spectra also suggests both CuO and $ \mathrm{C u}_{2} \mathrm{O} $ peaks in Ru-CuONW vanished after the pre-reduction process (Supplementary Fig. 30). High-resolution Ru 3d XPS spectrums (Fig. 3a) was conducted to analyze the Ru electronic structure changes. After Ru-CuONW reduced into Ru-CuNW, negative 1.0 eV energy shifts in Ru 3d peaks were observed, suggesting the electronic state of Ru is reduced from $ \mathrm{R u}^{4+} $ to $ \mathrm{R u}^{0} $ after the pre-reduction step $ ^{45,46} $ . Surface elemental ratio analysis (Supplementary Table 3) suggests the surface Ru:Cu is approximately 16:84, which is consistent with the EDS mapping result.

X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) measurements provide further insight into the electronic properties of Cu matrix and coordination structures of Ru dopants. The ex situ Cu K-edge X-ray absorption near-edge spectroscopy (XANES) and white line (Supplementary Fig. 31) show the Cu oxidation state in Ru-CuNW is identical to the reference Cu foil, suggesting CuO reduced to the metallic state after the pre-reduction. To further confirm this, we performed synchrotron-based in situ XAS (Supplementary Fig. 32), and clearly demonstrated that the Cu oxidation state of Ru-CuONW pre-catalyst was gradually turned from original Cu oxides into metallic Cu during the pre-reduction (Fig. 3b). In situ XAS analysis of Ru-CuNW during $ \mathrm{N O_{3}} $ RR also suggest the metallic Cu state was well maintained (Supplementary Fig. 33a). The in situ and ex situ Ru K-edge XANES profile and energy absorption threshold (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 34) indicate the Ru oxidation state was shifted from positive in Ru-CuONW to metallic state in Ru-CuNW. In situ XAS during $ \mathrm{N O_{3}} $ RR further confirmed the metallic Ru state is the active phase under the reaction conditions (Supplementary Fig. 33b).

Synchrotron-based pair distribution function (PDF) analysis was performed to further probe the local bonding environments, as it is a powerful tool for studying the atomic pair correlations $ ^{50,51} $ . The first peak of Ru-CuNW has a maximum at 2.56 $ \mathring{\mathrm{A}} $ (Fig. 3f), which exactly matches the Cu-Cu nearest neighbor distance based on the known crystal structure (PDF: 00-004-0836) and the bond length of Cu-Cu and Cu-Ru predicted by DFT model (Supplementary Table 4). The first peak of Ru foil (2.70 $ \mathring{\mathrm{A}} $ ) is close to the expected distance for Ru-Ru and matches well with the DFT model. The $ \mathrm{RuO_{2}} $ has a Ru-O peak at 1.98 $ \mathring{\mathrm{A}} $ but no such peaks were observed in Ru-CuNW (Supplementary Fig. 37), which further demonstrates that Ru in Ru-CuNW was fully reduced. Cu-Ru distances are expected to broaden Cu-Cu peak slightly, but no apparent shoulder is observed, suggesting the absence of Ru-Ru bonds in Ru-CuNW. The PDF results therefore provide supporting evidence for the absence of significant Ru clustering.

Theoretical analysis. To explain the high $ \mathrm{N H_{3}} $ FE over Ru-CuNW compared to CuNW and RuNP, we used DFT to calculate the minimum energy pathway of $ \mathrm{N O_{3}} $ RR and HER The $ \mathrm{N O_{3}} $ RR pathway on the electrode surface includes: adsorption of $ \mathrm{N O_{3}}^{-} $ , deoxygenation of N-species, hydrogenation on N-species and/or N-N bond formation, and desorption of reduced species. Figure 4a shows the overall pathway for $ \mathrm{N O_{3}} $ RR at 0 V vs. RHE at pH 14.

DFT calculations predict that $ \mathrm{N H_{3}} $ formation is the most favorable $ \mathrm{N O_{3}}^{-} $ reaction pathway at the Ru site on Ru-CuNW, because the activation barriers along the $ \mathrm{N H_{3}} $ path are much lower than the rate controlling barrier for the other pathways $ (\mathrm{N O_{2}}, \mathrm{N O}, \mathrm{N_{2} O} $ and $ \mathrm{N_{2}} $ , Supplementary Fig. 39 and 40). Ru-CuNW minimizes the HER as $ \mathrm{N O_{3}}^{-} $ adsorption is 0.92 eV more favorable than $ \mathrm{H^{+}} $ adsorption at the Ru site (Fig. 4b) and HER is unlikely to occur on the Cu sites due to endothermic adsorption $ (+0.47 \mathrm{eV}) $ of $ \mathrm{H_{2}O}^{} $ , the HER reactant at pH 14 (Supplementary Fig. 41). Although more negative potential increases the likelihood of $ \mathrm{N O_{2}}^{-} $ desorption by reducing the desorption energy, the barrier for $ \mathrm{N O_{2}}^{-} $ release remains higher than the $ \mathrm{N H_{3}} $ pathway until $ \sim-0.9 \mathrm{V} $ vs. RHE. The highly dispersed and isolated Ru active site additionally acts to restrict the $ \mathrm{N O_{3}} $ RR to the Ru atom due to high reaction energy (0.39 eV) required for $ \mathrm{N^{}} $ to migrate from Ru-Cu to Cu-Cu sites (Supplementary Fig. 40), thus minimizing $ \mathrm{N_{2}} $ formation with N atoms from other Ru sites. Overall, the combination of suppressed HER, endothermic $ \mathrm{N O_{2}}^{-} $ desorption and restricted movement of $ \mathrm{N^{*}} $ species on the surface lead to high $ \mathrm{N H_{3}} $ FE for Ru-CuNW over a wide range of potentials.

The poorer performance of CuNW compared to Ru-CuNW is attributed to the weaker adsorption of $ \mathrm{N O}{3}^{-} $ , poor ability to hydrogenate reduced N atoms, and the sensitivity of $ \mathrm{N H}{3} $ vs. $ \mathrm{N O}{2}^{-} $ path to the potential. In summary, these results explain why Ru-CuNW has a superior performance for $ \mathrm{N H_{3}} $ production via $ \mathrm{N O_{3}} $ RR compared to CuNW and RuNP, and highlight the importance of highly dispersed Ru in Cu matrix.

Ammonia products collection. With the impressive $ \mathrm{N O_{3}} $ RR performance of our RuCuNW catalyst, we further demonstrated its practical applications by coupling the electrocatalysis with an air striping method to continuously collect high-purity ammonia products (Fig. 5a). The catalyst's good stability under industrial relevant current density is a prerequisite for such applications. We first conducted a long-term chronopotentiometry (CP) stability test of Ru-CuNW in a H-cell with continuous electrolyte flow. The voltage required to achieve 400 mA $ \mathrm{c m}^{-2} $ over our catalyst remained stable over 100 hours, with over 90% $ \mathrm{N H_{3}} $ FE maintained (Fig. 5b). The effluent with generated $ \mathrm{N H_{3}} $ in it was air stripped (see Methods) for an efficient ammonia product collection due to the high ammonia vapor pressure in alkaline environment $ ^{52-54} $ . As a result, we successfully stripped out over 99.7% of the generated ammonia from the electrolyte, with only 1.7 ppm remaining, suggesting a full denitrification of the water source and simultaneous generation of valuable ammonia (Fig. 5c). The stripped-out ammonia vapor was either trapped in a HCl solution or cold condensed into high-purity ammonia water. Most of the flowing out ammonia vapor ( $ \sim 97\% $ ) can be trapped in the acid solution, and finally formed high-purity $ \mathrm{N H_{4} C l_{(s)}} $ powder, an important fertilizer, after performing rotary evaporation. Secondarily, the liberated $ \mathrm{N H_{3}} $ was subject to a cold condensation process to produce pure $ \mathrm{N H_{3}(a q)} $ with a $ \mathrm{N H_{3}} $ concentration up to 0.3 wt.% . The overall collection efficiency of both products is over 85% (Fig. 5c). The generation of $ \mathrm{N H_{4} C l_{(s)}} $ was confirmed by XRD (Fig. 5d) and $ \mathrm{N H_{3}(a q)} $

Conclusions

In summary, we synthesized a high-performance $ \mathrm{N O_{3}} $ RR catalyst of highly dispersed Ru atoms on Cu nanowires, which delivers industrial-relevant ammonia generation current while maintaining high FE and stability. DFT revealed that the uniformly distributed Ru site shows an activated $ \mathrm{N O_{3}}^{-} $ -to- $ \mathrm{N H_{3}} $ pathway and the neighboring Cu sites help suppress the undesired competing HER. The effluent from our electrolysis of $ \mathrm{N O_{3}} $ RR was combined with an air stripping process to produce $ \mathrm{N H_{4} C l} $ fertilizer and pure $ \mathrm{N H_{3}} $ aqueous products. Future studies can be focused on how to translate this excellent catalytic performance into membrane electrode assembly devices where no liquid electrolyte is needed for more practical implementations.

Methods

Catalyst synthesis. In a typical procedure, $ 1\times1 \mathrm{c m}^{2} $ Cu foam was first washed with isopropyl alcohol and then 0.1 M HCl to clean the surface. Next, the washed Cu foam was soaked in $ 0.1 \mathrm{M} \left( \mathrm{N H}{4} \right){2} \mathrm{S}{2} \mathrm{O}{8} $ with 1 M NaOH under ambient conditions for 1 h to oxidize Cu into $ \mathrm{C u} \left( \mathrm{O H} \right){2} \mathrm{N W}. $ The synthesized $ \mathrm{C u} \left( \mathrm{O H} \right){2} \mathrm{N W} $ was then washed with deionized water and soaked in 10 mM $ \mathrm{R u C l}{3} $ solution under ambient conditions for 12 h to go through a cation exchange process. After ion exchange, the product was dried at $ 70^{\circ} \mathrm{C} $ in an oven for 1 h and then annealed in a flowing Ar atmosphere at $ 2 0 0^{\circ} \mathrm{C} $ for 2 h to convert it into RuCuONW. Finally, an in-situ electrochemical pre-reduction step was performed after annealing, which operated by the CP method at $ 1 0 0 \mathrm{m A} \mathrm{c m}^{-2} $ for 12 h and was followed by $ 7 0 0 \mathrm{m A} \mathrm{c m}^{-2} $ for 1 h to obtain the final Ru-CuNW catalyst. Different noble-metal doped catalysts were synthesized via the same procedure, except 10 mM of $ \mathrm{R h C l}{3} \cdot \mathrm{x H}{2} \mathrm{O}, $ $ \mathrm{O s C l}{3} \cdot \mathrm{x H}{2} \mathrm{O}, $ and $ \mathrm{I r C l}{3} \cdot \mathrm{x H}{2} \mathrm{O} $ solutions were used instead of $ \mathrm{R u C l}{3}. $ Ru-CuNW with different Ru doping levels were synthesized following the same procedure, except 2 mM, 30 mM, and 50 mM of $ \mathrm{R u C l}{3} $ solutions were used. CuNW was synthesized by performing the same annealing and electrochemical pre-reduction procedure on $ \mathrm{C u} \left( \mathrm{O H} \right){2} \mathrm{N W} $ without ion exchange process. RuNP was synthesized by mixing 0.2052 g of $ \mathrm{R u C l}{3} $ and 0.4 g of carbon black (Cabot, BP2000) into 150 mL of 1 M HCl and sonicated for 2 h. Next, the mixture solution was dried by rotary evaporator, and the collected powder was annealed in a flowing $ \mathrm{A r} / \mathrm{H}{2} $ atmosphere at $ 9 0 0^{\circ} \mathrm{C} $ for 2 h to synthesize the final RuNP product.

Material characterization. SEM was performed on an FEI Quanta 400 field emission scanning electron microscope. TEM, HRTEM, HADDF-STEM, HR-HADDF-STEM, and EDS elemental mapping were performed using an FEI Titan Themis aberration-corrected transmission electron microscope at 300 kV and Talos F200X transmission electron microscope at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. XPS data was conducted on an on a PHI Quantera spectrometer, using a monochromatic Al K $ \alpha $ radiation (1486.6 eV) and a low-energy flood gun as the neutralizer. All XPS spectra were calibrated by shifting the detected carbon C 1s peak to 284.8 eV. Raman spectrum was obtained on a Raman Microscope using 633 nm laser excitation wavelength. XAS measurements were performed at the Sector 20-BM beamline of the Advanced Photon Source at Argonne National Laboratory. The samples were measured in fluorescence mode, and the data processing for FT-EXAFS were performed using the Athena program. HEXRD measurements was carried out at 17-BM at the Advanced Photon Source at Argonne National Laboratory, and the obtained 2D diffraction patterns were converted to 1D patterns using GSAS II software. Regular XRD data was collected on a Rigaku SmartLab X-ray diffractometer with Copper K- $ \alpha $ radiation. Total scattering data for PDF was collected on the Brockhouse High Energy Wiggler beamline of the Canadian Light Source using 65 keV X-ray radiation. The Ru-CuNW foam was mounted directly on the beamline. A thin sheet of Ru metal was also measured as a reference and a $ \mathrm{RuO_{2}} $ powder reference sample was measured in a 0.63 mm inner diameter Kapton capillary. Appropriate background measurements were made for each sample. The pair distribution functions, $ G(r) $ , were produced using a $ Q_{\mathrm{max}} $ of 27 $ \mathrm{\AA}^{-1} $ .

Atom probe tomography. APT analytical imaging is based on the combination of the field evaporation and the principles of the time-of-flight mass spectrometry (ToF-MS). Field

evaporation defines the phenomenon during which individual atoms can be evaporated from the surface they come from as ions if a strong electric field (several tens of $ \mathrm{V} \mathrm{n m}^{-1} $ ) is applied on the surface. To reach such intense electric field, the samples were first fabricated as a sharp needle. APT sample preparations were performed using the standard focused ion beam (FIB) lift out process $ ^{55} $ with a FEI Quanta dual beam $ ^{56} $ (Supplementary Fig. 38). Considering the important presence of voids in the Ru-CuNW, Pt welding was used to fill and consolidate the final needle structure. Field evaporation is controllably provoked with the application of either short voltage pulses ( $ \sim10 $ to 100 ns duration) or assisted with laser beam pulses combined with a steady electric field. Atoms are extracted one by one and projected towards a position sensitive detector, that will situate the detected ion in 3D space for the reconstruction. The spatial positions and the chemical identification by ToF-MS are used to reconstruct 3D chemical map the evaporated samples. A full description of the technique can be found in the previous work $ ^{57} $ . In this work, we used a LEAP 4000X-HR Local Electrode Atom Probe system by CAMECA Instruments equipped with a 355 nm UV laser. Analysis was performed with 125 kHz laser pulse frequency, 100 pJ power at 45.5 K. 3D reconstructions and analysis have been conducted using CAMECA's Integrated Visualization & Analysis Software (IVAS) version 3.8.8. Reconstruction parameters were determined with the help of tip profile recorded by SEM after its fabrication by FIB-SEM (Supplementary Fig. 38c).

Electrochemical measurement. All electrochemical measurements were carried out in a customized H-type cell with bipolar membrane (Fuel Cell Store) under ambient condition in this study. Electrochemical responses were recorded by a BioLogic VMP3 workstation. In general, 2000 ppm $ \mathrm{N O_{3}^{-}} $ with 1 M KOH was used as the electrolyte in both cathode and anode unless otherwise specified. Initial electrolyte volume in each H-cell compartments were 25 mL, and the electrolyte was continuously flowed in and out as a flow-system H-cell measurement (Supplementary Note 1) unless otherwise specified. A stir bar was used in the H-cell at a stirring rate of 1600 r.p.m to minimize the mass transfer limitation in the porous electrodes. In a typical three-electrode system, a Ni foam and a saturated calomel electrode (SCE, CH Instruments) were used as the counter and reference electrode, respectively. In this work, all potentials measured against SCE were converted to the reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) scale by: E (vs. RHE) = E (vs. SCE) + 0.241 V + $ 0.0591\times $ pH. In the 1 M KOH electrolyte used in this work, the pH value is 14, as determined by an Orion Star A111 pH Meter (Thermo Scientific). The synthesized RuCuNW, Ru-CuONW, and CuNW were directly used as working electrode. For RuNP working electrode, 5 mg of catalyst was added into 1 mL isopropyl alcohol and 20 $ \mu\mathrm{L} $ Nafion 117 containing solution (5%, Sigma-Aldrich) and sonicated for 1 h to obtain a well-dispersed catalyst ink, and then 80 $ \mu\mathrm{L} $ of the catalyst ink was drop-casted on a $ 1\times1 \mathrm{c m}^{2} $ Toray Carbon Paper (Fuel Cell Store), resulting in a loading of 0.4 mg $ \mathrm{c m}^{-2} $ . Solution resistance (Rs) was measured by the potentiostatic electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (PEIS) at frequencies from 0.1 Hz to 200 kHz, and all the measured potentials were 50% iR-compensated unless otherwise specified. Chronoamperometry (CA) tests were performed at a stirring rate of 1600 r.p.m. at constant potentials for 10 min or until a stable current to confirm a steady-state is reached. The current densities were recorded and the electrolytes were collected to analyze the catalytic performances. LSV tests were recorded at a scan rate of 5 mV $ \mathrm{s}^{-1} $ . Complete nitrate removal tests were conducted in a

FE, partial current density, yield rate, and selectivity calculation. FE of $ \mathrm{N O_{3}} $ RR toward $ \mathrm{N H_{3}} $ and $ \mathrm{N O_{2}^{-}} $ products were calculated by the following equation:

$$ \mathrm {F E} = \left(n \times \nu \times C \times F\right) / i \# (2) $$

where n is the electron transfer number required to form the products, which is 8 for $ \mathrm{N H_{3}} $ and 2 for $ \mathrm{N O_{2}^{-}} $ ; $ \nu $ is the flow rate of catholyte (L s $ ^{-1} $ ); C represents the concentration of the outlet products (M); F is the Faraday constant (96,485 C mol $ ^{-1} $ ); i is the total current (A). If the experiments were conducted under batch system, the FE was calculated by:

$$ \mathrm {F E} = (n \times V \times C \times F) / (i \times t) \# (3) $$

where V is the catholyte volume (L); t is the total reaction time (s).

To calculate the partial current density of $ \mathrm{N H_{3}} $ , the following equation was used:

$$ i _ {N H _ {3}} = i \times \mathrm {F E} _ {\mathrm {N H} _ {3}} \# (4) $$

In addition, the $ \mathrm{N H_{3}} $ yield rate was calculated by:

$$ r _ {N H _ {3}} = \nu \times C _ {N H _ {3}} \# (5) $$

In the complete nitrate removal experiment in the batch system, the $ \mathrm{N O}{3}^{-} $ -to- $ \mathrm{N H}{3} $ selectivity was calculated by:

$$ \mathrm {N O} _ {3} ^ {-} - \mathrm {t o} - \mathrm {N H} _ {3} \mathrm {s e l e c t i v i t y} = \mathrm {C} _ {\mathrm {N H} _ {3}} / \Delta \mathrm {C} _ {\mathrm {N O} _ {3} ^ {-}} \# (6) $$

ECSA analysis. The ECSA was determined by:

$$ \mathrm {E C S A} = \mathrm {C} _ {\mathrm {d l}} / \mathrm {C} _ {\mathrm {s}} \# (7) $$

where $ C_{\mathrm{d l}} $ is the double layer capacitance, and $ C_{\mathrm{s}} $ is the specific capacitance of the sample. A general specific capacitance of $ C_{\mathrm{s}}=0.040 \mathrm{mF} \mathrm{cm}^{-2} $ was used in this study based on previous reported values $ ^{34,40}. $ $ C_{\mathrm{d l}} $ was determined by the equation:

$$ C _ {\mathrm {d l}} = \mathrm {i} _ {\mathrm {c}} / v \# (8) $$

where $ i_{\mathrm{c}} $ is the charging current and $ v $ is the scan rate. A series of cyclic voltammogram (CV) tests within the non-faradaic potential region 0.40-0.50 V (vs. RHE) under different scan rates were performed. Next, by plotting the measured $ i_{\mathrm{c}} $ vs. $ v $ and performing linear fitting, $ C_{\mathrm{dl}} $ can be obtained from the slopes.
