Abstract

Antibiotics as emerging contaminants are of global concern due to the development of antibiotic resistant genes potentially causing sewage. Current wastewater treatment technology cannot sufficiently remove antibiotics from sewage, hence new and low-cost technology is needed. Adsorptive materials have extensively used for the conditioning, remediation and removal of inorganic and organic materials, although their application for removing antibiotics has been reported 30 out of 250 antibiotics so far. The literature on the adsorptive removal of antibiotics using different adsorptive materials is summarized and critically reviewed, by comparing different adsorbents with varying physicochemical characteristics. The efficiency removing antibiotics from water and wastewater by different adsorbents has been evaluated examining their adsorption coefficient $ K_{d} $ values. For sulfamethoxazole the different adsorbents followed the trend: biochar (BC) > multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNT) graphite > clay minerals, and for tetracycline the adsorptive materials followed the trend: SWCNT > graphite > MWCNT = AC > bentonite = humic substance = clay minerals Underlying controlling parameters for the adsorption technology have been examined. diitian, the cost of preparing adsorbents has been estimated, which followed the order of BCACs < ion exchange resins < MWCNTs. The future research challenge process integration, production and modification of low-cost adsorbents are elaborated.

Keywords: adsorption, antibiotic, biochar, activated carbon nanotube, regeneration

2. Adsorptive processes for antibiotic removal

The term adsorption is the accumulation of matter gas or liquid phase to the surface of an adsorbent, which could involve physical and chemical adsorption. Although adsorption is a well-known process, in the past decades of this technology for antibiotic removal has not been extensively explored. The widely reported adsorbents for antibiotic removal include ACs, CNTs, bentonite, exchange resins and BCs. Overviews of published journal articles for antibiotic removal adsorptive materials are presented in Tables 2-6. In most of the adsorption studies, concentrations of antibiotics used are at mg/L level, which are significantly higher than environmentally relevant concentrations (ng/L - $ \mu\mathrm{g}/\mathrm{L} $ ). To ensure a consistent comparison between adsorbents, the discussion often referred to the linear range of the adsorption histories by calculating the adsorption coefficient $ (K_{d}) $ . The adsorption efficiency is directly related the adsorbent's properties such as specific surface area (SSA), porosity (macrine porosity), pore diameter and functional groups (Estevinho et al., 2007).

3.1. Adsorption kinetics

Adsorption kinetics typically involve four steps: bulk transport, film transport, intra-particle transport and adsorption the adsorbent. 7. Conclusions

The removal of antibiotic residues from water and water by adsorption technology has been critically reviewed based on extensive scientific reports. It can be summarized that adsorption is an effective method for antibiotic removal from contaminated waters with an efficacy of 90-100% at mg/L concentration range. Most widely used adsorbents with effective removal are ACs, CNTs and BC. For the reason of sulfamethazole, it was found that adsorptive materials followed the order of BC6> BC500 > MWCNT > graphite = clay minerals, while for the removal of tetracycline, adsorptive materials followed the trend: SWCNT > graphite > MWCNT = AC > bentonite = humidity = clay minerals. The high material cost and potential high regeneration costs are the major drawbacks for both ACs and CNTs. In comparison, BCs can be prepared significantly lower cost with equal or better adsorptive capacity for antibiotic removal should be explored further. It was

The mechanism of antibiotic adsorption primarily involved $ \pi-\pi $ EDA interactions, electrostatic interactions, hydrophobic interaction, hydrogen bonds, and pore filling.
