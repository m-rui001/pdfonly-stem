第 3 页

Engineering copper-based catalysts that favor high-value alcohols is desired in view of the energy density, ready transport, and established use of these liquid fuels. In the design of catalysts, much progress has been made to target deliberately the C-C coupling step; while comparatively little effort has been expended to target post-C-C coupling reaction intermediates. Here we report a class of core/shell vacancy engineering catalysts that utilize sulfur atoms in the nanoparticle core and copper vacancies in the shell to achieve efficient electrochemical $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ reduction to propanol and ethanol. These catalysts shift selectivity away from the competing ethylene reaction and toward liquid alcohols. We increase the ratio of alcohol-to-ethylene by over 6x compared to bare-copper nanoparticles, highlighting an alternative approach to electroproduce alcohols instead of alkenes. We achieve a C2+ alcohol production rate of $ 1 2 6 \pm 5 \mathrm{m A c m}^{-2} $ with a selectivity of $ 3 2 \pm 1 \% $ Faradaic efficiency.

第 4 页

The renewable-energy-powered electrocatalytic reduction of carbon dioxide $ \mathrm{(CO_{2})} $ to value-added carbon-based products offers to provide crucial energy storage to address growing energy demand $ ^{1-5} $ . Recent progress in electrocatalytically converting $ \mathrm{CO_{2}} $ has resulted in the production of gaseous carbon products such as carbon monoxide $ (\mathrm{CO})^{6} $ methane $ (\mathrm{CH}{4})^{7} $ , and ethylene $ (\mathrm{C{2}H_{4}})^{8} $ , and C1 liquids such as formate $ (\mathrm{HCOOH})^{9-15} $ .

The production of liquid multi-carbon alcohols is also highly desired: it stands to enable the synthesis of sustainable fuels that leverage high energy densities $ ^{16} $ (21 MJ/L ethanol, 27 MJ/L propanol) for long-range and heavy freight transportation applications. Unfortunately, the production of multi-carbon alcohols via direct $ \mathrm{CO_{2}} $ electroreduction remains below that required for economic viability due to the limited selectivity and low activity of present-day catalysts $ ^{17,18} $

Among electrocatalysts for $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ reduction, Cu-based materials are the most prone to reduce $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ to C2 and above products, with alkenes traditionally dominating multi carbon product formation $ ^{1 9-2 1} $ . To develop more efficient electrocatalysts for alcohol production, it is crucial to modify the catalyst structure to promote the desired alcohol and suppress alkene electrosynthesis. Additionally, it is vital to understand the mechanisms that underlie selectivity to enable further catalyst refinement.

We reasoned that - since ethylene and ethanol share a penultimate reaction intermediate $ \left( \mathrm{^*} C_{2} H_{3} O \right)^{2 2} $ - we could potentially modify a catalyst's surface structure to target the hydrogenation of this intermediate, and thereby promote C2 liquid production. Suppressing oft-produced ethylene would enhance production and selectivity towards alcohols.

第 5 页

Several avenues have previously been employed to engineer Cu surfaces to tune the energetics of intermediate binding $ ^{20,23-26} $ . Introducing metal atomic vacancy defects influences electrocatalytic performance by adjusting the electronic structure of neighboring atoms and consequently the energy barriers of the rate-limiting reaction intermediates $ ^{27} $ . Copper sulfide structures are of interest because they can provide a means to form stable surface defects and control the density of surface vacancies $ ^{28,29} $ . Additionally, copper sulfide derived catalysts merit investigation in view of the longrange modification of the local density of surface states by introducing sulfur into the Cu structure and affect thereby CO binding $ ^{30} $ .

In this study, we obtained preliminary results that reveal that varying a Cu catalyst by introducing S could lead to a notable difference in the ratio of ethanol to ethylene produced (Supplementary Table 1). These data motivated us to examine in greater depth, using computational analysis, a key rate-limiting step along the $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ -to-C2 pathway. In the computational studies, we found that a modified $ \mathrm{C u_{2} S} $ core with Cu surface vacancies could account for a modulation in branching in favour of ethanol relative to ethylene. Specifically, DFT studies examined the energetics seen by the adsorbed $ \mathrm{C H_{2} C H O} $ intermediate that might influence alcohol vs. ethylene electrosynthesis. These studies point to strategies to switch $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ reduction reaction pathways from ethylene to alcohol. We then embarked upon a systematic study of S-enriched Cu and surface vacancies, synthesizing a $ \mathrm{C u_{2} S / C u - V} $ (V: vacancy) nanoparticle structure that enables the controllable introduction of vacancies on a copper surface shell with a copper sulfide core. Our core/shell-vacancy engineering (CSVE) catalyst enabled us to modify

the C2 reaction pathway, shifting selectivity away from ethylene and towards multi carbon alcohols.

Results

Theoretical simulations predict selectivity control. To investigate the influence of a modified Cu core/shell structure and surface Cu vacancies on the final ethylene and ethanol reaction steps, we used density functional theory (DFT) to characterize changes in energy barriers relative to the case of pristine Cu. $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ reduction to multi-carbon products proceeds through the dimerization or protonation of adsorbed CO intermediates $ ^{31-33} $ . It has been shown that CO electroreduction to ethanol shares a similar pathway with ethylene until the last three proton-coupled electron transfer steps. At this late stage, the adsorbed $ \mathrm{C H_{2} C H O} $ intermediate $ \mathrm{(^{*}C_{2} H_{3} O)} $ may proceed to ethanol through further carbon protonation; or to ethylene by leaving an oxygen atom adsorbed on the surface $ ^{22,34} $ .

We focused therefore on the effect of surface modifications on the thermodynamics of the adsorbed $ \mathrm{C H_{2} C H O} $ intermediate, and in turn on the selectivity for ethylene vs. ethanol production. We began by creating three model systems: a pristine copper slab, a copper slab with a single atomic vacancy, and a $ \mathrm{C u_{2} S / C u} $ core/shell model with an atomic vacancy (Figure 1a, Supplementary Fig. 1). We then explored the following proposed reaction mechanisms representative of ethylene and ethanol production: Ethylene Pathway:

$$ ^ {*} \mathrm {O} + \left(\mathrm {H} ^ {+} + \mathrm {e} ^ {-}\right)\rightarrow^ {*} \mathrm {O H} $$

We then limited our thermodynamic analyses to mechanistic steps starting with the bound $ ^{*} \mathrm{C H_{2} C H O} $ intermediate. We found that on pristine copper (Figure 1b, Supplementary Fig. 2-3, and Supplementary Table 5), both ethylene and ethanol have a low thermodynamic energy barrier ( $ \sim0 $ eV) with a kinetic energy barrier of 0.560 and 0.645 eV (at applied -0.5 V potential, the required potential to overcome the rate determining C-C coupling step). The introduction of a surface copper vacancy (Figure 1c, Supplementary Fig. 4, and Supplementary Table 5) slightly increases the energy barrier for ethylene production (0.025 eV), though it remains thermodynamically favored; while the vacancy has a negligible effect on the ethanol pathway. Thus, the activation energy barriers at applied potential of -0.5 V for both ethylene (0.375 eV) and ethanol (0.531 eV) could be surmounted at room temperature.

Interestingly, a vacancy present on a copper shell with a $ \mathrm{C u_{2} S} $ core increases the energy barrier in the ethylene pathway (1.148 eV), while leaving the ethanol pathway mostly unaffected (0.427 eV) (Figure 1d). Thus, if one applies an overpotential of 0.5 V (i.e. the required overpotential to overcome the early C-C coupling energy barrier), the ethanol pathway remains exergonic, but the ethylene pathway becomes unfavorable (Figure 1e-g Supplementary Fig. 5, and Supplementary Table 5) suggesting selective

These results suggest that subsurface sulfur atoms and copper vacancy defects together shift the balance in favor of ethanol by suppressing ethylene production through this common pathway (details in Supplementary Fig. 1-6 and Supplementary Table 2-5).

Catalyst synthesis and characterization. In light of these DFT findings, we sought to synthesize a surface-vacancy-enriched copper sulfide/copper core/shell catalyst. We first prepared colloidal vacancy enriched $ \mathrm{C u_{2} S} $ (V-Cu $ {2} S $ ) nanoparticles. Cuprous sulfide nanoparticles synthesized using the solvothermal method enable controlled copper vacancies in the surface structure of the catalyst $ ^{35} $ . Next, we reduced the $ \mathrm{V-Cu{2} S} $ in a $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ saturated electrolyte, yielding a reduced metallic copper shell with enriched vacancies surrounding a $ \mathrm{C u_{2} S} $ core (Figure 2a).

To investigate the core/shell structure and to query the extent of surface vacancies, we analyzed the nanoparticles before and after reduction. The morphology of the copper sulfide nanoparticles was characterized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Supplementary Fig. 7a), high-resolution TEM (HRTEM, Supplementary Fig. 7b) and high-angle annular dark-field scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM, Figure 2b and Supplementary Fig. 7c). The nanoparticles have a high degree of uniformity and an average size of 8.5 nm. Energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping data (Figure 2c) reveal that copper and sulfur are distributed evenly throughout the particle. Powder x-ray diffraction (PXRD) analysis (Supplementary Fig. 7d) shows good agreement with the standard $ \mathrm{C u}_{1.94} \mathrm{S} $ djurleite phase $ ^{35,36} $ , thus indicating a non-

stoichiometric ratio between copper and sulfur.

The active nanocatalyst used for $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ reduction was then produced from the electrochemical reduction of the $ \mathrm{V-C u_{2} S} $ nanoparticles which removed sulfur from the surface, thereby constructing $ \mathrm{C u_{2} S} / \mathrm{C u-V} $ , i.e. a $ \mathrm{C u_{2} S} $ core with an ultrathin metallic copper shell containing copper vacancies. In these nanoparticles, elemental Cu enriches the nanostructure, while the sulfur signal is decreased but still present, indicating that a fraction of sulfur has been removed during $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ reduction (Figure 2d-e). To examine the sulfur distribution in the derived structure in detail, we acquired an EDS line scan: it revealed that sulfur mainly concentrates in the core of the nanoparticle, confirming the picture of a $ \mathrm{C u_{2} S} / \mathrm{C u} $ core/shell nanostructure (Figure 2f-g).

To explore the variation in nanoparticles structure during $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ reduction, and gain insight into the electronic configuration, we used X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) to probe the chemical composition of the copper sulfide nanoparticles before and after the $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ reduction reaction. Results of XPS (Supplementary Fig. 8) and XPS sputter depth profiling (Figure 3a, orange curve), taken together, show the S 2p spectra of the catalyst after reaction. These indicate that sulfur is present at the core of the nanoparticle. Positron annihilation spectroscopy (PAS), which probes the type and relative concentration of defect vacancies $ ^{41} $ , was carried out to investigate the copper surface. The positron lifetime spectra (Figure 3e) and the lifetime parameters (Supplementary Table 6) show that both the CSVE catalyst, and also the $ \mathrm{C u_{2} S} $ bulk control, exhibit three lifetimes. We ascribe the component $ \tau_{2} $ (circa 380 ps) to positron annihilation in trapped Cu vacancies. The relative intensities (I) of these lifetimes reflect that copper vacancies and bulk character are predominant in the CSVE nanoparticles and bulk $ \mathrm{C u_{2} S} $ , respectively. Simulated results in Figure 3f and g reveal the projection of the positron density distribution for the pure $ \mathrm{C u_{2} S} $ bulk and the CSVE nanoparticle sample, indicating the high vacancy-associated concentration in the latter. Performance in electrochemical $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ reduction. To probe electrocatalytic properties, we first characterized the electrochemical $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ reduction activity and selectivity using

第 11 页

a three-electrode H-cell system. The catalysts were loaded onto a glassy carbon electrode. Linear sweep voltammetry curves were obtained in a $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ -saturated 0.1 M $ \mathrm{K H C O_{3}} $ aqueous solution (Figure 4a). The CSVE catalyst exhibits a total geometric current density of $ \sim 3 2 \mathrm{m A c m}^{-2} $ at -0.95 V versus a reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) during 16 h of continuous electrocatalysis (Figure 4b).

Electrocatalysis was performed using cathodic potentials in the range of -0.85 V to 1.15 V versus RHE (Figure 4c). As the applied potential becomes more negative (-0.9 V versus RHE), multi-carbon products were observed, indicating that C-C coupling occurs beyond this potential (Supplementary Fig. 11a).

At potentials of -0.95 V vs RHE, 500 mV above the potential at which C-C coupling is first observed, the CSVE nanocatalyst shows peak alcohol production. Interestingly, propanol $ \mathrm{(C_{3}H_{7}OH)} $ and ethanol $ \mathrm{(C_{2}H_{5}OH)} $ follow a similar selectivity trend with changes to the applied potential, indicating that ethanol and propanol share common intermediates along their reaction pathways. This is consistent with the assumption made in our DFT study, wherein we used ethanol as a proxy for overall alcohol electrosynthesis. The maximum FE for $ \mathrm{C_{3}H_{7}OH} $ and $ \mathrm{C_{2}H_{5}OH} $ reaches 8 $ \pm $ 0.7 % and 15 $ \pm $ 1 % with a partial current density of 2.5 $ \pm $ 0.1 and 4.8 $ \pm $ 0.1 mA $ \mathrm{cm}^{-2} $ at -0.95 V versus RHE, respectively (Figure 4d and Supplementary Fig. 11-12).

These represent 6x, 19x, 46x, and 44x improvements in partial current density for C2+ alcohols compared with those of $ \mathrm{C u_{2} S} $ NPs without vacancy, pure Cu NPs, bulk $ \mathrm{C u_{2} S} $ and bulk Cu under the same operating conditions (Supplementary Fig. 13-15).

Moreover, the alcohol-to-ethylene ratio is enhanced from 0.18 on the bare-Cu NPs

to 1.2, a 6x increase with the CVSE catalyst, while overall C2+ selectivity remains similar (Figure 4d and Supplementary Table 7). It indicates that faradaic current was shifted from producing ethylene to producing alcohols. The performance improvement elucidates the synergetic effect of the $ \mathrm{C u_{2} S} $ core and the surface copper vacancies in promoting alcohol production. We should note that it is experimentally challenging to completely decouple the role of copper vacancies and the role of the core sulfur.

To characterize the intrinsic catalytic activity, we measured the electrochemically active surface area (ECSA) of each catalyst. While the $ \mathrm{C u_{2} S / C u-V} $ nanoparticles had a slightly larger ECSA than the $ \mathrm{C u_{2} S} $ and Cu control nanoparticles, the ECSA difference between the CSVE and control catalysts is less than 4% (Supplementary Fig. 16-17 and Supplementary Table 8-9). The shift in the C2 production pathway and the high current density towards alcohols is therefore ascribed to the catalyst itself rather than to any substantive increase in electrochemically active surface area.

We then sought a way to engineer the reaction environment to work in tandem with the CSVE catalyst to suppress ethylene and boost multi-carbon alcohol production. At a high pH, the concentration of $ \mathrm{O H}^{-} $ will be increased proximate to the catalyst surface affecting the bound $ \mathrm{O}^{*} $ intermediate along the ethylene pathway (equation 2), but is expected to leave the ethanol pathway largely unaffected. We turned to a flow-cell configuration that allowed us to operate in alkaline KOH electrolyte without compromising $ \mathrm{C O}{2} $ availability. We chose highly alkaline KOH as the electrolyte to increase the pH and electrolyte conductivity, and further enhance $ \mathrm{C O}{2} $ reduction reaction kinetics by suppressing hydrogen evolution $ ^{44-46} $ . The configuration bypasses

第 13 页

the low $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ solubility in KOH via the diffusion of $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ across a gas-liquid interface adjacent to the catalyst surface (Figure 5a) $ ^{47} $ . We first deposited the catalyst by spray coating a nanoparticle ink onto a carbon gas-diffusion electrode (details in Supplementary Methods). Linear sweep voltammetry curves (Figure 5b) of the three catalysts show the lower overpotentials of the CSVE-Cu catalyst at fixed current densities highlighting the improved activity.

We then evaluated the $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ reduction reaction activity in the current density range 200-600 mA $ \mathrm{c m}^{-2} $ in 1M KOH (Figure 5c, Supplementary Fig. 18 and Supplementary Table 10), a range enabled by the use of a flow cell and the alkaline electrolyte. For the CSVE-Cu catalyst, we achieved a Faradaic efficiency of 32% for multi-carbon alcohols $ \left( \mathrm{C_{2} H_{5} O H} \right. $ 25 $ \pm $ 1% and $ \mathrm{C_{3} H_{7} O H} $ 7 $ \pm $ 0.5% ). The partial current density exceeded 120 mA $ \mathrm{c m}^{-2} $ for multi-carbon alcohols.

The FE and partial current density exceeded, by a factor of 2, those of control catalysts, including both $ \mathrm{C u_{2} S} $ without vacancy, and also pure Cu (Supplementary Fig. 19). The C2+ alcohol partial current density versus maximum C2+ alcohol Faradaic efficiency for a range of catalysts reported in the literature is provided in Figure 5d, Supplementary Fig. 20, and Supplementary Table 11.

Conclusions

By incorporating sulfur atoms in the catalyst core, and copper vacancies in its shell, we realized $ \mathrm{C u_{2} S / C u - V} $ core/shell nanoparticles that enhance $ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ reduction to multi carbon alcohols. DFT calculations suggested the $ \mathrm{C u_{2} S / C u - V} $ structure steers products beyond alkenes and towards alcohols. Structural characterization, X-ray studies, and

第 14 页

electrochemical measurements attest to the role of CSVE in improving catalytic performance. These findings indicate an alternative catalytic approach that targets the suppression of unwanted C2, rather than just C1, products. This works offers avenues to the design of efficient catalysts that selectively produce higher-carbon liquid alcohols.

Methods

Computational details. The projected augmented wave (PAW) approach $ ^{48,49} $ and the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) of Perdew, Burke and Ernzerhof (PBE) $ ^{50} $ with DFT-D3 method for the van der Waals correction $ ^{51} $ employed in the Vienna ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) $ ^{52} $ were used to perform all the plane wave density functional theory (DFT) computations.

V-Cu $ {2} $ S nanoparticle synthesis. Synthesis of the copper (I) sulfide nanocrystals with controlled copper vacancies was performed according to a previously reported method $ ^{35} $ with slight modification. In a typical procedure, 130 mg $ \mathrm{Cu(acac)}{2} $ was dissolved by 30 mL dodecanethiol (DDT) in a three-neck flask with magnetic stirring under the protection of nitrogen gas and heated at $ 240\mathrm{^\circ C} $ for 20 min. The resulting $ \mathrm{V-Cu}_{2} $ S nanoparticles (V: vacancy) were collected by centrifugation, washed with acetone and hexane three times, and dried within a vacuum chamber.

$ \mathrm{C u_{2} S} $ nanoparticles synthesis. The copper (I) sulfide nanoparticles without copper vacancies were prepared using a previously reported method $ ^{53} $ . 1.25 mmol of ammonium diethyldithiocarbamate was mixed with 10 mL of dodecanethiol and 17 mL of oleic acid in a three-neck flask. The solution was heated up to $ 1 1 0 \mathrm{~} ^ {\circ} \mathrm{C} $ under Argon

第 15 页

(Ar) flow followed by a quick injection of a suspension composed of 1 mmol of copper(II) acetylacetonate and 3 mL of oleic acid. The solution was then quickly heated up to $ 1 8 0 \, \mathrm{^ {\circ} C} $ and kept at that temperature for 20 min. The resulting $ \mathrm{C u_{2} S} $ nanoparticles were collected by centrifugation, washed with acetone and hexane three times and further dried in a vacuum chamber.

Cu nanoparticles synthesis. In a typical synthesis of metallic copper nanoparticles $ ^{54} $ 1.2 mmol CuBr and 15 mL oleylamine (OLA) were mixed in a three-neck flask under stirring at 80 $ ^{\circ} \mathrm{C} $ for 30 min, then 1 mmol trioctylphosphine (TOP) was added and reacted at 80 $ ^{\circ} \mathrm{C} $ until obtaining a colorless solution. The mixed solution was heated to 260 $ ^{\circ} \mathrm{C} $ quickly and kept for 1h. The resulting Cu nanoparticles were precipitated by centrifugation, purified with acetone and hexane for three times and further dried in a vacuum chamber.

Electrochemical measurements. Electrocatalytic measurements were carried out in a three-electrode system at an electrochemical station (AUT50517). All potentials were measured against an Ag/AgCl reference electrode (3M KCl, BASi) and converted to the reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) reference scale using:

$$ E \left(v e r s u s R H E\right) = E \left(v e r s u s A g / A g C l\right) + 0. 1 9 7 V + 0. 0 5 9 1 \times p H $$

$ \mathrm{C O_{2}} $ reduction electrolysis and product analysis. The electrolysis was performed in a two-compartment electrochemical H-cell with a proton exchange membrane (Nafion 117) as the separator and a flow-cell configuration consisting of a gas-diffusion layer with an anion exchange membrane.
