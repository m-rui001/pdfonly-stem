# Mechanism of Hydrogen Evolution Reaction on 1T-MoS $ _{2} $ from First Principles

Qing Tang and De-en Jiang*

Department of Chemistry, University of California, Riverside, CA 92521, USA

*To whom correspondence should be addressed. E-mail: de-en.jiang@ucr.edu.

Abstract: The 1T phase of transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs) has been demonstrated in recent experiments to display catalytic activity for hydrogen evolution reaction (HER), but the catalytic mechanism has not been elucidated so far. Herein, using 1T $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ as the prototypical TMD material, we studied the HER activity on its basal plane from periodic density functional theory (DFT) calculations. Compared to the non-reactive basal plane of 2H phase $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ , the catalytic activity of the basal plane of 1T phase $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ mainly arises from its affinity for binding H at the surface S sites. Using the binding free energy $ (\Delta\mathrm{G_{H}}) $ of H as the descriptor, we found that the optimum evolution of $ \mathrm{H_{2}} $ will proceed at surface H coverage of 12.5% $ \sim $ 25%. Within this coverage, we examined the reaction energy and kinetic activation barrier for the three elementary steps of the HER process. The Volmer step was found to be facile, while the subsequent Heyrovsky reaction is kinetically more favorable than the Tafel reaction. Our results suggest that at low overpotential, HER can take place readily on the basal plane of 1T $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ via the Volmer-Heyrovsky mechanism. We further screened the dopants for the HER activity and found that substitutional doping of the Mo atom by metals such as Mn, Cr, Cu, Ni, and Fe can make 1T $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ a better HER catalyst.

Keywords: 1T $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ , hydrogen evolution reaction, catalytic activity, basal plane, Volmer- Heyrovsky mechanism, substitutional doping

1. Introduction

Molecular hydrogen is the cleanest energy carrier. Water splitting from either light or renewable energy $ ^{1,2} $ provides the most sustainable supply of $ \mathrm{H}{2} $ . Electrochemical hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) in acidic media is most effectively catalyzed by Pt and its alloys. However, the scarcity and high cost of the Pt-group metals prevent wider adoption of Pt. $ ^{3} $ For years, the scientific community has been actively exploring earth-abundant materials to replace Pt. Many types of promising HER electrocatalysts have been developed, $ ^{4,5} $ including non-noble transition metals (Fe, Co, Ni), $ ^{6,7} $ transition metal phosphides $ (\mathrm{N i}{2} \mathrm{P},^{8} \mathrm{C o P}^{9}) $ , carbides $ (\mathrm{M o}{2} \mathrm{C}),^{10} $ nitrides, $ ^{11} $ metal-free catalysts (for example, fullerenol, $ ^{12} $ carbon nanotubes, $ ^{13} $ boron $ ^{14} $ or nitrogen doped graphene, $ ^{15} $ $ \mathrm{C}{3} \mathrm{N}_{4} $ $ ^{16} $ ), and pyrite-type cobalt phosphosulphide (CoPS) - currently the best non-Pt catalyst. $ ^{17} $

Recently, molybdenum disulfide $ \mathrm{(M o S_{2})} $ has been identified as a promising active and acidstable catalyst for HER. $ ^{18-20} $ $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ belongs to a family of layered transition-metal dichalcogenides (TMDs) where the individual S-metal-S sandwich layers weakly interact with each other. $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ naturally occurs as the semiconducting and thermodynamically favorable 2H phase. The bulk layered $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ itself, however, has poor HER activity, and its (0001) basal planes are electrocatalytically inert due to the poor electrical conductivity which hinders charge transfer kinetics. In 2005, Nørskov et al. suggested from theoretical studies that the electrocatalytic hydrogen evolution on $ 2 \mathrm{H}-\mathrm{M o S}{2} $ nanoparticles is mainly driven by the metallic edges. $ ^{21} $ In 2007, Chorkendroff et al. experimentally proved that the exposed edge sites (a sulfided Mo edge) of $ 2 \mathrm{H}-\mathrm{M o S}{2} $ are indeed the active sites for HER. $ ^{22} $ Moreover, a direct experimental comparison between the basal and edge planes of $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ verified that the edge plane exhibits much higher electrochemical activity and faster heterogeneous electron transfer (HET) rate over the inactive

basal plane. $ ^{23} $ This understanding has ignited significant interest in the investigations and developments of $ 2 \mathrm{H}-\mathrm{M o S}_{2} $ nanostructures with high percentage of active edge sites as electrocatalysts for achieving better HER performance. $ ^{24-33} $

The discovery of $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ -based HER catalyst opens up an exciting field for research activity. However, the overall catalytic activity of $ 2 \mathrm{H}-\mathrm{M o S}{2} $ is still limited by poor electrical transport and inefficient charge transfer at the interface, which can be tuned by electrochemical pretreatment such as electrochemical reduction. $ ^{34,35} $ Excitingly, recent advances have showed that the HER catalytic activity can be significantly enhanced when the semiconducting 2H phase $ \mathrm{M o S{2}} $ is converted into metallic 1T phase $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ nanosheets. $ ^{36-39} $ The Tafel slope was greatly reduced from 110 mV/decade for $ 2 \mathrm{H}-\mathrm{M o S}{2} $ to 43 mV/decade for $ 1 \mathrm{T}-\mathrm{M o S}{2} $ , approaching that of Pt at 30 mV/decade. $ ^{38} $ However, the detailed mechanism is unclear.

The meta-stable 1T phase is not found in nature, but can be made via intercalation of the $ 2 \mathrm{H}-\mathrm{M o S}{2} $ lattice with lithium or organolithium compounds. $ ^{36,40} $ In 2H phase, each Mo center is prismatically coordinated by six surrounding S atoms, whereas in the 1T phase the Mo atom is octahedrally coordinated to six neighboring S. Recent experimental $ ^{41} $ and computational $ ^{42} $ results revealed that $ 1 \mathrm{T M o S}{2} $ has high reactivity towards surface covalent functionalization. By contrast, the 2H phase cannot be functionalized directly. Particularly for the catalytic properties, the surface reactivity of the conducting $ 1 \mathrm{T M o S}{2} $ nanosheets towards binding H as well as the improved charge transfer kinetics might be responsible for their substantially enhanced HER catalytic activity compared to few-layer $ 2 \mathrm{H}-\mathrm{M o S}{2} $ nanosheets. Unlike $ 2 \mathrm{H M o S}{2} $ where the catalytic activity arises from the edges, expriments by Chhowalla et al. sugggested that the active sites of chemically exfoliated $ 1 \mathrm{T M o S}{2} $ nanosheets are mainly located on the basal plane and the contribution of the metallic edges to the overall HER efficiency is relatively small. $ ^{36} $ The much

greater active surface area of 1T nanosheets with respect to the edge portion thus guarantees the higher HER activity. In addition, the catalytic activity of exfoliated $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ depends strongly on the organolithium compounds used: exfoliations with n-butyllithium and t-butyllithium produced $ 1 \mathrm{T} \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ sheets that were catalytically more active than those obtained with methyllithium. $ ^{43} $ It is worth noting that the polymorph control from 2H phase to 1T phase has demonstrated improved catalytic and electrochemical activity not only for $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ but also for other analogous TMD materials such as $ \mathrm{M o S e_{2}} $ , $ \mathrm{W S_{2}} $ , and $ \mathrm{W S e_{2}} $ $ ^{44-46} $ This implies that the phase control of $ \mathrm{M X_{2}} $ structures may play an important role on their HER properties.

Despite the exciting experimental advances, the reaction mechanism and reaction pathways of the HER process with 1T $ \mathrm{M X_{2}} $ catalysts remain unknown. As a representative $ \mathrm{M X_{2}} $ material, understanding the HER process on 1T $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ will shed light on the understanding of other $ \mathrm{M X_{2}} $ structures that might be relevant for the given application. Several recent theoretical studies examined the HER activity of 1T $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ and related $ \mathrm{M X_{2}} $ materials $ ^{47-54} $ and mainly focused on the H adsorption free energy. But a complete HER pathway with the explicit consideration of the water layer has not been reported. More important, the metastatble 1T $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ can be stabilized by chemical functionalization, so it would be highly desirable to find out whether the functionalized 1T is active for HER. Since hydrogen atom can functionalize the 1T-MoS $ 2 $ phase and adsorbed H is also important for HER in acidic conditions, here we use density functional theory (DFT) calculations to examine the HER activity and mechanism on the basal plane of the 1T $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ monolayer at different hydrogen coverages. We further consider tuning the HER activity by doping.

2. Method

The DFT simulations of the adsorption and reaction process involved in HER on 1T $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ were performed by using the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP). $ ^{55} $ The ion-electron interaction is described with the projector augmented wave (PAW) method. $ ^{56} $ Electron exchangecorrelation is represented by the functional of Perdew, Burke and Ernzerhof (PBE) of generalized gradient approximation (GGA). $ ^{57} $ A cutoff energy of 400 eV was used for the planewave basis set. The $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ /electrolyte interface is modeled using 1T $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ monolayer with one layer of water film on top and excess H atoms in the water layer. In this model, we used the recently developed DFT-D3 method to include dispersion correction for the hydrogen bonding interactions between water molecules. $ ^{58} $ The advantage of including the water layer is that we can correlate the potential with the calculated work function and study the proton-electron transfer reaction (Volmer & Heyrovsky), which would be impossible for previous DFT calculations without considering the water layer.

The calculations were carried out in periodically repeated $ 6 \times4\sqrt{3} $ and $ 9 \times4\sqrt{3} $ rectangular supercells of $ 1 \mathrm{T} \mathrm{M o S}{2} $ monolayer with $ 4 \times4 \times1 $ and $ 2 \times3 \times1 $ Monkhorst Pack k-point sampling, respectively. The climbing-image nudged elastic band (CI-NEB) method $ ^{59} $ implemented in VASP was used to determine the diffusion energy barrier and the minimum energy pathways for $ \mathrm{H}{2} $ evolution on $ 1 \mathrm{T} \mathrm{M o S}{2} $ surface. The convergence threshold for structural optimization was set to be 0.02 eV/ $ \mathring{\mathrm{A}} $ in force. The transition states were obtained by relaxing the force below 0.05 eV/ $ \mathring{\mathrm{A}} $ . Note that with adsorption of H, the $ 1 \mathrm{T} \mathrm{M o S}{2} $ lattice will become structurally distorted to the $ 1 \mathrm{T}^{\prime} $ phase, as denoted in other theoretical papers. $ ^{47,50,51} $ In this paper, we do not explicitly distinguish the two phases but use the 1T phase for both cases.

The free-energy of the adsorption atomic hydrogen $ \left( \Delta G_{\mathrm{H}} \right) $ is obtained by $ \Delta G_{\mathrm{H}}=\Delta E_{\mathrm{H}}+\Delta E_{\mathrm{ZPE}}-T \Delta S_{\mathrm{H}}. $ $ \Delta E_{\mathrm{H}} $ describes the energy needed to increase the coverage by one hydrogen atom, which is calculated as follows: $ \Delta E_{\mathrm{H}}=\mathrm{E}[\mathrm{M o S}{2}+\mathrm{n H}]-\mathrm{E}[\mathrm{M o S}{2}+(\mathrm{n}-1)\mathrm{H}]-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{E}[\mathrm{H}{2}], $ where $ \mathrm{E}[\mathrm{M o S}{2}+\mathrm{n H}] $ and $ \mathrm{E}[\mathrm{M o S}{2}+(\mathrm{n}-1)\mathrm{H}] $ represent the total energy of the 1T-MoS $ {2} $ system with n and n-1 adsorbed hydrogen atoms on the surface, respectively, while $ \mathrm{E} \left( \mathrm{H}{2} \right) $ represents the total energy of a gas phase $ \mathrm{H}{2} $ molecule. $ \Delta E_{\mathrm{ZPE}} $ is calculated by: $ \Delta E_{ZPE}=E_{ZPE}^{n H}-Z_{ZPE}^{(n-1) H}-\frac{1}{2} E_{ZPE}^{H_{2}} $ where $ E_{ZPE}^{n H} $ denotes the zero-point energy of n adsorbed hydrogens on 1T-MoS $ {2} $ (without the contribution of 1T-MoS $ {2} $ catalyst), and $ E_{ZPE}^{H_{2}} $ denotes the zero-point energy of gas phase $ \mathrm{H}{2}. $ The calculated vibrational frequencies of H adsorbed on 1T-MoS $ {2} $ are 2280 $ \mathrm{cm}^{-1} $ , 690 $ \mathrm{cm}^{-1} $ and 570 $ \mathrm{cm}^{-1} $ , which do not differ notably for the different H coverages. The calculated vibrational frequencies for $ \mathrm{H}{2} $ gas are 2730 $ \mathrm{cm}^{-1} $ , 210 $ \mathrm{cm}^{-1} $ and 150 $ \mathrm{cm}^{-1} $ . Thus $ \Delta E{\mathrm{ZPE}} $ is simplified as 0.06 eV. Further, $ \Delta S_{\mathrm{H}} $ is obtained by: $ \Delta S_{H}\cong \frac{1}{2} S_{H_{2}}^{0}, $ where $ S_{H_{2}}^{0} $ is the entropy of $ \mathrm{H}{2} $ in the gas phase at standard condition, and the gas phase value can be taken from reference $ \left( S{H_{2}}^{0} \sim1 3 0 \mathrm{~J} \cdot\mathrm{m o l}^{-1} \cdot\mathrm{K}^{-1} \right). $ $ ^{60} $ Therefore, $ \Delta G_{\mathrm{H}} $ can be rewritten as $ \Delta G_{\mathrm{H}}=\Delta E_{\mathrm{H}}+0.26 $ , where 0.26 eV is a correction constant.

3. Results and discussion

  1. 1 Gibbs free energy $ \Delta G_{\mathrm{H}} $ of atomic hydrogen adsorbed on a catalyst is considered as a good descriptor of HER activity. $ ^{61} $ An optimum HER activity can be achieved at a value of $ \Delta G_{\mathrm{H}} $ close to zero $ (\Delta G_{\mathrm{H}}\sim0) $ indicating that the free energy of adsorbed H is close to that of the reactant or product. Lower $ \Delta G_{\mathrm{H}} $ will lead to strong bonding to adsorbed hydrogen, while higher $ \Delta G_{\mathrm{H}} $ will make the protons bonded too weakly to the catalyst surface, both leading to slow HER kinetics. The calculated free energy $ \Delta G_{\mathrm{H}} $ as a function of surface H coverage in the absence of water and a bias potential is shown in Figure 1. The adsorption geometries at different H coverage were provided in Supporting Information (Figure S1). Once H is adsorbed, the 1T phase structurally transforms to the 1T' phase (Figure S2). This structure change helps adsorption of H on the 1T phase, but another important factor is the 1T phase's metallicity and partially filled Mo 4d states. $ ^{42} $ From Figure 1, one can see that the free energy close to zero occurs around 12.5% $ \sim $ 25% H coverage (the $ \Delta G_{\mathrm{H}} $ value is between -0.28 eV and 0.13 eV). Therefore, it is most likely that the hydrogen evolution reaction is mainly driven by the hydrogen adsorption at 12.5% $ \sim $ 25% coverage. Particularly, the appropriate H adsorption energy suggests that the basal plane of 1T $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ is catalytically active, in good agreement with other theoretical results. $ ^{47,48,50} $ The adsorbed H prefers to bind to the surface S atoms, regardless of the coverage. Our previous work showed that the metastable 1T $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ can be stabilized by hydrogenation and after a crossover coverage of 25%, the 1T phase becomes more stable than the 2H phase. $ ^{42} $ Therefore, considering $ \Delta G_{\mathrm{H}} $ and the relative stability together, one can see that 25% coverage of hydrogen is optimal.

  1. 2 The water-solid interface. Now that we have found out the optimal coverage of H on 1T $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ , next we examine its HER activity. To simulate the water-solid interface under an electrochemical potential, here we adopted an approach previously used by Nørskov et al. in elucidating the reaction mechanism of HER on Pt(111) electrode. $ ^{62} $ The interface region (or the Helmholtz layer) is often approximated by about 3 $ \mathring{\mathrm{A}} $ thick electrical double layer, which can be represented by one water layer. Each proton concentration in the water layer corresponds to a certain electrode potential versus the normal hydrogen electrode (NHE), where the electrode potential is deduced from the calculated work function. By varying the number of hydrogen atoms added, the electrostatic potential of the water-solid interface can be varied.

In our system, the water-solid interface in an acid solution was modeled with one layer of proton-containing water molecules on the surface of H-adsorbed 1T-MoS $ {2} $ monolayer. We considered intermediate-sized $ 6 \times4 \sqrt{3} $ (Figure 2a) and $ 9 \times4 \sqrt{3} $ (Figure 2b) lateral cells of 1T- $ \mathrm{M o S{2}} $ monolayer with 25% H coverage. To determine a stable concentration of protons in the surface water layer, we performed 10 ps of DFT molecular dynamics simulation at different numbers of protons in the water layer and found that the 1T-MoS $ {2} $ monolayer having two protons (or excess hydrogen atoms) in the water layer containing 16 $ \mathrm{H{2}O} $ molecules is stable. Moreover, to determine the stable water structure, we performed parallel DFT-MD simulations with three different initial water structures: (1) the molecular plane of $ \mathrm{H_{2}O} $ molecule parallel to the surface; (2) the H atom of each $ \mathrm{H_{2}O} $ molecule pointing down towards the surface; (3) the H atom of each $ \mathrm{H_{2}O} $ molecule pointing up away from the surface. The structure with the lowest energy after MD runs was chosen as the model for the subsequent mechanistic studies. The annealed water model is different from the initial water structure. Specifically, in the annealed structure, the $ \mathrm{H_{2}O} $ molecules atop surface S atoms will adopt the H-down configuration, due to the hydrogen bond

between H and the surface S, while those $ \mathrm{H}{2} \mathrm{O} $ molecules with adsorbed H underneath will adopt the flat-lying configuration. Figure 2a presents the optimized structure of $ 1 \mathrm{T}-\mathrm{M o S}{2} $ containing 2 solvated protons at 25% H coverage, where the two hydronium ions are indicated by arrows. The water layer in our model is disordered, probalby caused by the structural distortion of the $ 1 \mathrm{T} $ $ \mathrm{M o S}{2} $ lattice, the adsorbed H atoms on the surface, and the hydronium ions in the water layer. To our knowledge, there has been no experimental study of the exact structure of the water overlayer on $ 1 \mathrm{T} \mathrm{M o S}{2} $ . The calculated electrode potential (relative to NHE) corresponding to the model system in Figure 2a is -0.22 V. This is in good agreement with the experimentally measured overpotential, where the exfoliated $ 1 \mathrm{T}-\mathrm{M o S}{2} $ nanosheets exhibit an onset of HER activity at a low overpotential of approximately -0.20 V vs reversible hydrogen electrode. In our following study on the elementary reaction steps of HER process, we will keep the proton concentration in the water layer fixed to be 1/8, i.e., 2 protons out of 16 water molecules for the $ 6 \times4 \sqrt{3} $ superstructure (Figure 2a). To examine the effect of cell size, we also considered a larger unit cell, the $ 9 \times4 \sqrt{3} $ superstructure, with its water layer (Figure 2b) that contains 3 protons out of 24 water molecules, thereby maintaining the same $ \mathrm{H}^{+} / \mathrm{H}{2} \mathrm{O} $ ratio of 1/8.

The Volmer reaction refers to the initial adsorption of protons from the acid solution to form adsorbed $ \mathrm{H}\left(\mathrm{H}^{+}+\mathrm{e}^{-}\rightarrow\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{ad}}) $ , which is usually considered to be fast. First we consider the initial step of HER process: the Volmer reaction. Figure 3a presents the minimum-energy path of transferring one of the solvated protons to the $ 1 \mathrm{T}-\mathrm{M o S}{2} $ surface for the $ 6 \times4 \sqrt{3} $ supercell; one can see that the activation energy is small (at 0.004 eV). At the transition state, the proton H is bonded between O and S atoms, and the O-H and S-H bond lengths are about 1.25 $ \mathring{\mathrm{A}} $ and 1.64 $ \mathring{\mathrm{A}} $ , respectively. We also considered a larger unit cell, the $ 9 \times4 \sqrt{3} $ supercell, and the minimum-energy path shows an activation energy of 0.05 eV and a similar transition state (Figure 3b). To extrapolate the reaction and activation energies to the limit where the potential does not change $ (\Delta U $ close to 0 V), we also included the data for another system with a different hydrogen coverage in the final state and the results are shown in Figure 4. We obtained a reaction energy $ (\Delta E $ between final state and initial state) of -0.07 eV (Figure 4a) and an activation energy of 0.16 eV (Figure 4b). This indicates that the Volmer process (Scheme 1) is thermally neutral $ (\Delta E\sim0) $ and has a small energy barrier at the electrode potential around -0.2~- 0.3 V vs. NHE and hydrogen coverage around 25% on the $ 1 \mathrm{T}-\mathrm{M o S}{2} $ monolayer.

  1. 5 The second step of the HER process via the Tafel reaction. We next turn to the Tafel reaction, where two surface H atoms chemically desorb to form $ \mathrm{H_{2}} $ . Since there is no charge transfer involved in the Tafel process (the work functions of the initial and final states remain nearly unchanged, so the potential of the reaction system will stay the same), it is not necessary to use the extrapolation method as used for the Volmer and Heyrovsky reactions to determine $ \Delta E $ and Ea. In this case, we calculated the energetics of Tafel reaction on the $ 6 \times4 \sqrt{3} $ superstructure. Figure 7a presents the minimum-energy path of the Tafel reaction with an initial hydrogen coverage of 5/16. The initial state has an H-H distance (two adjacent H atoms bonded to the surface S atoms) of 3.56 $ \mathrm{\AA} $ . The two H atoms then approached and formed a transition state with H-H distance of 1.24. In the final state, the evolved $ \mathrm{H_{2}} $ molecule desorbs from the surface with H-H bond length of 0.76 $ \mathrm{\AA} $ . The activation energy for this Tafel-step reaction is 1.07 eV, with a favorable reaction energy of -0.79 eV.

Since the Tafel reaction is a surface-dominated process and less dependent on the water layer, it will be more sensitive to the surface hydrogen coverage. As shown in Figure 7b, as the hydrogen coverage increases, the activation barrier of the Tafel reaction decreases from 1.19 eV for hydrogen coverage of 4/16 to 0.89 eV for hydrogen coverage of 7/16. We also computed the corresponding electrochemical potential (from work function) at different hydrogen coverages (Figure 7b). One can see that the potential becomes more negative with coverage and the Tafel step of homolytic hydrogen desorption from surface becomes easier under more negative overpotential. For the electrode potential between $ - 0. 2 \sim- 0. 3 \mathrm{V} $ where the HER happens on $ 1 \mathrm{T}- \mathrm{M o S}{2} $ , the corresponding hydrogen coverage lies between 4/16 and 5/16, and the activation barrier for the Tafel reaction is still higher than 1 eV. In addition, Figure 1 shows that 25% (4/16) is an optimal coverage, in terms of binding of hydrogen with $ 1 \mathrm{T}- \mathrm{M o S}{2}. $

Experimentally the Tafel slope is viewed as an intrinsic property of electrocatalysts, which is determined by the rate-limiting step and allows for the distinction of possible mechanisms for HER. Normally, the HER kinetic models suggest that the Volmer, Heyrovsky or Tafel reaction will be the rate-determining step for a Tafel slope of about 120, 40 or 30 mV per decade, respectively. $ ^{65} $ In fact, the experimentally measured Tafel slope from exfoliated metallic nanosheets of $ 1 \mathrm{T}-\mathrm{M o S}{2} $ at a low overpotential of $ \sim0.2 $ eV is about 40 mV/decade. $ ^{36,38} $ This low Tafel slope suggests that the Heyrovsky-step-limited Volmer-Heyrovsky mechanism is responsible for HER catalyzed by $ 1 \mathrm{T}-\mathrm{M o S}{2} $ nanosheets, in agreement with our DFT results. Here we note that in the microkinetic analyses of some experimental HER data, the Heyrovsky mechanism was found to be the rate-limiting step in some extreme cases. $ ^{66} $

  1. 7 Comparison with previous DFT studies. Recently, Goddard et al. reported DFT calculations to determine the HER pathways on the Mo-edge of 2H $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ monolayer using both a $ \mathrm{M o}{1 0} \mathrm{S}{2 1} $ cluster model and a periodic slab. $ ^{6 7} $ Based on free-energy barriers, they found that the HER process at 2H $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ edges takes place mainly through the Volmer-Heyrovsky mechanism and the complete pathway involves adsorption of hydrogen on S, diffusion of hydrogen from S to Mo to form molybdenum hydride (Mo-H), and formation of $ \mathrm{H_{2}} $ from (Mo-H) and a hydronium ion $ \left( \mathrm{H}{3} \mathrm{O}^{+} \right) $ . Although here we also found that HER happens via the Volmer-Heyrovsky process on the basal plane of 1T $ \mathrm{M o S{2}} $ , the detailed mechanism is very different: the formation of molybdenum hydride on 1T $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ is highly unfavorable; instead, we found that the Heyrovsky process can happen directly between hydrogen adsorbed on sulfur and a hydrogen from the hydronium ion. It is interesting to note that our estimated activation energy from the Heyrovsky step on 1T $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ is about 0.62 eV, which is comparable to the energy barrier of 0.78 eV on 2H $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ from Goddard et al. This implies that the basal plane of 1T $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ could be more

electrocatalytically active than the edges of 2H $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ . Considering the high reactivity and important catalytic role of the unsaturated edges, it would also be worthwhile in the future to extend the current study to investigate the HER activity at the edge sites of 1T $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ . More important, the high activity of the basal plane of 1T $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ suggests that the 1T phase can provide much more active areas for HER and that one could further engineer the basal plane such as by doping or creating defects to enhance the HER activity.

Several recent theoretical reports examined the HER activity of 1T $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ $ ^{47-54} $ most of them using the $ \Delta G_{\mathrm{H}} $ descriptor. Lau et al., $ ^{47} $ Kuo et al., $ ^{50} $ Du et al. $ ^{51} $ and Abild-Pedersen et al. $ ^{48} $ reported that both the basal surfaces and the edges sites of 1T- $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ are active for HER, with $ \Delta G_{\mathrm{H}} $ close to thermoneutral. Our findings on the reactivity of the basal plane are in agreement with their results. Abild-Pedersen et al. $ ^{48} $ also calculated the possibility of $ \mathrm{H_{2}} $ desorption via the Tafel mechanism on the 1T- $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ basal plane, and found an insurmountably high barrier of about 1.5 eV, which suggests that the Tafel mechanism is less unlikely the relevant route for HER. However, their calculations did not consider the interfacial solvent or the Heyrovsky mechanism. Our complete HER pathway further confirmed that the Tafel route is less favorable than the Heyrovsky one.

3.8 Doping of 1T- $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ . Doping has been widely used to tune the physical and chemical properties of materials. It is well-known that the hydrodesulfurization (HDS) performance of $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ can be significantly increased by addition of metal promoters such as Co or Ni. $ ^{68-70} $ The introduction of metal dopants such as Pt, Fe, Co and Ni has been recently demonstrated to promote the catalytic activity of 2H $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ for hydrogen generation. $ ^{71-75} $ Moreover, there have also been experimental reports of anion doping in 2H $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ lattice by $ \mathrm{O}^{76} $ and Cl, $ ^{77} $ leading to dramatically enhanced HER activity. Here we tested the doping effect on the HER activity of 1T

4. Summary and conclusions

In conclusion, we carried out DFT calculations in this work to understand the electrocatalytic HER mechanism on the basal plane of conducting 1T $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ nanosheets. Calculated $ \Delta G_{\mathrm{H}} $ indicates that the HER process is most likely to occur at the surface hydrogen coverage between 12.5%~25%, where the adsorbed H prefers to be bonded to surface S atoms. We computed reaction pathways and kinetic barriers for the three elementary steps involved in HER process. We found that at $ \sim 25\% $ hydrogen coverage (electrochemical potential close to - 0.22 V vs hydrogen electrode), the Volmer reaction is very fast (activation barrier $ \sim 0.16 $ eV); while for the hydrogen desorption process, the Heyrovsky reaction (activation barrier $ \sim 0.62 $ eV) has relatively much lower barrier than the Tafel reaction (activation barrier $ \sim 1.07 $ eV). Our finding revealed that the HER happens mainly via the Volmer-Heyrovsky mechanism on the basal plane of 1T $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ electrode at a low overpotential. The previously measured Tafel slope ( $ \sim $ 40 mv/decade at overpotential of -0.20 V) supports this mechanism. In addition, we discovered that the HER activity of 1T $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ can be further increased by doping of the $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ lattice with other metal atoms (e.g., Mn, Cr, Cu, Ni, Fe). These results offer useful insights for understanding and improving the HER activity of the 1T phase of $ \mathrm{M o S_{2}} $ and other 2D TMDs nanosheets.
