Abstract

Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have reached a certified 25.2% efficiency in 2019 due to their high absorption coefficient, high carrier mobility, long diffusion length, and tunable direct bandgap. However, due to the nature of solution processing and rapid crystal growth of perovskite thin films, a variety of defects can form as a result of the precursor compositions and processing conditions. The use of additives can affect perovskite crystallization and film formation, defect passivation in the bulk and/or at the surface, as well as influence the interface tuning of structure and energetics. In this review, we first discuss recent progress on additive engineering during perovskite film formation according to the following common categories: Lewis acid (e.g., metal cations, fullerene derivatives), Lewis base based on the donor type (e.g., O-donor, S-donor, and N-donor), ammonium salts, low-dimensional perovskites, and ionic liquid. We then compare various additive-assisted strategies for interface optimization; additives include modifiers to improve

electron- and hole-transport layers as well as those to modify perovskite surface properties. Finally, we provide an outlook on the research trends with respect to additive engineering in PSC development.

1. Introduction

Metal halide perovskites were first synthesized and reported in the 1970s, and they are in the form of $ \mathrm{A B X_{3}} $ , where $ \mathrm{A^{+}} $ = methylammonium $ (\mathrm{C H_{3} N H_{3}}^{+} $ or $ \mathrm{M A}^{+} $ ), formamidinium $ (\mathrm{H C} (\mathrm{N H_{2}}){2}^{+} $ or $ \mathrm{F A}^{+} $ ), cesium $ (\mathrm{C s}^{+}) $ , rubidium $ (\mathrm{R b}^{+}) $ ; $ \mathrm{B}^{2+} $ = lead $ (\mathrm{P b}^{2+}) $ or tin $ (\mathrm{S n}^{2+}) $ ; and $ \mathrm{X}^{-} $ = chlorine $ (\mathrm{C l}^{-}) $ , bromine $ (\mathrm{B r}^{-}) $ , or iodine $ (\mathrm{I}^{-}) $ . $ ^{[1]} $ In the 1990s, Mitzi et al. further developed more advanced solution processing as well as the understanding of structural and optoelectronic properties of these hybrid materials. $ ^{[2]} $ However, these hybrid materials did not gain widespread interest until the first few reports in 2009-2012 on solar cell application using $ \mathrm{M A P b I{3}} $ $ ^{[3-5]} $

In 2009, Miyasaka et al. first reported that the organic metal halide perovskites $ \mathrm{M A P b I_{3}} $ and $ \mathrm{M A P b B r_{3}} $ ) could function similarly as dye molecules to absorb sunlight, and they were subsequently applied as the sensitizers in dye-sensitized solar cells (DSSCs); by using a liquid electrolyte (common to DSSCs), a non-stable power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 3.8% was demonstrated. $ ^{[3]} $ In 2012, Park et al. reported all-solid-state perovskite solar cells (PSCs) with a PCE of 9.7% employing a solid-state hole-transport layer—2,2',7,7'-tetrakis(N,N-di-p-methoxyphenylamine)-9,9'-spirobifluorene (spiro-OMeTAD). $ ^{[4]} $ Around the same time, Snaith et al. also reported spiro-OMeTAD-based solid-state PSCs with PCEs $ > 10\% $ $ ^{[5]} $ These pioneering studies triggered an explosive research effort worldwide to improve the PSC fabrication with rapid advances demonstrated on optimizing device architectures and perovskite properties. $ ^{[6-10,11-14]} $ At

Due to the nature of solution processing and rapid crystal growth of perovskite thin films at relatively elevated temperatures (e.g., $ 1 0 0^{\circ} \mathrm{C}-5 0^{\circ} \mathrm{C} $), a wide range of defects are expected to form depending on the precursor composition and processing conditions. Some intrinsic point defects form shallow-level traps, such as I or MA vacancies. 2. Progress on Additive Engineering during Perovskite Formation

2.1. Lewis Acid

A Lewis acid with the capability of accepting a pair of nonbonding electrons has the capability to passivate electron-rich defects, which can also be considered as Lewis bases. $ ^{[31]} $ The addition of a Lewis acid in perovskite can form a Lewis adduct with the undercoordinated halides and $ \mathrm{P b I}_{3}^{-} $ through coordinate bonding or ionic bonding, eliminating the corresponding defects or traps and reducing undesired non-radiative recombination for longer carrier lifetimes. In this section, we divide Lewis acid into two catergories: metal cations and fullerene derivatives We disscuss the detailed effects of different strategies in each part.

2.1.1. Metal Cations

Positively charged metal cations are effective passivating agents via ionic bonding and other electrostatic interactions with the negatively charged defects in perovskites. $ \mathrm{P b I_{2}} $ is one of the typical Lewis acids. $ ^{[32]} $ Chen et al. reported an approach for self-induced passivation in $ \mathrm{M A P b I_{3}} $

第 9 页

thin film and found that releasing a moderate amount of $ \mathrm{P b I_{2}} $ would positively affect passivating surface defects at GBs, leading to the reduction of charge-carrier recombination. $ ^{[33]} $ Kim et al. also noticed that a small amount of excess $ \mathrm{P b I_{2}} $ in perovskite could greatly improve the overall performance of PSCs. $ ^{[34]} $ Presumably a small amount of $ \mathrm{P b I_{2}} $ could influence perovskite crystallinity as well as hinder ion migrations and reduce recombination (Figure 4a). Thus, adding excess (often a few percent) of $ \mathrm{P b I_{2}} $ in the absorber is generally adopted by several groups as standard process to enhance the PSC performance. $ ^{[20,35,36]} $ Following this general strategy, Ke et al. reported using a small amount of lead thiocyanate $ (\mathrm{P b(SCN){2}}) $ additive in the perovskite precursor; they hypothesized that during the perovskite film formation, $ \mathrm{M A^{+}} $ cations and $ \mathrm{S C N^{-}} $ anions react and form HSCN and MA gases, which promote the grain growth, in addition to the passivation effect from the formation of $ \mathrm{P b I{2}} $ at GBs reducing dark current (Figure 4b). $ ^{[37]} $

Bi et al. found that sodium ions $ \mathrm{(N a^{+}}) $ , which were present on substrates, were capable of diffusing into GBs over time, leading to a self-passivation to reduce defect density. $ ^{[38]} $ $ \mathrm{N a^{+}} $ could potentially passivate negatively charged defects, either by being adsorbed at MA vacancies or forming an ionic bond with undercoordinated halides. Li et al. found the similar phenomenon of $ \mathrm{L i^{+}} $ diffusion from HTL to perovskite, leading to improved PSC performance. $ ^{[39]} $ Abdi-Jalebi et al. recently showed that when adding potassium iodide (KI) to a $ \mathrm{C s}{0.06} \mathrm{F A}{0.79} \mathrm{M A}{0.15} \mathrm{P b} \left( \mathrm{I}{0.85} \mathrm{B r}{0.15} \right){3} $ perovskite precursor, the potassium ions $ \left( \mathrm{K}^{+} \right) $ could accumulate at the surface and GBs to passivate those extended defects, producing perovskite thin films with a higher PCE of 21.5% (Table 1). $ ^{[11]} $ The improved device performance was mainly attributed to two functions of KI (Figure 4c). First, the excess $ \mathrm{I}^{-} $ from KI can help fill in the $ \mathrm{I}^{-} $ vacancies and thus suppress potential pathways for ion migration. Second, the $ \mathrm{K}^{+} $ cations can also ionically bond with undercoordinated halides or

第 10 页

passivate negatively charged trap states at the grain surface, which is also expected to reduce ion migration through suppressing the pathways or raising the activation energy for ion migration.

The crystal structure and properties of hybrid halide perovskites incorporating various alkali-metal cations were investigated systematically by Tang et al. $ ^{[40]} $ The X-ray diffraction (XRD) results suggested that the lattice constant of the perovskites incorporating $ \mathrm{L i}^{+}, \mathrm{N a}^{+}, $ or $ \mathrm{K}^{+} $ is larger than that of the pristine perovskite, whereas the incorporation of $ \mathrm{R b}^{+} $ or $ \mathrm{C s}^{+} $ does not change the lattice constant. The measurements of carrier lifetime and defect density showed that the $ \mathrm{K}^{+} $ - containing perovskite had the least number of defects, followed by the perovskites containing $ \mathrm{R b}^{+}, $ $ \mathrm{C s}^{+}, $ and $ \mathrm{N a}^{+}, $ which agrees with the device performance. The same results were also confirmed by others. $ ^{[41]} $ The ionic interaction between $ \mathrm{K}^{+} $ and halides at GBs has been confirmed by Kubicki et al. using solid-state nuclear magnetic resonance; the presence of KI with no change in the local environments of N and C atoms was observed. $ ^{[42]} $ Through bonding with halides, $ \mathrm{K}^{+} $ is also effective at suppressing ion migration (Figure 4d). $ ^{[43]} $ Note that calculations by Son et al. show that small alkali-metal ions such as $ \mathrm{K}^{+}, $ with radii of 1.38 $ \mathrm{\AA}, $ could be energetically favorable to occupy the interstitial sites within the perovskite lattice (Figure 4e). $ ^{[44]} $ These filled interstitial sites could prevent the formation of iodide Frenkel defects and hinder ion migration and result in a similar passivation effect to ionic bonding at the surface and GBs. The same mechanism has also been reported by others. $ ^{[45]} $ $ \mathrm{K}^{+} $ is apparently effective for passivating defects in perovskites, but the true underlying mechanisms need to be fully studied with both experimental and theoretical supports. One challenge is associated with conflicting reports on the positions of metal ions within lattice or at the grain surface/boundaries. $ ^{[11, 42, 44, 46]} $

In a recent study, Li et al. employed fluoride to simultaneously passivate both anion and cation vacancies by taking advantage of the extremely high electronegativity of fluoride. $ ^{[47]} $ NaF

第 11 页

was shown to be present in the perovskite thin film and forms hydrogen bonds with organic cations within the perovskite crystals, which effectively retards the diffusion of these cations and their dissociation. Fluoride ions also increase the ionic bonding, thus immobilizing both organic cations and halide anions, leading to reduced degradation with the device by retaining 90% of its initial cell efficiency (>21%) after 1000 h of operation at the maximum power point (MPP). In another study, Wang et al. showed that the europium ion pair $ \mathrm{E u}^{3+}-\mathrm{E u}^{2+} $ can act as the "redox shuttle" to selectively oxidize metallic Pb $ (\mathrm{P b}^{0}) $ and reduce iodine $ (\mathrm{I}^{0}) $ defects in a cyclical transition (Figure 4f) for improved device stability. $ ^{[48]} $ It is evident that other metal ions, such as $ \mathrm{C u}^{+}, \mathrm{C u}^{2+}, \mathrm{A g}^{+}, $ $ \mathrm{Z n}^{2+}, \mathrm{M g}^{2+}, \mathrm{C a}^{2+}, \mathrm{N i}^{2+}, \mathrm{S r}^{2+}, \mathrm{B a}^{2+}, \mathrm{F e}^{2+}, \mathrm{R b}^{+} $ and $ \mathrm{B i}^{3+} $ are also capable of defect passivation. $ ^{[46,49, 50, 51-53]} $ However, additional research is needed to fully uncover the specific mechanisms that would be beneficial to the field moving forward.

2.1.2. Fullerene Derivatives

Since the discovery of fullerenes in 1985, $ ^{[54]} $ they have been extensively investigated because of their unique structural and electronic properties. Fullerenes have several attractive

第 14 页

features for PV application, such as high electron affinity, small reorganization energy, and high electron mobility. $ ^{[55]} $ Fullerene-based materials have been widely employed in PSCs, acting as electron-transport materials, interfacial-modification materials, or trap-state passivators within the perovskite layer. $ ^{[56]} $ These fullerene organic molecules can coat the grain surfaces and boundaries, providing a steric hinderance to ion migration by physically blocking mobile ions moving along grain boundaries. $ ^{[57]} $ In this part, we focus on the trap-state passivation within the perovskite layer.

$ \mathrm{C}{60} $ derivatives as well as pristine fullerene are also used to form perovskite-fullerene bulk heterojunctions. $ ^{[58,59,60,61]} $ The heterojunction of $ \mathrm{C}{60} $ -MAPb $ 0.75 $ Sn $ 0.25 $ I $ {3} $ exhibits longer bulk and surface recombination lifetimes and lower trap density, leading to an early demonstration of 13.9% efficiency with reduced hysteresis and improved stability (Table 2). $ ^{[58]} $ Wang et al. demonstrated a remarkable fill factor (FF) of 86.7% by mixing perovskites with the water-/alcohol-soluble fullerene derivative $ \mathrm{A}{10} \mathrm{C}{60} $ (where A is a carboxylic acid group regiospecifically functionalized on the $ \mathrm{C}{60} $ head) with balanced charge-carrier extraction and enlarged perovskite- $ \mathrm{A}{10} \mathrm{C}{60} $ interfacial area. $ ^{[60]} $ A cross-linkable [6,6]-phenyl- $ \mathrm{C}_{61} $ -butyric styryl dendron ester (PCBSD) was introduced into the perovskite to improve device stability. $ ^{[61]} $

In addition to $ \mathrm{C}{60} $ , Phenyl-C61-butyric acid methyl ester $ (\mathrm{P C}{61}\mathrm{B M}) $ has been extensively investigated in a perovskite- $ \mathrm{P C}{61}\mathrm{B M} $ bulk heterojunction to enhance device efficiency and reduce hysteresis. Shao et al. showed that the spun $ \mathrm{P C}{61}\mathrm{B M} $ layer can penetrate into the perovskite layer via GBs during annealing in a p-i-n device configuration. $ ^{[62]} $ Xu et al. later reported the use of a perovskite- $ \mathrm{P C}{61}\mathrm{B M} $ bulk heterojunction in a n-i-p device configuration with reduced hysteresis and improved performance that was attributed to $ \mathrm{P C}{61}\mathrm{B M} $ passivation of iodide-rich trap sites at the GBs and which promoted electron extraction (Figure 5a). $ ^{[63]} $ The perovskite- $ \mathrm{P C}_{61}\mathrm{B M} $ bulk heterojunction concept was further shown to be an effective approach for improving perovskite

第 15 页

grain size in p-i-n PSCs with less hysteresis (Figure 5b). $ ^{[64]} $ Recent studies further revealed that incorporating $ \mathrm{P C}{61} $ BM in perovskite can also hinder the drift of ions and promote charge-extraction efficiency. $ ^{[65]} $ Ran et al. reported one-dimensional (1D) $ \mathrm{P C}{61} $ BM nanorods as an effective additive to form a wrinkle-like bicontinuous perovskite layer. $ ^{[66]} $ The resultant interconnected 1D PCBM nanorod within the perovskite material can facilitate the charge separation and transport, resulting in about a 50% improvement of device performance.

Fullerene additives can also be used in the anti-solvent to improve perovskite growth and passivate the defects. To simultaneously improve the device performance and stability, hydrophobic fullerene derivatives have been introduced into perovskite-fullerene bulk heterojunction devices through the antisolvent step. $ ^{[67-71,72]} $ A novel perovskite-fullerene graded heterojunction (GHJ) was introduced by Wu et al. by adding $ \mathrm{P C}{61} \mathrm{B M} $ in the antisolvent during spin coating of the perovskite precursor in a p-i-n structure (Figure 5c). $ ^{[67]} $ Compared to a planar heterojunction and bulk heterojunction, the graded heterojunction structure was beneficial for separation of electrons and holes into opposite directions at the junction interface for more effective charge collection. As a result, the GHJ structure significantly reduced recombination loss, resulting in higher device performance. In another study, isomer-pure bis(1-[3-(methoxycarbonyl)propyl]-1-phenyl)-[6.6] $ \mathrm{C}{62} $ $ (\alpha $ -bis-PCBM, Figure 5d) was employed as a templating agent to assist perovskite growth and film formation. $ ^{[68]} $ The introduction of $ \alpha $ -bis-PCBM into the perovskite improved perovskite crystallization, electron extraction, and device stability under heat and light soaking. The better stability was attributed to the use of $ \alpha $ -bis-PCBM, which passivates the voids or pinholes generated in the active layer and prevents the moisture-induced erosion of perovskites. The device efficiency was also improved to 20.8% with $ \alpha $ -bis-PCBM, compared with 19.9% of $ \mathrm{P C}_{61} $ BM-containing PSCs. Martin et al. reported site-selective

2.2. Lewis Base

Lewis bases usually function as electron donors, which can bind to the positively charged undercoordinated $ \mathrm{P b}^{2+} $ ions, forming a Lewis adduct and passivating the defects. $ ^{[75]} $ In this section, we describe typical examples of Lewis-base additives based on the donor type such as O-donor, S-donor, and N-donor. We also summarize recent progress with details on the corresponding donor bases.

2.2.1. O-Donor Lewis Base

O-donor Lewis base describes a structure where an oxygen-bearing lone pair of electrons forms a coordinate covalent bond with undercoordinated $ \mathrm{P b}^{2+} $ ions. Some solvents—such as N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF), N,N-dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP)—are found to be polar aprotic and with an oxygen-bearing lone pair of electrons, which indicates that those solvents can act as O-donor Lewis bases. Solvent molecules (e.g., DMF) can interact with MAI and $ \mathrm{P b I}{2} $ to form a metastable intermediate (e.g., $ \mathrm{M A P b I}{3}\cdot \mathrm{D M F} $ ） $ ^{[76]} $ The morphology of the intermediate often determines the final perovskite film morphology. In 2014, Jeon et al. demonstrated a solvent engineering method by using an antisolvent washing step during spin coating of the perovskite precursor to cause rapid supersaturation for uniform and dense nucleaion and formation of perovskite grains leading to the formation of a compact, smooth, fully

第 20 页

covered, highly crystalline perovskite thin film. In this study, the S-donor Lewis base DMSO was used to retard the rapid reaction between MAI and $ \mathrm{P b I_{2}} $ via formation of a stable and uniform MAI $ \cdot $ $ \mathrm{P b I_{2}} $ $ \cdot $ DMSO intermediate phase, which is later converted to perovskite via annealing (Figure 6a). $ ^{[77]} $ Later, Ahn et al. reported on the adduct formation of $ \mathrm{P b I_{2}} $ $ \cdot $ DMSO and MAI $ \cdot $ $ \mathrm{P b I_{2}} $ $ \cdot $ DMSO from the interaction between Lewis base DMSO and/or iodide $ (\mathrm{I}^{-}) $ and Lewis acid $ \mathrm{P b I_{2}} $ ; the adduct was shown to be critical for higher charge extraction and slower recombination rate of the final perovskite films. $ ^{[12]} $ Similar results were also reported by others with different types of perovskites. $ ^{[78]} $ Lee et al. recently demonstrated that replacement of DMSO with NMP enabled the formation of a stable intermediate adduct phase, which can be converted into a uniform and pinhole-free $ \mathrm{F A P b I_{3}} $ film with the highest PSC efficiency over 20% (Figure 6b, Table 3). $ ^{[79]} $ This study also pointed out several criteria for further development of Lewis bases for preparing highquality and stable perovskite films: (1) high hydrogen-bond acceptor ability and low hydrogen-bond donor ability; (2) sterically accessible electron-donating atom; and (3) matching the hardness and softness of Lewis acid and Lewis base.

During the early stage of PSC development, Liang et al. first showed for the mixed halide recipe (i.e., $ 3 \mathrm{MAI}+ \mathrm{P b C l}{2} $ ) of PSC development that using 1,8-diiodooctane (DIO) increased the solubility of $ \mathrm{P b C l}{2} $ and also tuned the crystallization kinetics for much improved film coverage and smoothness. $ ^{[80]} $ In a recent study, Guan et al. demonstrated the feasibility of defect passivation by nonvolatile small molecules with carboxylic acid groups (e.g., benzoic acid, p-phthalic acid, and trimesic acid). $ ^{[81]} $

Low-cost and green polymer, ethyl cellulose (EC), was also used recently as an additive to enlarge crystal grain size and decrease defect density. $ ^{[82]} $ These improvements were attributed to the EC additives with hydrogen-bonding interactions with perovskites, leading to passivation of

defect traps at the GBs. The long chain of EC also acts as a scaffold for maintaining the perovskite structure, which suppresses the lattice strain induced by annealing during the film fabrication process. Bi et al. showed that poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA), when introduced to perovskite fabrication from the antisolvent step, can enable heterogeneous nucleation and slow down the perovskite crystallization kinetics by forming an intermediate adduct with $ \mathrm{P b I}_{2} $ , which also allows the randomly formed nuclei to adjust their orientation to minimize the total Gibbs free energy and grow in thermodynamically preferred orientation (Figure 6c). $ ^{[7]} $ These synergistic effects result in smooth perovskite films with fewer defects and larger oriented grains, leading to PSCs with PCEs over 21%.

Lee et al. reported a strategy for increasing perovskite film uniformity (reducing heterogeneity) by using urea, a bifunctional non-volatile Lewis base additive (Figure 6d). $ ^{[83]} $ The first role of the Lewis base urea is to interact with the solution precursor to retard crystal growth and enhance crystallinity; it also precipitates at GBs to passivate defects after completion of crystal growth. As a result, the macroscopic photoluminescence lifetime is enhanced, and trap-mediated non-radiative recombination is reduced. In a more recent study, Wang et al. reported using a small molecule 1,3,7-trimethylxanthine (also known as caffeine) in the MA-based perovskite (Figure 6e). $ ^{[84]} $ With its carboxyl groups, caffeine served as an effective molecular lock that interacted strongly with $ \mathrm{P b}^{2+} $ ions to slow down the perovskite grain growth and also induced a preferred orientation with reduced defect density. The use of caffeine enabled a champion PCE of 20.25% and thermal stability at $ 85^{\circ} \mathrm{C} $ for over 1300 h.

Niu et al. introduced n-type semiconducting molecules (e.g., (3,9-bis(2-methylene)-(3-(1,1- dicyanomethylene)-indanone))-5,5,11,11-tetrakis(4-hexylphenyl)-dithieno[2,3-d: $ 2^{\prime},3^{\prime}-d^{\prime} $]-s- indaceno[1,2-b:5,6-b']dithiophene) (referred to as ITIC); $ 7,7^{\prime}-[4,4-bis(2-ethylhexyl)-4H-silolo $

2.2.2. S-Donor Lewis Base

S-donor Lewis bases have a sulfur-bearing lone pair of electrons that can bind to undercoordinated $ \mathrm{P b}^{2+} $ ions. S-donors were reported to be stronger than O-donors. $ ^{[86]} $ The most commonly used S-donor Lewis bases include thiophene, thiourea, and their derivatives (Table 4). Noel et al. first showed that thiophene was effective for passivating the surface traps in perovskites because of their interaction with the undercoordinated Pb atoms (Figure 7a). $ ^{[87]} $ The derivatives of thiophene (e.g., 3-alkylthiophene $ ^{[88,]} $ and polythiophene, $ ^{[89]} $ poly[(thiophene)-alt-(6,7-difluoro- 2-(2-hexyldecyloxy)quinoxaline)] （PTQ10）， $ ^{[90]} $ （2,2'-((5Z,5'Z)-5,5'-((3,3''',3''',4'-tetraoctyl $ [2,2':5',2':5',2''':5''',2'''-quinquethio-phene]-5,5''''-diyl)bis(methanyly-lidene))bis(3-ethyl-4- oxothiazoli-dine-5,2-diylidene))dimalononitrile) (DRCN5T) $ ^{[91]} $ ）also exhibited similar defect passivation functions leading to improved device performance.

Fei et al. introduced thiourea in the $ \mathrm{M A P b I}_{3} $ precursor and demonstrated significantly increased perovskite crystallinity and morphology along with improved light harvesting, suppressed defect state, and enhanced charge separation and transport. $ ^{[92]} $ Thiourea has two amino

groups and one S-donor; the amino group can form hydrogen bonds with I whereas the S-donors can form Lewis acid-base adducts with Pb or hydrogen bonds with the $ \mathrm{N H_{2}} $ group in perovskites. $ ^{[93]} $ The formation of $ \mathrm{M A I}\cdot \mathrm{P b I}{2}\cdot \mathrm{D M S O}\cdot \mathrm{t i o u r e a} $ adduct also significantly impacts the perovskite crystallinity and morphology for enhanced optoelectronic properties. $ ^{[94]} $ In a recent study, Zhang et al. reported the cooperation of a Lewis base and a Lewis acid by combining commercial bis-PCBM mixed isomers as the Lewis acid in the antisolvent and N-(4- bromophenyl)thiourea (BrPh-ThR) as the Lewis base in the perovskite solution precursor. $ ^{[95]} $ The combination of the Lewis base and the Lewis acid synergistically passivates $ \mathrm{P b}^{2+} $ and $ \mathrm{P b X}^{3-} $ antisite defects (Figure 7b), and improves charge-carrier separation and transport, leading to improved device efficiency from 19.3% to 21.7%. In addition, this Lewis base and acid combination also suppresses moisture incursion, leading to much improved device stability: unencapsulated PSCs remained at 93% of the initial PCE in ambient air (10% $ -20\% $ relative humidity) after 3600 h at $ 20^{\circ} \mathrm{C}-25^{\circ} \mathrm{C} $ and dropped by 10% after 1500 h continuous operation at $ \sim1 $ sun and $ 55^{\circ} \mathrm{C} $ in $ \mathrm{N{2}}. $

2-pyridylthiourea combining N-donor with S-donor to coordinate with $ \mathrm{P b I_{2}} $ was first reported by Zhang et al. to produce uniform, continuous, and compact perovskite films with larger grains. $ ^{[96]} $ In his later study, two thiadiazole derivatives (1,3,4-thiadiazolidine-2-thione, TDZT and 1,3,4-thiadiazolidine-2,5-dithione, TDZDT), which are five-membered heterocyclic-containing S-donor and N-donor materials, were added into a perovskite precursor to modulate $ \mathrm{M A P b I_{3}} $ perovskite crystal growth, leading to a PCE of 19.04% and good stability under humidity and heat. $ ^{[97]} $ Following the same design guidance, Wu et al. developed three organic D- $ \pi $ -A molecules—with different substituted phenyl units as the electron donor, thiophene $ \pi $ -spacer, and cyanoacetic acid as the acceptor—to passivate the ionic defects within $ \mathrm{M A P b I_{3}} $ $ ^{[98]} $ The strong

2.2.3. N-Donor Lewis Base

N-donor Lewis bases have a nitrogen-bearing lone pair of electrons that can bind to undercoordinated $ \mathrm{P b}^{2+} $ ions. The commonly used N-donor Lewis bases are pyridine $ ^{[101]} $ its derivatives, and some $ -\mathrm{N H}{2} $ derivatives (Table 5). Liu et al. applied a small amount of pyridine additive in a precursor mixed solution, resulting in large-crystal, dense, pinhole-free perovskite films (Figure 8a). $ ^{[102]} $ The improved perovskite film quality was attributed to the pyridine retarding the crystal growth and rapid nucleation, along with passivating surface defects associated with the rapid formation of ion coordinate bonds between $ \mathrm{C s}^{+} $ and the perovskite absorbers. The corresponding PSC performance was also significantly improved from 16.94% (no pyridine) to 19.03% (with pyridine). 4-tert-butylpyridine (tBP), a derivative of pyridine and a common additive used in HTL, was also recently introduced into $ \mathrm{M A P b I}{3} $ via the antisolvent process to obtain high-quality perovskite layers with a gradient structure, which is beneficial for both cell efficiency and stability. $ ^{[103]} $

Zuo et al. demonstrated the passivation effect of long-chain polymers (Figure 8b) with different functional groups (amino, Lewis base pyridine, and carboxylic acid groups). $ ^{[104]} $ These polymers can be localized at GBs and strongly coordinated with perovskites. This would benefit trap-state passivation. The PSCs show the best performance with the pyridine group, which has a binding energy of 37.70 kJ/mol with $ \mathrm{P b}^{2+} $ .

Wu et al. demonstrate that simple tailoring of the perovskite ink formulation with a small amount of bilateral alkylamine (BAA) additive (e.g., 1,3-diaminopropane (DAP), 1,6-diaminohexane (DAH), and 1,8-diaminooctane (DAO)) can passivate the perovskite grain surface as well as improve perovskite film formation with uniform grain size distribution and fewer pinholes. $ ^{[105]} $ The $ - \mathrm{N H_{2}} $ tails from these BAAs can form coordination bonds with undercoordinated

$ \mathrm{P b}^{2+} $ ions to suppress crystallographic defects at the grain surface (Figure 8c). In addition, the $ \mathrm{N H_{2}} $ tails can also fill in A-site vacancies. 2.3. Ammonium Salts

Ideally, stoichiometric and defect-free perovskite crystals should not have any excess atoms in the structure. For any kind of inorganic material, nonstoichiometric atomic ratios may result in phase segregation (bulk or surface) during the film formation process. In PSCs, this can be utilized for a self-passivation effect. $ ^{[109]} $ There have been long, extended efforts in using additives of excessive ammonium salts derivatives in the perovskite precursor solution to assist perovskite growth with enhanced crystalline properties and surface morphology. Typical ammonium salts derivative-based additives, as shown in Table 6, include organic halides (e.g., methylammonium iodide (MAI), $ ^{[110,111]} $ methylammonium chloride (MACl), $ ^{[9,13,112-114,115-118]} $ methylammonium bromide (MABr), $ ^{[119]} $ and ammonium chloride $ (\mathrm{N H_{4} C l})^{[120-123]} $ ), organic pseudo-halides (e.g., methylammonium thiocyanate (MASCN), $ ^{[124]} $ guanidine thiocyanate (GuaSCN) $ ^{[125]} $ , ammonium thiocyanate $ (\mathrm{N H_{4} S C N})^{[126]} $ ), organic non-halides (e.g., methylammonium acetate (MAOAc) $ ^{[127]} $ and ammonium acetate $ (\mathrm{N H_{4} O A c})^{[128]} $ ).

In the case of MAI, Cohen et al. found that unreacted MAI during the two-step conversion reaction to form perovskite can remain on the perovskite surface, which causes a drop in the current density possibly due to reduced conductivity of the perovskites. $ ^{[110]} $ In contrast, Son et al. found that adding a small excess of MAI into the precursor solution induces the accumulation of an excess of MAI GBs for reducing non-radiative recombination for higher device performance

第 34 页

(Figure 9a) $ ^{[111]} $ Yang et al. showed in an earlier study that $ \sim 20\% $ excess MAI in an otherwise stoichiometric $ \mathrm{M A P b I}_{3} $ precursor solution enabled a higher-temperature annealing of perovskite without thermal decomposition, leading to much enlarged, uniform grain growth over a large substrate area for the first demonstration of $ > 1 5 \% $ -efficient $ 1 - \mathrm{c m}^{2} $ PSCs.

Zhao et al. first reported using MACl additive during the early stage of perovskite thinfilm preparation before the report of the commonly accepted antisolvent treatment procedure. $ ^{[112]} $ MACl slowed down the perovskite film formation process and led to a darkened color of the final perovskite films(Figure 9b); the slow crystallization process improved the film coverage on a planar substrate whereas the darkened film enhanced the absorption, leading to much improved device performance for both the planar and mesoporous cell structures. MACl was later used as an additive for preparing a variety of perovskite compositions including $ \mathrm{M A P b I}{3}, $ $ \mathrm{F A P b I}{3}, $ mixed cation (e.g., FAMA or CsFAMA) as well as low-dimensional perovskites. $ ^{[9,13,113,114,115-118]} $ However, the roles of MACl, especially the roles of $ \mathrm{C l}^{-} $ , are still not fully understood. Kim et al. systematically studied the role of MACl additive in $ \mathrm{F A P b I}{3} $ -based PSCs (Figure 9c). $ ^{[129]} $ In addition to the larger grain size, MACl effectively stabilizes an intermediate to form the pure $ \alpha $ - $ \mathrm{F A P b I}{3} $ without annealing—only through cationic site substitution. The formation energy of the resulting perovskite structure is related to the amount of incorporated MACl. Consequently, MACl additive induces intermediate phase stabilization for over 24% efficiency.

Furthermore, the impact of $ \mathrm{N H_{4} C l} $ additive on the perovskite crystallinity and surface morphology have also been studied. Zuo et al. showed that $ \mathrm{N H_{4} C l} $ improved crystallinity and morphology of perovskite thin films compared to the neat and MACl-based analogues, likely due to easy removal of the additive during perovskite formation. $ ^{[120]} $ The mechanism was further elucidated by Rong et al., who showed that in the presence of moisture, deprotonation of $ \mathrm{N H_{4}^{+}} $ in

the intermediates by water molecules induced the formation of $ \mathrm{N H_{3}} $ and HI/HCl until volatile $ \mathrm{N H_{4}}^{+} $ was completely decomposed during thermal annealing, facilitating $ \mathrm{M A P b I_{3}} $ crystallization (Figure 9d). $ ^{[121]} $

Quaternary ammonium halides (e.g., choline chloride) was first reported by Zheng et al. as an effective additive for defect passivation at the perovskite surface, enabling a certified PCE of 20.59% and increased stability against humidity and light (Figure 9e). $ ^{[130]} $ In a later study, the influence of the molecular structure of passivation molecules on their passivation effect was thoroughly studied. $ ^{[107]} $ A new type of amino acid passivation molecule, D4TBP, which combines several passivation groups, was found to result in a $ \mathrm{V_{oc}} $ deficit of 0.34 V for a p-i-n PSC and 21.4% cell efficiency. Li et al. showed that adding butylphosphonic acid 4-ammonium chloride (4-ABPACl) in the $ \mathrm{MAPbI_{3}} $ perovskite precursor enabled crosslinking between perovskite grains through strong hydrogen bonding of the $ -\mathrm{PO(OH)}{2} $ and $ -\mathrm{NH}{3}^{+} $ terminal groups to the perovskite surface, leading to almost 2x increase of cell efficiency along with stronger resistance to moisture (Figure 9f). $ ^{[131]} $ Li et al. embedded a monoammonium zinc porphyrin (ZnP) compound into perovskite film for blade-coating of large-area uniform perovskite films as large as $ 16\mathrm{cm}^{2}. $ $ ^{[132]} $ With the ZnP additive passivating defects at both perovskite surfaces and GBs, $ 1.96-\mathrm{cm}^{2} $ and $ 0.1-\mathrm{cm}^{2} $ cells showed 18.3% and 20.5% efficiencies, respectively. 2.4. Low-Dimensional Perovskites

Two-dimensional (2D) perovskites have recently attracted extensive attention for better moisture resistance and phase stability than the conventional 3-dimensional (3D) perovskites. $ ^{[133]} $ The enhanced stability is often attributed to the bulky organic cations that form 2D layered

第 39 页

structures. However, the long, bulky organic cations often form a barrier hindering charge transport. The 2D perovskite additives used in FAPbI $ {3} $ or inorganic CsPbI $ {3} $ are mostly for phase stabilization. $ ^{[134]} $ Zhang et al. recently introduced a small amount of 2D perovskite component EDAPbI $ {4} $ (EDA: ethylenediamine) into the CsPbI $ {3} $ perovskite based on a precursor of PbI $ {2} $ $ \cdot $ xHI ( $ x > 1.3 $ ) and CsI to significantly improve the stability of $ \alpha $ -CsPbI $ {3} $ (Table 7). $ ^{[135]} $ Following this preparation approach, the $ \alpha $ -CsPbI $ {3} $ can remain stable for months under storage in ambient condition and also survive for more than a week at $ 100^{\circ} \mathrm{C} $ (Figure 10a), and the all-inorganic CsPbI $ {3} $ PSC displayed a PCE of 11.8%. The (110) layered structure of EDAPbI $ {4} $ perovskite is essential for the enhanced stability. Similar to this work, other studies also showed improved CsPbI $ {3} $ PSCs by using different 2D perovskites. $ ^{[136]} $ Jiang et al. demonstrated the use of PEA $ {2} $ PbI $ {4} $ based 2D perovskite to improve CsPbI $ {3} $ PSCs with enhanced phase stability, lower defect density, and higher efficiency of 12.4%. $ ^{[137]} $ These results confirm that constructing a quasi-2D or 2D/3D perovskite is an effective way to stabilize inorganic CsPbI $ {3} $ perovskite, but at a cost of impairing carrier transportation.

The phase-stability issue is also critical to developing $ \mathrm{F A P b I}{3} $ , which has a more ideal bandgap ( $ \sim1.4 $ -1.55eV) for single-junction solar cells. $ ^{[138]} $ Tuning the tolerance factor (e.g., by adding Cs) represents one effective way to improve the phase stability of $ \alpha $ -FAPbI $ {3} $ $ ^{[139]} $ however, the A-site alloying generally widens the bandgap. Incorporating 1.67 mol% of 2D $ \mathrm{P E A}{2} \mathrm{P b I}{4} $ into the precursor solution enables the formation of phase-pure formamidinium perovskite with an order-of-magnitude enhanced photoluminescence lifetime, leading to the demonstration of a stabilized PCE of 20.64% (certified stabilized PCE of 19.77%) with significantly enhanced operational stability. $ ^{[140]} $ The benefits of incorporating $ \mathrm{P E A}^{+} $ into 3D perovskite were also reported by Lee at al. $ ^{[141]} $ The presence of wide-bandgap PEA-based quasi-

2D perovskite is good for reducing defect density and slowing down the degradation of unencapsulated devices. An n-butylammonium (BA)-based Ruddlesden-Popper (RP)/3D heterostructure was also recently tested along with molecule passivation to improve $ \alpha $ - FAPbI $ _{3} $ films for high-performance and ambient-air-stable PSCs. $ ^{[142]} $

2D additives have also been applied to other perovskite compositions with different bandgaps toward tandem applications. For a 1.68-eV wide-bandgap perovskite, complementary additives PEAI and $ \mathrm{P b} (\mathrm{S C N}){2} $ were used in the perovskite precursor to enable a demonstration of PSC with an efficiency of $ \sim 20\% $ $ ^{[143]} $ The coupling of $ \mathrm{P E A}^{+} $ and $ \mathrm{S C N}^{-} $ provides a synergistic effect (Figure 10b) that overcomes growth challenges, with either additive individually leading to improved perovskite crystallinity, reduced formation of excess $ \mathrm{P b I}{2} $ , lower energetic disorder, and enhanced carrier mobility and lifetime. Tong et al. applied guanidinium thiocyanate (GuaSCN) to Sn-Pb mixed, low-bandgap ( $ \sim 1.25 $ eV) perovskite thin films with marked improvements in the structural and optoelectronic properties, primarily due to the formation of a 2D structure at GBs that results from a small amount of GuaSCN additive (Figure 10c) $ ^{[6]} $ The formation of such a 2D structure passivates GBs and surfaces; suppresses the formation of excessive Sn vacancies by blocking diffusion of Sn out of grains; and reduces oxidation of $ \mathrm{S n}^{2+} $ by hindering oxygen diffusion into grains.

Other large organic-cation-based additives include 1,1,1-trifluoro-ethyl ammonium iodide (FEAI; Figure 10d) and imidazolium iodide, which are likely to result in the formation of 1D structures. Bi et al. reported FEAI additive in $ \mathrm{M A P b I}{3} $ with the possible formation of 1D $ \mathrm{F E A P b I}{3} $ perovskite that enhanced the overall perovskite morphology and stability of the perovskite devices. $ ^{[144]} $ Imidazolium iodide was also shown as an effective additive in a perovskite precursor to boost PSC performance via defect passivation. $ ^{[145]} $ Fumigation with MA results in the

2.5. Ionic Liquids (ILs)

Ionic liquids (ILs) are salts consisting of large organic cations and various organic or inorganic anions, and they are in liquid form below $ 1 0 0^{\circ} \mathrm{C} $ . They are almost non-volatile, resulting from the strong interionic electrostatic force. ILs usually exhibit high ionic conductivity and high thermal and electrochemical stability. In addition, ILs are capable of dissolving various organic and inorganic compounds. Choosing variable permutation and combinations of cations and anions can produce a wide range of binary and ternary ionic liquids. $ ^{[1 4 7, 1 4 8]} $ Ionic liquid additives can control crystallization and passivate certain defects of perovskite films resulting in decreased ion migration and hysteresis.

The temperature range for processing perovskites is generally mild $ (\sim 1 0 0^{\circ} \mathrm{C}-1 7 0^{\circ} \mathrm{C})^{[3 2]} $ to avoid significant degradation/decomposition at elevated temperature. $ ^{[1 4 9]} $ The IL methylammonium formate (MAFa) was used as the solvent to produce high-quality, crystalline thin films of $ \mathrm{M A P b I}_{3} $ with improved chemical stability over a broad range of temperatures and

第 44 页

times. $ ^{[150]} $ Shahiduzzaman et al. $ ^{[151]} $ added 1 wt% IL 1-hexyl-3-methylimidazolium chloride (HMImCl) into $ \mathrm{M A P b I}{3} $ solution in DMF to get uniform and pinhole-free $ \mathrm{M A P b I}{3} $ film by spin coating. The addition of IL in the perovskite precursor solution facilitated uniform nucleation and prevented rapid crystal formation and resulted in uniform and smooth films. A follow-up study from the same group further examined the effect of ILs with different viscosities on perovskite thin films and their impact on PSC performance. $ ^{[152]} $ Changing the organic cation to reduce the viscosity of ILs resulted in improved morphology and crystallinity of the perovskite thin films and higher PSC performance. By using IL MAF, IL-driven crystallization was further used to prepare a record planar PSC with stabilized power output of 19.5% $ ^{[153]} $

Salado et al. exploited the hydrophobicity of IL 1-methyl-3-(1H,1H,2H, 2H nonafluorohexyl) imidazolium iodide (FIm) to increase the stability of MA-FA-based mixedcation PSCs. $ ^{[154]} $ The same IL FIm (0.5% w/w) was later used in the Cs-FA-MA-based PSCs and a significant increase was observed in both the stability and PV performance of the devices. $ ^{[155]} $ The perovskite films showed no degradation even after storing the samples under 55% $ -60\% $ relative humidity conditions for over 150 days because of the hydrophobicity of the IL dopant to repel water molecules away, along with the defect passivation associated with iodide vacancies from the iodide in FIm.

In a recent study by Wang et al., three types of water-soluble triazolium ILs with an amino group $ \left(-\mathrm{N H}_{2}\right) $ and an adjustable alkyl side-chain, containing a cation of 1-alkyl-4-amino-1,2,4-triazolium (termed as RATZ; $ \mathrm{R}= $ ethyl (EATZ), butyl (BATZ), octyl (OATZ), and ATZ represents 4-amino-1,2,4-triazolium) were used as an additive in the precursor solution (Figure 11a). $ ^{[156]} $ Interestingly, the electron lone pair available in the amino group of the ILs interacted with the $ \mathrm{P b}^{2+} $ defects and formed a self-assembled monolayer, keeping the alkyl chains vertically on the

第 45 页

perovskite layer with a hydrophobic surface. As a result, unencapsulated devices maintained $ \sim 80\% $ of their initial efficiency after being exposed to $ \sim 40\% $ relative humidity at room temperature and dark conditions for 3500 h. Zhang et al. studied the interaction of ionic liquids with precursors based on imidazolium iodide modified with different groups as additives, including $ -\mathrm{CH}{2}-\mathrm{CH}=\mathrm{CH}{2} $ (CC2) $ -\mathrm{CH}{2}\mathrm{C}\equiv\mathrm{CH} $ (CC3), and $ -\mathrm{CH}{2}\mathrm{C}\equiv\mathrm{N} $ (CN) （Figure 11b）. $ ^{[157]} $ The interaction results in imidazolium- $ \mathrm{PbI}{3}^{-} $ salts with 1D $ \mathrm{PbI}{3}^{-} $ chains based on bridging iodide ligands that affect perovskite crystal growth. The devices were fabricated with 2 mol% of ionic liquid in perovskite precursor solution followed by a fumigation process; the CC2-based PSC exhibited the best PCE of 19.21%.

Bai et al. recently incorporated IL 1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium tetrafluoroborate (BMIMBF4) as an additive in the Cs-FA-MA triple-cation perovskite absorber sandwiched between NiO HTL and PCBM ETL. The use of BMIMBF4 enhanced the device efficiency from 18.5% to 19.9% and substantially improved the long-term device stability, with only 5% performance degradation of an encapsulated device under sunlight soaking for more than 1800 hours at $ 7 0^{\circ} \mathrm{C}-7 5^{\circ} \mathrm{C} $ (Figure 11c). $ ^{[158]} $ The improvement of performance as well as stability was attributed to beneficial effects observed with the use of IL BMIMBF4 such as enlarged grains, improved energetic alignment at interfaces, improved interaction between perovskite and NiO HTL, and suppressed ion migration. In other studies, ionic liquids 1-butyl-3- methylimidazolium bromine $ (\mathrm{B M I B r})^{[1 5 9]} $ and 1-(4-ethenylbenzyl)-3-(3,3,4,4,5,5,6,6,7,7,8,8,8-tridecafluorooctylimidazolium iodide $ (\mathrm{E T I})^{[1 6 0]} $ were used as additives in $ \mathrm{M A}^{+} $ -contained perovskite precursor solutions. In the latter case, it was shown that the formation of hydrogen bonds between ILs and MA restrains the $ \mathrm{M A}^{+} $ cation movement from bulk to the surface, resulting in enhanced thermal stability (Figure 11d).

3. Progress on Additive-assisted Interface Optimization

The photovoltaic process requires two successive steps: photoinduced charge generation via light absorption and separation/extraction of charge carriers to the external circuit to generate electric power. $ ^{[162]} $ An ideal selective contact should not deteriorate the light-absorbing layer and also should not induce and/or accelerate degradation within the device. In addition, there are also no energy losses when photogenerated carriers are injected from the light-absorbing material into the selective contact, minimum recombination at the contact interfaces, and the Fermi level of its corresponding carrier is maintained at the interface without any energy loss. $ ^{[163]} $ As reported previously, capacitive current probably originated from ion migration and charge accumulation can generate capacitor components near the ETL/perovskite and perovskite/HTL junctions. $ ^{[164]} $ This means that interfaces and bulk perovskite near interface are critical in controlling the hysteresis. Thus, interfacial engineering and bulk perovskite engineering are both necessary toward hysteresis-free PSCs. $ ^{[165]} $

The interface between ETL/perovskite absorber and perovskite absorber/HTL are critical to the performance of PSCs. In addition, perovskite/electrode interface $ ^{[166]} $ and carrier transport layer/electrode interface $ ^{[167]} $ are also crucial for efficient extraction of charge carriers and the improvement of moisture resistance. The presence of dangling bonds on the surface of charge transport layers can generate interfacial trap states, affecting charge extraction from the perovskite absorber layer with enhanced surface recombination and reduced PCE. Moreover, these surface defects may also act as crystallization sites, affecting perovskite nucleation. $ ^{[109]} $ This motivates the development of interlayer materials and methods for contact passivation. In this section, we only focus on recent studies on using additive-assisted strategies for interface optimization in PSCs,

第 49 页

including ETL modifiers, HTL modifiers, and a surface modifier of the perovskite absorber. The progress in each of these categories is discussed in detail.

3.1. Electron-Transport Layer (ETL) Modifiers

$ \mathrm{T i O_{2}} $ is the most widely used ETL material for n-i-p PSCs. However, $ \mathrm{T i O_{2}} $ -based solar cells suffer from reduced stability upon ultraviolet exposure and hysteretic behavior when measured under different scan conditions (e.g., direction and rate). $ ^{[168]} $ Various fullerene derivatives have been developed to address these issues. During the early stage of PSC development, Snaith and co-workers employed a fullerene self-assembled monolayer $ (\mathrm{C}{60}-\mathrm{SAM}) $ to modify the mesoporous $ \mathrm{T i O{2}} $ $ (\mathrm{m-T i O_{2}}) $ surface $ ^{[169]} $ as well as the compact $ \mathrm{T i O_{2}} $ $ (\mathrm{c-T i O_{2}}) $ surface $ ^{[170]} $ in n-i-p devices with improved interfacial properties (e.g., charge-transfer dynamics and reduced ionic defect density) and reduced $ \mathrm{V_{o c}} $ loss and smaller hysteresis. In addition to C60-SAM, fullerene $ \mathrm{C}{60} $ and its derivative $ \mathrm{P C}{61} $ BM are frequently used as the ETL modifier for n-i-p PSCs. $ ^{[171,172,173]} $ Modification of $ \mathrm{T i O_{2}} $ with hydrophobic $ \mathrm{P C}{61} $ BM and a hydrophilic ethanolamine-functionalized fullerene $ (\mathrm{C}{60}- $ ETA) improved device performance compared with devices made with each individual surface modification. $ ^{[172]} $ The $ \mathrm{P C}{61} $ BM layer reduced the trap density at the $ \mathrm{T i O{2}} $ surface, whereas the hydrophilic $ \mathrm{C}{60}- $ ETA layer can improve the wettability of the perovskite film on the ETL and facilitate electron collection. Modifying the $ \mathrm{T i O{2}} $ surface with a triblock fullerene derivative with multifunctional groups (PCBB-2CN-2C8) significantly improved charge extraction associated with defect passivation and increased $ \mathrm{V_{o c}} $ from 0.99 to 1.06 V, resulting from the higher work function of PCBB-2CN-2C8-modified $ \mathrm{T i O_{2}} $ $ ^{[173]} $ Peng et al. demonstrated an ultrathin layer of a poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA):PCBM mixture that can effectively passivate defects at the perovskite/$ \mathrm{T i O_{2}} $ interface. $ ^{[174]} $ This significantly suppressed interfacial recombination and

increased $ V_{\mathrm{oc}} $ of mixed-cation PSCs by as much as 80 mV, leading to PSCs with 20.4% steady state PCE and negligible hysteresis over a wide range of scan rates.

In addition to fullerene -based modifiers, Yang et al. reported the use of ionic liquid 1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium tetrafluoroborate ([BMIM]BF4) to modify mesoporous $ \mathrm{T i O_{2}} $ ETL in $ \mathrm{M A P b I_{3}} $ -based PSCs. $ ^{[175]} $ The use of an IL modifier facilitated smoother $ \mathrm{T i O_{2}} $ surface for perovskite crystal growth with reduced work function and increased transmittance and electron mobility. Also, it is worth noting that PSCs showed no hysteresis with an increased PCE of 19.62%. The interaction between $ \mathrm{B M I M}^{+} $ and perovskite also reduced trap density and suppressed ion migration. In a separate study, 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium hexafluorophosphate ([EMIM]PF6), another IL modifier, was used to treat the $ \mathrm{T i O_{2}} $ surface and resulted in similar improvement. $ ^{[176]} $

ZnO is another ETL that has several benefits (e.g., high electron mobility and facile solution process) when compared with $ \mathrm{T i O_{2}} $ . However, the accelerated degradation of perovskite materials on the ZnO surface due to proton transfer from $ \mathrm{M A}^{+} $ and residual hydroxyl group has significantly limited its application in the PSC field. $ ^{[177]} $ It was shown that ILs [EMIM]PF $ 6^{[178]} $ and $ [\mathrm{B M I M} ] \mathrm{B F 4}^{[1 7 9]} $ could be used to modify the $ \mathrm{Z n O / M A P b I_{3}} $ interface to mitigate the challenge associated with ZnO. In addition, $ \mathrm{P C}{61} $ BM was also used to modify the surface of ZnO for low-temperature-processed n-i-p PSCs. $ ^{[1 8 0]} $ The $ \mathrm{P C}{61} $ BM interlayer on ZnO was shown to facilitate growth of a high-quality perovskite absorber layer and resulted in hysteresis-free PSCs.

During the past few years, $ \mathrm{S n O_{2}} $ has become a popular ETL used in n-i-p PSCs. Despite its high electron mobility, $ \mathrm{S n O_{2}} $ ETL deposited at low temperature suffers from poor film crystallinity and large defect density, which usually reduces PSC efficiency. Huang et al. showed that adding IL tetramethylammonium hydroxide $ \mathrm{N(CH_{3}){4}OH} $ (TMAH) in the $ \mathrm{S n O{2}} $ nanoparticle suspension to form a stable and homogeneous suspension could result in a better $ \mathrm{S n O_{2}} $ ETL with lower defect

3.2. Hole-Transport Layer (HTL) Modifiers

HTL in PSCs plays a key role in extracting photogenerated holes from perovskite to the back contact. $ ^{[185]} $ Among various hole-transport materials (HTMs), spiro-OMeTAD and poly triarylamine (PTAA) are most frequently used in n-i-p and p-i-n device structures, respectively. However, the high cost of synthesis of these materials along with the need to use hydrophilic dopants present a challenge to PSC development. The stability issue under heat stress at $ 85^{\circ} \mathrm{C} $ could limit practical applications. $ ^{[147,186]} $ Attempts have been made to develop more cost-effective and dopant-free HTMs or some other efficient dopants for potential commercial applications of PSCs. $ ^{[187]} $ Calio et al. have recently demonstrated the use an N-heterocyclic hydrophobic ionic liquid, 1-butyl-3-methylpyridinium bis (trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide (BMPyTFSI), to replace LiTFSi as a hydrophobic dopant in spiro-OMeTAD HTL in a mesoporous n-i-p PSCs. $ ^{[188]} $ The two

$ \mathrm{C F_{3} S O_{2}}^{-} $ groups from the IL have strong electron affinity, resulting into a p-type dopant for spiro- OMeTAD. PSCs using with BMPyTFSI (7.8 mol%) showed a similar PCE compared with PSCs fabricated using conventional doped spiro- OMeTAD. However, the stability for unencapsulated PSCs using BMPyTFSI-doped spiro- OMeTAD retained 80% of its initial PCE after 7 months of aging in a humid (50% relative humidity) and dark environment. Other dopants, such as Ag-TFSI, $ ^{[189]} $ Zn-TFSI $ ^{[190]} $ and CuI, $ ^{[191]} $ were also tested to replace conventional Li-TFSI to improve PSC stability. In a recent study, Huang et al. reported using vanadic oxide $ (\mathrm{V}{2}\mathrm{O}{5}) $ to replace LiTFSi in spiro- OMeTAD. $ ^{[192]} $ $ \mathrm{V}{2}\mathrm{O}{5} $ has a strong redox potential and can easily oxidize spiro- OMeTAD in the absence of LiTFSi. The champion PSC based on $ \mathrm{V}{2}\mathrm{O}{5} $ -doped HTM reached a PCE of 20.5%, whereas the device without $ \mathrm{V}{2}\mathrm{O}{5} $ exhibits a PCE of 18.1%. The change of the interfacial properties also resulted in suppressed hysteresis and improved stability.

Poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene) polystyrene sulfonate (PEDOT:PSS) is another well-known HTM, especially for Sn-Pb-based low-bandgap PSCs. However, PEDOT:PSS is acidic and hydrophilic; it can corrode the ITO layer and absorbs moisture to induce damage in the perovskite layer. $ ^{[193]} $ To address this issue, Wang et al. reported using a $ \mathrm{V_{2}O_{5}} $ /PEDOT:PSS bilayer structure on ITO substrate. The $ \mathrm{V_{2}O_{5}} $ interlayer insulates the ITO from direct contacting with the PEDOT layer, which not only reduced the corrosive degradation of the ITO, but also enhanced PCE by $ 20\% $ $ ^{[194]} $ With a similar approach, Yu et al. reported the use of PEDOT:PSS and a graphene oxide (PEDOT:GO) composite layer to replace PEDOT:PSS as the HTL with improved shelf-life stability. $ ^{[195]} $ Hu et al. demonstrated the use of a fluorosurfactant dopant to PEDOT:PSS electrode, leading to high conductivity (>4,000 S/cm), improved transmittance (over 80% in the range of 400-900 nm), and high mechanical endurance. $ ^{[196]} $ Flexible PSCs with this electrode showed a stabilized PCE of 19.0% at $ 0.1\mathrm{cm}^{2} $ and 10.9% at $ 25\mathrm{cm}^{2} $ , respectively.

3.3. Surface Modifier of Perovskite Absorber

In Section 2, we discussed the addition to the perovskite modification during the bulk perovskite growth with various additives. But additive modification to the existing perovskite layer in a device stack is also often found useful to improve device efficiency and stability. So far, most studies have focused on modifying the perovskite/HTL interface in an n-i-p device structure. The modifier is usually applied after the perovskite growth and before the HTL deposition.

Salado et al. showed that post-growth treatment of IL imidazolium iodide (ImI) can significantly affect the perovskite/HTL interface with enhanced cell performance. $ ^{[197]} $ PSCs based on ImI-modified MAPbI $ {3} $ perovskite reached an efficiency of 19.44% (for optimized concentration 5 mg/mL of IL). This improvement in PCE has been attributed to uniform coverage of perovskite film and reduction in surface traps associated with surface-defect passivation by ImI IL. It is also possible that local iodine vacancies can be effectively filled by iodide ions from the IL, with restricted formation of superoxide leading to improved PSC stability. In another report, Huang et al. reported on the use of wide-bandgap $ \mathrm{P b S O}{4} $ and $ \mathrm{P b}{3} (\mathrm{P O}{4})_{2} $ as the surface inorganic layers through a facile chemical reaction to enhance the water resistance of the perovskite films and reduce the defect density by passivating undercoordinated surface Pb centers. $ ^{[14]} $

To facilitate hole transfer from perovskite to HTL, a series of perovskite quantum dots (QDs) with various Br:I ratios (bandgap and energy levels) were synthesized and incorporated between the $ \mathrm{M A P b I}{3} $ perovskite and HTM layers for interface engineering. $ ^{[198]} $ Adjusting the valance band edge of the QD between the VB edge of $ \mathrm{M A P b I}{3} $ and HOMO level of the HTL resulted in more effective hole transfer, leading to increased charge-carrier lifetime and reduced charge-transfer resistance across the interface, with a notable 29% increase in PCE. Similarly, Liu et al. showed that inserting $ \mathrm{C s P b I}{3} $ QDs between FAMAPbI $ {3} $ and HTL also forms cascading

energy levels for extracting holes and blocking electrons, leading to enhanced PSC efficiency (in particular, short-circuit current density) and stability. $ ^{[199]} $ Zheng et al. recently showed that a trace amount of ligand-capped CsPbBrCl $ {2} $ QDs can be introduced into $ \mathrm{M A P b I}{3} $ perovskite film via an antisolvent dripping process in a p-i-n device structure. $ ^{[200]} $ These doped $ \mathrm{M A P b I}{3} $ solar cells exhibited reduced band-tail states, smaller trap density, and longer carrier lifetime, leading to an impressive PCE of 21.5% for $ \mathrm{M A P b I}{3} $ PSC. In another study, $ \mathrm{C s P b B r}_{3} $ perovskite nanowires (NWs) were introduced into perovskite during the antisolvent step to form a near-ideal graded heterojunction. $ ^{[201]} $ The NW morphology was found to be key to achieving lateral homogeneity in the perovskite film surface states. The resulting PSCs showed high PCE of up to 21.4% with improved stability.

A thin layer of 2D perovskite film can also be introduced as an interfacial layer on top of 3D perovskites. The hybrid 3D/2D perovskite films often exhibit longer carrier lifetimes and lower defect densities. $ ^{[202]} $ The 2D interfacial layer is normally introduced as a post-growth treatment of 3D perovskite with an isopropyl alcohol solution containing bulky organic cations that normally react with $ \mathrm{P b I_{2}} $ to form 2D perovskites. Examples include n-hexyl trimethyl ammonium bromide (HTAB), $ ^{[13]} $ butylammonium iodide (BAI), $ ^{[202]} $ octylammonium iodide (OAI), $ ^{[202]} $ cyclopropylammonium iodide (CAI), $ ^{[203]} $ phenylethylammonium iodide (PEAI), $ ^{[204]} $ PEAI-MAI mixture, $ ^{[205]} $ 5-ammonium valeric acid iodide (5-AVAI), $ ^{[206]} $ pentafluorophenylethylammonium iodide (FEAI), $ ^{[207]} $ and n-hexylammonium bromide $ (\mathrm{C}_{6}\mathrm{B r}) $ $ ^{[208]} $ PEAI-FAI mixture, $ ^{[209]} $ diethylenetriamine (DETA). $ ^{[210]} $ The growth planes of 2D perovskites are primarily parallel to the substrate $ ^{[211]} $ so the design of some new organic bulky cations with enhanced out-plane charge transport will promote the efficiency of 2D-modified 3D PSCs. $ ^{[212]} $ Imidazolium iodide (IAI) was

applied to the post-treatment to form an 1D capping layer with increased performance and stability. $ ^{[213]} $

During the ammonium derivatives or ammonium salt treatment of perovskite films, an organic cation layer can sometimes form rather than a 2D perovskite capping layer, which could also passivation defects and improve the device efficiency and stability. Examples include tetraethyl ammonium (TEA), $ ^{[214]} $ octylammonium iodide (OAI), $ ^{[215]} $ FAI/MABr, $ ^{[216]} $ mixed oleylamine and hydroiodic acid (OLA-HI), $ ^{[217]} $ choline iodine (CHI), $ ^{[218]} $ PEAI, $ ^{[8, 219]} $ phenyltrimethylammonium bromide (PTABr), $ ^{[220]} $ and guanidinium bromide(GuaBr). $ ^{[221]} $

These discussed strategies are mostly used to form a wide-bandgap semiconductor on top of the perovskite surface, which not only could eliminate the deep level traps at grain surfaces but also form a heterojunction to further hinder non-radiative recombination. The energy offset at these junctions may create an electronic barrier to hinder the charge transfer across perovskite grains and between perovskite and charge transport layers. Although the wide-bandgap interfacial layer could suppress recombination, it is also critical to control the thickness and/or the energy levels of the wide bandgap materials at the perovskite/charge transport layer interface to facilitate charge extraction for efficient devices.[30]

4. Conclusion and Outlook

A PCE exceeding 25% has been achieved in PSCs with unprecedented rapid development. But the commercialization of PSCs will require synergistically developing high-efficiency devices at large areas along with long-term operation stability as a competitive PV technology. The actual long-term stability of PSCs at high efficiencies still hangs behind the outstanding efficiency

第 56 页

progress. Various mechanisms for devices degradation still need to be explored in greater depth to obtain PSCs that simultaneously exhibit high performance and high stability. The stability of the perovskite layer is usually optimized by compositional engineering, the application of low-D materials and additives engineering. On the other hand, incorporating proper charge-transport layers and electrodes or interfacial modifiers into PSCs are also considered as effective ways to improve device stability and efficiency of PSCs.

Tailoring functional additives to passivate the defects in perovskite films and interfacial traps have proven to be effective approaches to reduce energy loss and improve PSC performance. Many groups have investigated the possible mechanisms of additive-engineered perovskite formation, but some in-situ characterization techniques are valuable for fully understanding how the additives control perovskite crystallization. To guide the design of efficient additives, we need to fully characterize the types and density of defects that form in different perovskite materials and gain a fundamental understanding of their impact on electronic transport properties. The crystal structure of passivation agents is also important for revealing the role of additives in perovskite formation. Comprehensive studies need to be conducted to reveal the effects and functions of additives with different molecular structures. In addition, standard characterization protocols should be established to properly evaluate the role of additives on the performance and stability of PSCs. In principle, a molecular library could be established by investigating the effect of functional groups, conjugated system, and the substituent of the additives on the passivation capacity. With research data accumulated and shared among the entire research field, we can use machine learning to discover the underlying principles for selecting materials and optimizing devices.
